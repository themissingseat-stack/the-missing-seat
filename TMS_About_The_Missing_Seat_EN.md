# About The Missing Seat

### The story changes when we ask who wasn't in the room.

Every day, decisions are made that affect millions of people. Governments pass laws. Courts rule on rights. Institutions set policies. Companies make promises. Officials announce progress.

But there's a question we don't ask nearly enough.

**Who was missing from the conversation that led to the decision?**

And what happened to those people afterward?

That's why The Missing Seat exists.

We want to know more than what happened. We want to understand why it happened, who had the power to make it happen, who benefited, and who was left dealing with the consequences.

Sometimes the answers are uncomfortable. Sometimes they challenge things we've always taken for granted. And sometimes the people expected to explain a decision are the same people who made it.

That's worth a closer look.

### The person behind The Missing Seat

My name is Jansen Bowden. I'm African American. I've traveled around the world, experienced different cultures and ways of life, and made my home primarily in Germany.

Traveling and living in different countries have shown me how differently people think, how societies work, and how differently we can understand the very same ideas.

Freedom. Fairness. Equal opportunity. Responsibility.

We hear those words everywhere. But they don't always mean the same thing in practice.

I've seen how easily people accept something because it's familiar. Because it's always been done that way. Or because someone in authority says it's necessary.

I've also learned that countries don't have to share the same history to repeat similar mistakes.

And living outside the United States hasn't made me any less interested in what happens there. If anything, distance has helped me question things I once accepted without much thought.

I don't believe every problem has a simple solution. I don't think every disagreement means someone has bad intentions. And I certainly don't claim to have all the answers.

But I do believe we should be willing to question the answers we've already been given.

That's the thinking behind The Missing Seat.

### What we're trying to do

Information is everywhere. Understanding is another matter.

You can read a headline, hear a politician speak, or watch an institution announce a reform and still have little idea what actually changed.

Who made the decision? What evidence supported it? Were there other options? Who gained something? Who lost something? And did anyone ask the people most affected?

We investigate those questions through reporting, historical research, analysis, and commentary.

Our editorial work follows six basic questions:

1. What happened?
2. What went wrong?
3. Who benefited?
4. Who carried the cost?
5. Who made it possible?
6. What can people do now?

That last question is especially important.

What's the point of explaining a problem in detail if readers come away believing there's nothing they can do?

Sometimes taking action means writing to an elected representative. Sometimes it means finding an overlooked document, checking a public claim, supporting an existing effort, or simply asking a better question.

Not every problem can be solved right away.

But that's no reason to stop looking at it.

### Facts, opinions, and uncomfortable questions

The Missing Seat has a point of view. We don't hide it.

We believe human dignity, equal rights, fairness, and accountability should mean more than fine words in official speeches.

But having principles doesn't give us permission to twist facts to fit our opinions.

Whenever possible, we work with original documents, laws, court records, official data, and other primary sources. Credible journalism and research help us understand the context and examine claims.

We distinguish between what can be verified, what we infer from the evidence, what we think about it, and what remains unanswered.

A claim isn't proven just because it comes from a respected institution.

The evidence has to support the specific point being made.

And when the facts don't support an argument, the argument has to change.

Including our own.

### Independent doesn't mean indifferent

The Missing Seat is an independent editorial and civic-information project. We don't represent a political party, government, corporation, or advocacy organization.

We won't defend an institution simply because we support its stated goals. And we won't automatically condemn a decision just because we disagree with the people behind it.

We compare what was promised, what was actually done, and what happened afterward.

History matters here.

When an injustice causes harm that continues for generations, acknowledging the original injustice doesn't necessarily repair that harm.

When progress is announced, we want to know what it's being measured against.

And when powerful institutions demand accountability from everyone else, they should be willing to answer difficult questions themselves.

We have to hold ourselves to that standard, too.

### Making difficult subjects understandable

You shouldn't need a law degree to understand a decision that affects your rights.

You shouldn't need to be an economist to ask where public money went.

And you shouldn't have to spend hours looking up technical terms just to understand why a government, court, or company acted as it did.

We aim to write plainly without leaving out the details that matter.

If something is complicated, we'll explain why. If evidence conflicts, we'll show the contradiction. If an important question remains unanswered, we'll say so.

We don't expect readers to agree with every conclusion.

We want them to think for themselves.

And we believe they deserve enough information to reach their own conclusions.

### When we get things wrong

We'll make mistakes, too.

What matters is how we handle them.

Confirmed factual errors are corrected. Important new information may lead us to update an article. Substantial corrections should be visible, not quietly hidden.

We welcome readers who question our reasoning, challenge our conclusions, or point us toward information we've missed.

Criticism isn't the problem.

The problem starts when we refuse to examine legitimate criticism.

### How we use AI

We use AI tools to help with research and drafting. Jansen Bowden is responsible for what is published.

### What we are not

The Missing Seat is not a law firm, a legal aid service or a political party. We explain rights and point to organizations that can help. We cannot give legal advice or represent you. A listing in our directory is not an endorsement of everything an organization does.

### Something Worth Sharing

Not everything worth telling needs to be political, historical, or the result of an investigation.

Sometimes it's something you learned after a disappointment. An act of kindness. A different way of seeing something familiar. A lesson that took years to understand.

Or simply a thought you want to share.

*Something Worth Sharing* offers space for personal reflections, experiences, practical lessons, and poetry.

Contributions are reviewed before publication, and contributors approve the final version before it appears on the website.

Submitting something doesn't guarantee publication. Please don't include private information about other people. We aren't currently accepting photographs through this submission process.

### A question worth keeping

I didn't create The Missing Seat because I want everyone to agree with me.

I created it because too many decisions are accepted without enough people understanding how they were made, whose interests they served, or which alternatives were never seriously considered.

We talk a lot about everyone having a voice.

But having a voice and having a meaningful say in a decision are two different things.

**Who was missing from the conversation?**

And perhaps more importantly:

**What might have been different if those people had been included before the decision was made?**
