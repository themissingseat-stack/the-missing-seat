# TMS Research Ledger — Supreme Court, Corruption, and Accountability

Working editorial: **When Corruption Stops Counting: How the Supreme Court Has Narrowed America's Anti-Corruption Laws**

Status: Research verified and expanded. Not yet published.

## Core editorial thesis

The Supreme Court has not declared corruption legal. It has repeatedly narrowed the conduct federal bribery, fraud, honest-services, and campaign-finance law may reach. The editorial should distinguish:
1. what each decision actually held,
2. the Court's stated reasons,
3. the practical enforcement gap critics identify, and
4. which other accountability mechanisms remain effective.

## Verified Supreme Court decisions

### Skilling v. United States (2010)
- 18 U.S.C. §1346 honest-services fraud is limited to bribery and kickback schemes.
- Court's concern: broader readings would raise vagueness and due-process problems.
- Editorial significance: undisclosed self-dealing that is not a bribe/kickback falls outside §1346.

### Citizens United v. FEC (2010)
- Independent corporate and union expenditures receive First Amendment protection.
- Majority rejected ingratiation and access, standing alone, as corruption sufficient to justify the expenditure ban.
- Editorial significance: campaign-finance doctrine narrows cognizable corruption toward quid-pro-quo exchange.

### McCutcheon v. FEC (2014)
- Aggregate contribution limits struck down.
- Plurality described the permissible anti-corruption interest as quid-pro-quo corruption or its appearance.
- Influence or access alone was not enough.

### McDonnell v. United States (2016)
- "Official act" requires a decision or action on a specific and focused governmental question or matter involving formal governmental power.
- Setting up a meeting, making a call, or hosting an event does not alone qualify.
- Court emphasized fair notice, federalism, and the risk of criminalizing ordinary representative activity.
- Editorial significance: valuable access and introductions may be relevant evidence but are not themselves the official act required for the bribery theory at issue.

### Kelly v. United States (2020)
- Bridgegate convictions reversed because the federal property-fraud statutes require money or property to be the object of the scheme.
- The Court expressly described the conduct as wrongdoing, deception, corruption, and abuse of power, while holding those statutes did not criminalize all such conduct.
- Editorial significance: wrongful political abuse is not automatically federal fraud.

### Percoco v. United States (2023)
- A private citizen can sometimes owe a fiduciary duty to the public, but the jury instruction used in Percoco was too vague.
- Court rejected an ill-defined test based on a private person's political "clout."
- Editorial significance: influence without a sufficiently defined public duty cannot itself sustain honest-services liability.

### Ciminelli v. United States (2023)
- Rejected the "right-to-control" theory of wire fraud.
- Federal wire fraud protects traditional property interests.
- Editorial significance: deceptive procurement conduct cannot be converted into property fraud merely because a party was deprived of economically valuable information.

### Snyder v. United States (2024)
- 18 U.S.C. §666 prohibits bribes to state/local officials, not after-the-fact gratuities.
- Majority relied on text, statutory structure, sentencing anomalies, federalism, and state/local gratuity regimes.
- Dissent argued the statutory term "rewarded" naturally covers certain after-the-fact payments.
- Editorial significance: a corrupt reward after official action is not automatically covered by §666, although other federal or state laws may still apply.

### National Republican Senatorial Committee v. FEC (2026)
- Court struck down limits on coordinated political-party expenditures with candidates.
- Majority held the limits disproportionate given other anti-circumvention mechanisms and First Amendment interests.
- Dissent argued the caps were an important protection against donor circumvention.
- Editorial significance: further narrows permissible campaign-finance restrictions justified by anti-corruption interests.

## Verified accountability evidence

### GAO — FY2025
- GAO reported **$62.7 billion in financial benefits** from its work in fiscal year 2025.
- GAO reported **1,295 non-financial benefits** in FY2025.
- Since 2002, GAO reports about **$1.51 trillion in financial benefits**.

### Open GAO recommendations — 2026
- GAO estimates implementing current open recommendations could produce **$132 billion to $251 billion** in future financial benefits.
- Example: equalizing certain Medicare payment rates across settings could save an estimated **$156.9 billion over 10 years**.
- GAO reports agencies typically implement more than 75% of its recommendations.
- This supports an important editorial counterpoint: accountability mechanisms can produce large measurable results even when criminal public-corruption law is narrow.

## Recommended structural addition to editorial

Add a section after the Supreme Court case chain:

### "Accountability Still Works — When Somebody Has to Show the Receipts"

Use GAO as the strongest example:
- Court rulings constrain criminal prosecution.
- Audits, inspectors general, procurement oversight, ethics rules, disclosure systems, and state law remain separate accountability channels.
- GAO's FY2025 results show structural oversight can deliver measurable financial and operational benefits.
- The strongest TMS question is not "Does accountability work?" but "Which accountability mechanism still has teeth, who must implement its findings, and what happens when recommendations are ignored?"

## Race/civil-rights material — keep separate from corruption article

The user's pasted material also discusses the Supreme Court and racial equality. That is a separate editorial thesis and should not be folded casually into the corruption piece.

### Louisiana v. Callais (2026)
Verified holding:
- The Court held the Voting Rights Act did not require Louisiana to create the additional majority-minority district at issue.
- Therefore no compelling interest justified the specific race-based map, which the Court held was an unconstitutional racial gerrymander.
- The majority did **not** hold that compliance with §2 of the Voting Rights Act can never be a compelling interest; the opinion states that properly construed §2 can provide such an interest.
- Any TMS article must avoid the broader inaccurate shorthand that Callais simply held there is "no compelling interest" in race-conscious districting unless intentional discrimination is proven.

## Editorial guardrails

- Do not say "the Supreme Court legalized corruption."
- Do not imply every narrowed federal charge makes the underlying conduct lawful.
- Distinguish criminal statutes, campaign-finance law, ethics rules, state law, civil enforcement, audits, and administrative oversight.
- Note that several major narrowing decisions were unanimous.
- Attribute arguments about enforcement gaps to dissents, scholars, prosecutors, or watchdog groups where appropriate.
- Keep the TMS editorial conclusion focused on the cumulative legal architecture rather than assigning motives to justices.
