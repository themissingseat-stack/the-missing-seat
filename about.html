About The Missing Seat

The story changes when we ask who wasn't in the room.

Decisions are made every day that affect millions of people. Governments pass laws. Courts interpret rights. Institutions establish policies. Companies make promises. Officials announce progress.

But there's a question we don't ask nearly enough.

Who was missing from the conversation that led to the decision?

And what happened to those people afterward?

That's why The Missing Seat exists.

We're interested in more than what happened. We want to know why it happened, who had the power to make it happen, who benefited, and who was left dealing with the consequences.

Sometimes the answers are uncomfortable. Sometimes they challenge what we've been taught. And sometimes the people responsible for explaining a decision are the very people who made it.

That deserves a closer look.

The person behind The Missing Seat

My name is Jansen Bowden. I'm African American, I've traveled around the world, experienced different cultures and ways of life, and made my home primarily in Germany.

Traveling and living in different places have given me opportunities to see how people think, how societies function, and how differently we sometimes understand the same ideas.

Freedom. Fairness. Opportunity. Responsibility.

We use these words everywhere. But they don't always mean the same thing in practice.

I've seen how easy it is for people to accept something simply because it's familiar. Because that's how things have always been done. Because someone in authority says it's necessary.

I've also learned that a country doesn't have to share another country's history to repeat some of its mistakes.

And living outside the United States hasn't made me any less interested in what happens there. If anything, distance has given me another perspective from which to question things I once took for granted.

I don't believe every problem has a simple solution. I don't believe every disagreement means someone has bad intentions. And I certainly don't believe I have all the answers.

But I do believe we should be willing to examine the answers we've already been given.

That's the thinking behind The Missing Seat.

What we're trying to do

Information is everywhere. Understanding is another matter.

You can read a headline, hear a politician make a statement, or watch an institution announce a reform and still have little idea what actually changed.

Who made the decision? What evidence supported it? Was there another option? Who gained something? Who lost something? And did anyone bother asking the people most affected?

We examine those questions through reporting, historical research, analysis, and commentary.

Our editorials follow six basic questions:

  1. What happened?
  2. What went wrong?
  3. Who benefited?
  4. Who carried the cost?
  5. Who made it possible?
  6. What can people do now?

The last question matters enormously.

There's little value in explaining a problem so thoroughly that readers understand every detail but come away believing nothing can be done.

Sometimes action means contacting an elected representative. Sometimes it means finding an overlooked document, challenging a public claim, supporting an existing effort, or simply asking a better question.

Not every problem can be solved immediately.

That doesn't mean it should go unexamined.

Facts, opinions, and uncomfortable questions

The Missing Seat has a perspective. We don't hide that.

We believe human dignity, equal rights, fairness, and accountability deserve more than ceremonial recognition.

But having principles doesn't give us permission to bend the evidence.

We prefer original documents, legislation, court records, official data, and other primary sources wherever possible. Credible journalism and research help us establish context and investigate claims.

We separate what can be verified from what we believe, interpret, or still need to investigate.

A source doesn't prove something simply because it comes from a respected institution. Its evidence must actually support the claim.

And when the facts don't support an argument, that argument needs to change.

Including our own.

Independent doesn't mean indifferent

The Missing Seat is an independent editorial and civic-information project. We don't represent a political party, government, corporation, or advocacy organization.

It is funded by its founder, and no outside organization finances its work.

We aren't interested in defending an institution merely because we agree with its stated goals. Nor do we assume every decision is wrong because we disagree with the people making it.

We look at what was promised, what was done, and what happened afterward.

History matters here.

When an injustice produces consequences that continue for generations, acknowledging the original injustice isn't necessarily the same as repairing it.

When someone announces progress, we want to know what that progress is being measured against.

And when powerful institutions demand accountability from everyone else, we believe they should be prepared to answer difficult questions themselves.

The same standard applies to us.

Making complicated things understandable

You shouldn't need a law degree to understand a decision affecting your rights.

You shouldn't need to be an economist to ask where public money went.

And you shouldn't have to spend hours learning institutional terminology just to understand why a government, a court, or a company acted the way it did.

We aim to write plainly without oversimplifying the facts.

If something is complicated, we'll explain why. If the evidence is contradictory, we'll show the contradiction. If an important question remains unanswered, we'll say so.

We don't expect readers to agree with every conclusion.

We expect them to think.

And we believe they deserve enough information to reach conclusions of their own.

How we use AI

We use AI tools to help with research and drafting. Jansen Bowden is responsible for what is published.

When we get something wrong

We will make mistakes.

What matters is how we respond.

Confirmed factual errors are corrected. Important new information may lead to updates. Substantial corrections should be clearly identified, not quietly hidden.

We welcome readers who question our reasoning, challenge our interpretations, or bring evidence we haven't considered.

Being challenged isn't the problem.

Refusing to examine a legitimate challenge is.

Something Worth Sharing

Not everything worth saying needs to be political, historical, or investigative.

Sometimes it's something learned through disappointment. A moment of kindness. A change of perspective. A lesson that took years to understand.

Or a thought that simply deserves to be shared.

Something Worth Sharing is a space for personal reflections, practical lessons, and poetry.

Contributions are reviewed before publication, and contributors approve the final version of their work before it appears on the site.

Submitting something doesn't guarantee publication. Please don't include another person's private information. Photographs aren't currently accepted through this submission process.

What we are not

The Missing Seat is not a law firm, a legal aid service or a political party. We explain rights and point to organizations that can help. We cannot give legal advice or represent you. A listing in our directory is not an endorsement of everything an organization does.

Contact and contributions

One question worth keeping

I didn't create The Missing Seat because I think everyone should agree with me.

I created it because too many decisions are accepted without enough people understanding how they were made, whose interests they served, or what alternatives were ignored.

We talk a great deal about having a voice.

But having a voice and having a meaningful place in the conversation aren't always the same thing.

Who was missing from the conversation?

And perhaps more importantly:

What would have been different if they had been included before the decision was made?

“We aren't taking America's stories away. We're subpoenaing them.”
