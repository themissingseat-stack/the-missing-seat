RACE, CRIME & CONTEXT

“Black-on-Black Crime”: Naming the Violence Without Hiding Its History

Violence within Black communities is real. Its victims deserve safety, justice and public concern. But the phrase “Black-on-Black crime” is often used to isolate Black violence from the conditions, policies and patterns surrounding it.

Seat Summary

Seat Affected: Black victims’ right to safety without having their suffering used against their entire community

Seat Status: Recognized selectively

Location: United States

Theme: Violence, racial language, public policy and historical context

Let us begin without evasion: when a Black person kills, assaults or terrorizes another Black person, the violence is real. The victim’s suffering doesn’t become less important because the offender shares the victim’s race.

Families deserve answers. Communities deserve protection. Offenders should be held accountable through a fair legal process.

But accountability requires accuracy, and “Black-on-Black crime” is not a neutral description.

Most violence is local

Violent crime usually occurs between people whose lives place them near one another. People tend to live, work, socialize and attend school among others from the same communities. Because the United States remains heavily separated by residence, income and race, much violent crime is intraracial.

Bureau of Justice Statistics data for 2012 through 2015 found that most violent victimizations involving White victims and Black victims were committed by offenders perceived to be of the same race as the victim. Fifty-seven percent of White victims’ offenders were White. Sixty-three percent of Black victims’ offenders were Black.

Yet public discourse rarely treats violence between White people as a special racial category called “White-on-White crime.”

That difference tells us the phrase is doing more than describing the race of the people involved.

What the phrase is often used to do

“Black-on-Black crime” is frequently introduced when Black people raise police violence, racial discrimination or unequal treatment by the justice system.

The response is familiar: Why talk about what the police did when Black people are killing one another?

But one form of violence doesn’t cancel another.

A government officer’s use of force raises questions about public authority, constitutional limits, training, supervision and state accountability. Violence committed by a private person raises different legal and institutional questions.

Both matter. Neither excuses the other.

We don’t respond to financial fraud by saying domestic violence also exists. We don’t dismiss unsafe working conditions because people die in traffic collisions. Different harms require different evidence, remedies and responsible institutions.

Crime doesn’t emerge outside history

Naming personal responsibility doesn’t require pretending that public policy had no role in shaping the conditions in which violence occurs.

Black communities were subjected to slavery, racial terror, legal segregation, housing discrimination, school inequality, employment exclusion, discriminatory policing and the destruction or displacement of Black neighborhoods.

These weren’t abstract cultural influences. They affected where people could live, what property they could own, which schools they could attend, what employment was available and whether institutions treated them as citizens entitled to protection.

The history doesn’t make an individual offender innocent. It does make the public explanation incomplete when it blames an entire community while removing the policies that helped shape concentrated poverty, instability and unequal access to protection.

Black communities have never ignored the violence

The claim that Black Americans care about violence only when the offender is White erases the people working against violence inside Black communities every day.

Families organize vigils. Residents form intervention programs. Clergy open their buildings. Parents search for witnesses. Community workers mediate conflicts. Survivors speak publicly. Local organizations help young people find safety, employment and alternatives.

Much of that work receives little national attention because it doesn’t serve the political purpose for which the phrase “Black-on-Black crime” is often used.

Black victims deserve more than political use

Black victims are sometimes invoked by people who oppose the housing, education, health, employment and violence-prevention policies that could help protect them.

Their deaths become evidence in an argument about Black pathology, but their lives don’t receive the same sustained investment.

If concern is genuine, it should continue after the debate ends.

Did the family receive support? Was the case solved? Were witnesses protected? Was trauma care available? Did young people in the neighborhood receive safe places to go? Did the public institutions responsible for prevention change anything?

We can condemn violence within Black communities without turning Black suffering into an excuse for abandoning those communities.

Name the violence accurately

Say homicide. Say assault. Say domestic violence. Say robbery. Say gun violence. Say community violence. Identify the act, the victim, the offender and the conditions supported by the evidence.

Don’t turn race itself into the crime.

Black people aren’t asking for violence to be ignored. They are asking for their lives to matter consistently: when the offender is Black, when the offender is White and when the person causing the harm carries the authority of the state.

The honest position is not either-or.

Protect Black communities from interpersonal violence. Protect them from institutional violence. Hold individuals accountable. Hold governments accountable. Investigate the immediate act. Preserve the history that shaped the conditions.

That is not making excuses. That is refusing to hide part of the truth.

Verification Notice

Crime data cited here comes from the National Crime Victimization Survey and reflects victims’ perceptions of offender characteristics. Survey estimates have methodological limits and shouldn’t be used to assign criminality to an entire racial population.
