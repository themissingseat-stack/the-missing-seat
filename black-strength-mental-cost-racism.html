RACE & EQUAL HUMANITY • MENTAL HEALTH

America Keeps Measuring Black Anger. Who Is Measuring Black Strength?

America scrutinizes Black anger. It should also examine the strength it takes to keep going, the psychological cost, and the support still missing.

Sometimes I get angry. Sometimes I get frustrated when life is hard for no good goddamn reason, especially when the obstacle didn't have to be there in the first place. I can accept that life comes with problems. What wears me down is having to deal with problems people created, protected, and then expect me to discuss politely.

When that happens, I want a question asked before anybody starts evaluating my attitude: What have I been carrying?

America has spent generations judging Black people's reactions. The anger. The tone. The protest. The refusal to smile through something that should never have happened. I want us to spend more time examining the strength it takes to keep living, loving, working, raising children, and building something while carrying burdens we didn't choose.

That history stretches from slavery and family separation through racial terror, segregation, and exclusion. Laws changed. Rights were won. Those victories mattered. But a change in law doesn't automatically restore a family's lost wealth, remove discrimination from daily life, or give somebody back the years spent learning when it was safe to speak.

There is a psychological question inside that history: What does it cost to keep adapting to conditions that should have been changed?

Researchers have been studying that question for years. A 2012 analysis brought together 66 studies involving 18,140 people and found an association between reported experiences of racism and psychological distress among Black American adults. Anxiety, depression, and other psychiatric symptoms were among the outcomes examined. This is evidence of a relationship across studies, not a diagnosis of every Black person or proof that racism explains every individual difficulty. [1]

Another study examined what researchers call racism-related vigilance. In everyday language, that means staying prepared for discrimination: anticipating it, thinking about it, and being on guard. In that study, Black adults reported more vigilance than white adults, and greater vigilance was associated with greater sleep difficulty. The study doesn't establish that vigilance caused every sleep problem. It does show why the burden deserves attention even when no new confrontation is happening. [2]

Think about that. An incident can end while the preparation for the next one continues.

For some people, this can mean rehearsing what to say, calculating whether a complaint will make things worse, or wondering whether ordinary frustration will be read as aggression. These experiences won't describe every Black person's life. But we need room to talk about them without demanding that somebody prove they are suffering badly enough to deserve a hearing.

Psychiatry itself has a history to answer for. A 2024 historical study examined medical publications and other primary sources from slavery through Jim Crow, tracing how racist theories shaped psychiatric treatment of Black people. Its authors argue that this history still matters for how care is practiced and understood today. Distrust deserves investigation rather than dismissal as ignorance. [3]

Today's picture is complicated, and we should say so honestly. The latest figures on the federal Office of Minority Health page use 2024 survey data. They report mental illness in the previous year for 20.9% of Black adults, compared with 23.4% of adults overall. Those numbers do not support a sweeping claim that Black Americans have higher rates of every mental illness. [4]

But the same page shows a treatment gap. Among adults who experienced a major depressive episode, 52.1% of Black adults received depression treatment, compared with 64.4% overall. That is a more useful comparison than assuming that every difference in overall treatment use reflects unmet need. Even this comparison doesn't tell us, on its own, precisely why the gap exists. [4]

So we need to ask about access, cost, trust, treatment quality, and whether people feel understood when they finally get through the door. A system cannot call itself responsive merely because a service exists.

And we need to talk about strength without turning it into another obligation.

Black people have created joy, culture, families, institutions, movements, and possibilities under conditions that denied their humanity and limited their freedom. Our lives contain far more than injury. That deserves recognition. But praising resilience can become an easy way to avoid asking why so much resilience keeps being required.

The strong person still needs rest. The dependable person still needs help. The person who makes everybody laugh can still be exhausted. Functioning is not a complete account of what functioning costs.

Sometimes anger tells you that something is wrong. Sometimes it arrives alongside grief, fear, exhaustion, or the feeling that you've explained the same injury too many times. We should be able to take that seriously while remaining responsible for how we treat other people. Understanding anger doesn't require excusing harm. It requires looking beyond the reaction.

I don't want Black people reduced to a diagnosis. I also don't want our ability to survive used as evidence that the conditions were acceptable.

Who is missing when these conversations happen? The person who keeps going and is therefore assumed to be fine. The worker who needs support before a crisis. The child learning to manage other people's prejudice. The family whose strength gets celebrated while its requests for help go unanswered.

Meaningful recognition needs consequences. Employers should deal with discrimination complaints and examine what happened afterward. Mental health services should make care affordable and take racial stress seriously. Public institutions should measure whether people actually receive useful help, not simply count referrals, awareness campaigns, or appointments offered.

We can start closer to home, too. Ask someone what they're carrying and listen without immediately correcting their tone. If someone says they need help, don't answer by telling them how strong they are. Offer something concrete: time, company, practical assistance, or help finding care that respects their experience.

For The Missing Seat, the question is straightforward: Where is strength being praised while support is being withheld? Readers can help document that gap by describing the institution involved, the help requested, the response received, and what remains unresolved, sharing only what they are comfortable making public.

I want recognition of Black strength. I want recognition of its cost. And I want that recognition to lead to fewer unnecessary burdens.

We deserve more than compliments about how much we can take.
