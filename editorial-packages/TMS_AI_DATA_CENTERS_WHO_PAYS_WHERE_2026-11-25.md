## The Short Version

**What happened:** Loudoun County, Virginia, says data centers generated $1.2 billion in taxes in fiscal 2026 and are projected to provide about 40 percent of its fiscal 2027 budget. Across the Mississippi border from Memphis, the NAACP is suing over allegedly unpermitted turbines powering an xAI data center. In Maryland, a Black mother and her coalition helped put a $5 billion project on hold.

**Who it affects:** Neighborhoods where data centers are built, especially ones that already carry pollution and have little say in what gets built.

**What you can do:** Demand the Loudoun deal. If a data center comes to your area, ask for the same tax terms, a signed community benefits agreement and a public hearing before approval.

## Website editorial

Taylor Frazier McCollum did not own a home. In 2024, she and her young daughter were evicted from their apartment. They were living in her grandfather's house in Landover, Maryland, when she heard a developer had approval for a $5 billion data center campus less than a mile away, on the land of an old shopping mall. [1]

She started a petition. It gathered more than 25,000 signatures, led to well-attended protests, and helped produce local moratoriums on data centers. The project, which had been set to break ground in 2026 or 2027, is now on hold. [1] She did not do it alone. Longtime environmental activists, residents angry about their electric bills and new organizers stood with her. But she became the face of it, and she has taken her campaign to Black communities fighting data centers in Atlanta, Memphis and Zambia. [1]

Her question was simple: why put a building like that, with diesel generators and heavy water use, down the street from where people live, work and play? [2]

### Who is getting rich

The money is enormous. An April 30 report, citing the Financial Times's compilation of first-quarter earnings, put Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft's planned 2026 capital spending at about $725 billion, up 77 percent from 2025. It was a forecast, not money already spent, and included investment in data centers and their equipment. [3] Nvidia, which makes the chips, reported $115.8 billion in revenue in a single quarter. (We covered that in "The AI Boom Has a Customer.") Shareholders, chipmakers, developers and utilities are all being paid.

The question is who gets the money, and who gets the noise, the turbines and the risk.

### When a rich county hosts data centers, it gets paid

Loudoun County, Virginia, has the highest concentration of data centers in the world. [4] It is also often called America's richest county, with a median household income above $170,000. [5]

Data centers there pay. The county reports $1.2 billion in real and personal property tax revenue in fiscal year 2026, representing 39 percent of its budget. Its adopted fiscal 2027 budget projects $1.3 billion, about 40 percent. [4] The county says that revenue has helped it lower the homeowner real property tax rate through the past decade. [6]

That windfall has not made the problems disappear. On November 17, Loudoun's board formally adopted a pause on certain data-center and substation applications while it revises its development standards. The county's announcement describes residents' concerns about noise, air quality and energy infrastructure near homes. Rich communities deserve those protections, and so does everyone else. [16]

So the idea of putting them in a rich neighborhood is not hypothetical. A rich county took them, set the rules, and collected the checks.

### When a Black neighborhood is next door, it can get the turbines

In Memphis, Elon Musk's company xAI built Colossus, an AI supercomputer, near a long-burdened area of southwest Memphis. [7] To power Colossus 2, its company installed gas turbines just across the state line in Southaven, Mississippi, near homes, schools and churches. [8]

The national NAACP and its Mississippi State Conference sued xAI and its subsidiary MZX Tech on April 14, alleging that the Southaven plant operated without required air permits. Earthjustice and the Southern Environmental Law Center represent them. [9, 12] In their May 6 request for emergency action, the groups said the fleet had grown to 33 turbines, with the potential to emit more than 2,500 tons of smog-forming nitrogen oxides annually, along with fine particles and formaldehyde. That was an estimate of potential emissions, not a measurement of pollution actually released. [8] The companies dispute the suit; they argued that the turbines qualified for a mobile-source permitting exception. On October 30, the court rejected that argument and denied dismissal, allowing the claims to proceed. That ruling did not award damages or finally resolve every claim. [12]

Boxtown, near xAI's original Memphis campus, is reported to be about 90 percent Black, with a median household income around $37,000. [13] That campus and the Southaven turbine plant serving Colossus 2 are distinct sites.

The comparison is about what communities receive and what they must fight for. Loudoun's revenue is documented. The Southaven lawsuit seeks to enforce pollution protections.

### Is this a pattern?

Researchers disagree, and that matters.

A November report by researchers at George Washington University and American University compared more than 81,000 census tracts with records for 5,291 data-center sites, including operating, proposed and other development statuses. It found roughly 2.2 times the odds of hosting a site in a majority-Black neighborhood compared with an otherwise similar majority-white neighborhood in the same state. That is an adjusted association, not a finding that every listed facility operates or that any particular developer acted from racial intent. [14]

A May 2026 study from UC Berkeley's Energy Institute looked at past, current and planned data centers and found they are not systematically located in lower-income, higher-poverty or more nonwhite communities. [15]

Those findings differ. Their datasets and comparisons need to be understood before either becomes a blanket claim about all data centers. The November report also distinguishes large hyperscale campuses from smaller colocation facilities and the benefits associated with them. [14] For TMS, the practical question remains: what protections and revenue will each particular community receive, and will residents have a say before construction?

### Level the playing field: the Loudoun deal for everyone

If a data center is good enough for America's richest county, the deal Loudoun gets should be the floor everywhere else. Any community asked to host one should get the same payout, not a discount.

- **The same tax deal.** No special tax breaks for a data center next to a working-class or Black neighborhood that the company would not get in Loudoun. If the equipment inside is taxed in a rich county, it gets taxed in a poor one.
- **The money stays local.** A clear share of the revenue goes to the neighborhood that carries the risk, in schools, health clinics, lower utility bills and property tax relief, the way Loudoun homeowners have seen their tax rate cut for a decade.
- **Signed before shovels.** A written community benefits agreement, with amounts and deadlines, before approval. Not a promise after the turbines are running.
- **No permit, no power.** Any on-site power plant gets a full air permit, with public notice, before it runs.
- **A real hearing.** Neighbors hear about a project before approval, not after.
- **The company pays for its own load.** Grid upgrades, water and cleanup are paid by the companies that profit, not by the families next door.

If a company says equivalent protections and local benefits cost too much, it should publish the numbers and explain why the people carrying the burden should accept less.

Taylor Frazier McCollum wants a full ban on the Landover project and others like it. [1] Whether a community says yes or no, it deserves the same terms, the same respect and the same check as Loudoun. So does Boxtown.

## Pull Up A Chair

- **Look up your county's zoning or planning agenda** and search for "data center" or "large load".
- **Ask four questions in writing:** What will power it? Is the company getting a tax break Loudoun wouldn't give? How much of the money stays in our neighborhood? When is the public hearing?
- **Check air permits** at your state environmental agency before any on-site generator runs.
- **Join or start a petition.** A petition from one evicted mother passed 25,000 signatures.
