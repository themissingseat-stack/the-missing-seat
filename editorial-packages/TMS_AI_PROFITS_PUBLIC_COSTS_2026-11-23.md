## Website editorial

An AI company can announce a new computing facility with a promise of investment and jobs. A household may encounter the same development later in a utility bill. Between those two moments, regulators and companies negotiate who will pay for power infrastructure and who carries the risk if the project changes. That is where the public needs a seat before the commitments are signed.

Nvidia's November 17 earnings release reported $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue, including $107.6 billion from its data-center business. These are sales figures, and the category reaches beyond AI alone. They show the commercial scale of supplying the computing expansion. They do not show a local household's benefit, a developer's net profit or the complete public cost of a particular project. [1]

The mechanism worth following is electricity cost allocation. FERC's Office of Public Participation explains that large-load connections can significantly affect transmission planning and operations. Its educational account describes the interaction between federal transmission regulation and state decisions affecting retail customers. The actual payment obligation comes from applicable orders, tariffs and agreements. An educational explanation helps readers find those records. [2]

On June 18, FERC directed six regional grid operators and relevant transmission operators to justify or reform their rules for connecting large users. Its announcement combined faster access to electricity with ratepayer safeguards. That combination needs examination in each resulting proceeding. Investor certainty and consumer protection can appear in the same announcement while the detailed allocation remains contested. [3]

The questions are specific. Which upgrades serve the proposed customer? What commitment requires that customer to pay? How much security is provided if it withdraws? Who pays for capacity built ahead of its arrival? Are the assumptions public enough for a consumer advocate to test? An existing residential customer cannot negotiate an individual contract with the company responsible for the new demand.

This does not justify assigning every increase in electricity prices to AI. Fuel, weather, replacement of old equipment and other investment can affect costs. A credible account needs the utility's calculation and the regulator's allocation. The appropriate test is the amount that would otherwise fall on households and whether a binding agreement assigns it elsewhere.

The same discipline applies to recent software outages. A technical failure deserves its own incident evidence. The proximity of an outage and an AI announcement does not establish a common cause. The editorial question is whether institutions fund maintenance, redundancy and accountable operation with the same attention they give expansion. A product can be useful while its financing or operating arrangements deserve scrutiny.

A proposed public-interest standard would require publication of dedicated infrastructure costs, payment guarantees, major subsidies and the consequences of cancellation. It would also identify the public authority responsible for enforcement. Communities need a way to compare forecast benefits with what is actually delivered after construction.

Readers should locate their state utility commission's data-center or large-load tariff proceedings and contact the state consumer advocate. Ask for the docket number and the provision protecting existing customers if the project fails to meet its forecast. The money in an earnings announcement is easy to count. The obligation in a utility agreement deserves equally close attention.

## Pull Up A Chair

Identify your electric utility and state regulator. Ask the consumer advocate for the relevant large-load docket, proposed rate allocation, cancellation security and comment procedure. Use FERC's participation guidance for federal transmission questions.

## November 23 reporting update: Huang's return and the trading question

Nvidia's SEC Form 8-K states that the board reinstated Jensen Huang on November 22, effective immediately. The public announcement followed on November 23. The company describes a unanimous decision following meetings with Huang and input from senior U.S. government officials. Its independent investigation is continuing. Reinstatement therefore precedes the investigation's conclusion. Harvey Jones remains board chair, and Colette Kress continues as CFO.

Reuters reports that Trump told CNBC he spoke with Huang on Sunday night, considered his intervention important and was represented at the board meeting. This is Trump's public account. Nvidia separately confirms government input without identifying the officials or releasing the advice. The reporting questions are who supplied the assurance, what evidence supported it, when the board changed its position and who learned the decision before the announcement.

Yahoo Finance reports a more-than-30-percent premarket rise and an approximately $1.2 trillion decline in Nvidia's market capitalization from the November 11 close through Friday November 20. These are dated reporting figures, not a verified final Monday closing price or realized investor profits. Exchange volume does not identify unique buyers, and a change in total market value cannot be treated as money received by the company or a particular trader.

The number and identities of people who bought during the decline and sold profitably after the return have not been established in this research. Covered insider Form 4 filings generally have a two-business-day deadline, with exceptions. Institutional Form 13F reports are quarterly holdings snapshots, generally due within 45 days of quarter-end, and cannot reconstruct every trade or reveal all investors. An investigation should compare decision and disclosure timestamps with stock and options activity, then examine legally available ownership and transaction reports. A well-timed trade requires further evidence about information and conduct before alleging unlawful insider trading.
