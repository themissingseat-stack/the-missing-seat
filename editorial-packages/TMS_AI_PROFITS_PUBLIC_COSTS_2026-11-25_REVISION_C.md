## The Short Version

**What happened:** Nvidia's board brought Jensen Huang back as CEO on November 23, eleven days after he resigned amid an investigation into his relationship with a woman U.S. officials believed was a Chinese intelligence asset. The board said it acted after input from senior levels of the U.S. government. President Trump said his administration gave the board assurances. The stock jumped more than 30 percent in a day. The president's own Nvidia shares, by his disclosures, may have gained between $1.3 million and $14.6 million on paper.

**Who it affects:** Investors who were not in the room, the public whose security questions are still under investigation, and electricity customers paying for the grid the AI boom needs.

**What you can do:** Ask your state utility commission and consumer advocate who pays for data-center power upgrades. Ask your members of Congress to publish what assurances the government gave Nvidia's board, and on what evidence.

## Website editorial

Two weeks ago, Nvidia's board was investigating its chief executive. On Monday, it gave him his job back.

Jensen Huang resigned on November 12. The company's filing says the board accepted his resignation the day before. [1] His departure followed New York Times reporting on his relationship with Alice Huang, an executive tied to Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data-center company that bought Nvidia chips. U.S. officials believed she was an asset of China's Ministry of State Security. China denies it. [2] Huang had already admitted to employees that he was unfaithful to his wife. A second reported relationship, with an Nvidia senior vice president who reported to him, followed. [3]

The board hired Joon Kim, a former acting U.S. attorney, and the law firm Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz. The question was whether Huang's conduct had compromised national security or company secrets. [4] As late as November 16, people familiar with the board's thinking said it was not considering bringing him back. That same day, President Trump told reporters he "would not mind" a return. [5]

Seven days later, the board voted unanimously to reinstate him. Its statement says it was satisfied with Huang's "candor and ability to lead." It also says it received input from senior levels of the U.S. government and, based on that input, is confident his return poses no national security risk. The same statement says the independent committee's investigation will continue to its conclusion. [6]

Read that order of events slowly. The board reached its national-security conclusion before its own investigation finished, and it reached it on the strength of government assurances that have not been made public.

### What the president said, and what he owns

Trump has described his role. He told CNBC, "We gave them assurances, at least with everything we know." He has said he spoke with the board directly. [7]

Nvidia's stock rose more than 30 percent on Monday, its best day since May 2003, adding close to $1 trillion in market value. [8]

The president is an Nvidia shareholder. His financial disclosure, filed November 5 with the Office of Government Ethics, shows he held between 32,000 and 370,000 shares as of September 30. Applied to Monday's rise of nearly $40 a share, that range works out to a paper gain of $1.3 million to $14.6 million. [9] The disclosure does not show trades in October or November, so it cannot say what he held on Monday. These are not realized profits. The stock had fallen sharply after Huang resigned, so some of Monday's gain may simply have recovered earlier losses.

The White House says the president's portfolio is run by independent managers. A spokesman said neither the president nor any member of his family has any ability to direct or provide input on how it is invested or when. [10]

TMS is not alleging insider trading. We do not know who bought or sold Nvidia stock before the announcement, and a well-timed trade is not proof of wrongdoing. The problem is simpler. A president used the weight of his office on a private company's board, the board says that weight helped decide the matter, and the president owns shares of that company. That is a conflict of interest in plain view, whoever manages the account.

### Questions that still need answers

- **Who gave the assurances?** Which officials spoke to the board, what did they say, and what evidence did they rely on?
- **Did the assurances get ahead of the investigators?** Federal investigators are examining Megaspeed. [11] The Wall Street Journal has reported that the SEC is looking at whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors. [12] The Senate Banking Committee has sought Huang's testimony in early December. [13]
- **Who knew before the market did?** Covered insiders must generally report trades within two business days. [20] A full answer would compare the decision timeline with trading records.

### Where the public pays: the electricity bill

The AI boom runs on chips, and the chips run on electricity. Nvidia reported $115.8 billion in revenue for its latest quarter, $107.6 billion of it from data centers. [14] Those data centers need power plants and power lines, and much of that cost lands on ordinary customers.

On PJM, the country's largest power grid, which covers 13 states and Washington, D.C., capacity prices for the year that began June 1, 2026 cleared at $329.17 per megawatt-day. That is the auction's cap, and roughly ten times the price two years earlier. [15] PJM's independent market monitor found data centers responsible for 63 percent of the price increase in the 2025/2026 auction, about $9.3 billion. [15]

That shows up on real bills. Maryland's Office of People's Counsel estimated that the last two capacity auctions added about $16.49 a month for a typical Baltimore Gas and Electric household and $20.81 for Potomac Edison customers, before some partial credits. The People's Counsel said residential customers are not causing these costs and should not be paying for them. [16]

Some regulators are pushing back. In Ohio, AEP's data-center tariff requires large data-center customers to pay minimum monthly bills and exit fees if they leave early, so households are not stuck paying for lines built for projects that never arrive. [17] Industrial customers and tech companies appealed. The case is before the Ohio Supreme Court, and TMS found no ruling as of November 25. [18] On June 18, federal regulators ordered all six regional grid operators to justify or reform their rules for connecting data centers, including who pays for upgrades. [19]

### The same question twice

The boardroom and the utility bill are separate stories, but they share one question: who decides, and who carries the risk?

In the boardroom, a handful of directors and the president settled a national-security question while the investigation was still open, and the market moved a trillion dollars. On the grid, utilities and regulators decide whether a household in Baltimore helps pay for a data center in Virginia. In both rooms, the people most affected had no seat.

## Pull Up A Chair

- **Find your utility's data-center docket.** Search your state utility commission's site for "large load" or "data center tariff," or call your state consumer advocate's office and ask for the docket number.
- **Ask one question in writing:** If a data center cancels or uses less power than promised, who pays for the lines already built?
- **Contact your members of Congress.** Ask them to request the government assurances Nvidia's board relied on, and the officials who gave them.
- **Read disclosures yourself.** The president's periodic transaction reports are public at the Office of Government Ethics.
