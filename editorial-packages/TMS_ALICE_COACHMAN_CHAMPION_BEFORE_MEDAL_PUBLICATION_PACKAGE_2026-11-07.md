## Full editorial

Alice Coachman did not arrive at the 1948 Olympics as a promising beginner who happened to make history. She arrived with a record. USA Track & Field credits her with ten consecutive national high-jump titles from 1939 through 1948. Before the world watched her win, she had been winning for years.

That is the part worth keeping beside the famous first. Coachman became the first Black woman from any country to win an Olympic gold medal. Her place in history is secure. Her sporting achievement deserves more than a sentence about breaking a barrier, followed by a move to the next name on the list.

November 9 is the anniversary of her birth in Albany, Georgia, in 1923. It is a useful occasion to look at what she did: repeated national success, an Olympic record and a life that continued in education. Her story holds joy, skill and ambition as well as the conditions she had to work around.

## The result has detail

In London, Coachman cleared 1.68 metres, an Olympic and American record. Britain's Dorothy Tyler cleared the same height. Olympedia's event record shows that Coachman cleared it on her first attempt, while Tyler needed two. Coachman won gold. This was a close competition with an identifiable deciding performance, not simply a symbolic moment for a photograph.

The national titles give that performance context. USA Track & Field's biography also records sprinting successes and her role on Tuskegee Institute relay teams. High jump was her defining event, but her ability was not confined to one movement or one afternoon. A proper account should let readers meet the athlete.

There were also years when the Olympic opportunity did not exist. The 1940 and 1944 Games were cancelled because of World War II. We cannot award her medals from competitions that never happened. We can recognize that the timing of an international career depends on more than an athlete's readiness, and that her national record was already substantial before London.

## Talent had people around it

Coachman's early training took place under segregation. The U.S. Olympic & Paralympic Museum describes limited access to athletic facilities and improvised practice on dirt roads and homemade equipment. Those details show what she worked around. They should not become instructions telling today's children to make do without a safe place to train.

Albany State's account identifies people who encouraged her: her fifth-grade teacher Cora Bailey and her aunt Carrie Spry. It describes the route from school competition to Tuskegee's high-school programme, then further education. These are useful names and institutions to retain. An individual achievement can be extraordinary while still involving encouragement, coaching and places where someone can develop.

There is a familiar temptation to call a champion proof that barriers cannot hold people back. Coachman's result proves her performance. It does not tell us how many other children lost an opportunity, or what she might have achieved with different access. Her determination deserves credit without being used to excuse the restrictions around her.

At the same time, the restrictions should not swallow the story. She was a competitor who won. The ten-title sequence matters in its own right. People can enjoy the precision and satisfaction of a great sporting record without needing every paragraph to turn into an account of injury.

## A life beyond the podium

Coachman continued her education at Albany State College, now Albany State University, graduating in 1949 in home economics. She became a teacher. The university's account also describes the foundation she established in 1994 to assist young athletes and former Olympic competitors. That is part of her historical life; it is not a checked present-day application or donation route.

Her medal is sometimes treated as though it contains the whole person. Education and teaching widen the picture. They do not need to compete with the sporting achievement for importance. A person can earn a place in history for one result and still have other work worth knowing.

The institutions recording her career also have responsibilities. Sporting bodies hold records; museums decide what visitors learn; schools choose what a name above a door will mean to the children inside. Readers should be able to find the performance behind the honor. The people benefiting from that fuller account include young athletes looking for examples of excellence and anyone who enjoys understanding how sport is won.

There is no need to invent a modern financial remedy to justify telling this story. The record itself has value. A useful public response is to preserve it accurately and make it easier to learn, while giving current children practical access to coaching, equipment and facilities.

## Pull Up A Chair

If you teach or coach, put Coachman's ten national high-jump titles alongside her Olympic result. Ask students to compare two accounts: one that gives only the historic first and one that explains the actual competition. The second gives them more to understand about both her skill and the sport.

For a school or community club marking her birthday, use the governing-body biography and museum profile before circulating a quotation or an attractive claim. If you want to support today's athletes, ask a local programme which specific cost keeps children from participating, such as transport or equipment, and what assistance it already provides. That is a practical choice for your community, not a claim about Coachman's foundation today.
