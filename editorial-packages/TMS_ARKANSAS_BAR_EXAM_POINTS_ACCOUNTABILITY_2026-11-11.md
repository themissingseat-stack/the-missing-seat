### The Short Version · 20-second read
**What happened:** Arkansas prosecutors charged former bar-exam administrator Nancie Givens with 65 felony counts of tampering with public records. The state's Supreme Court says its investigation found evidence that points were transferred between candidates' examination scores between 2020 and 2025. Approximately 11,400 files were deleted or made unavailable. Givens pleaded not guilty.

## Website editorial

Somebody who took the Arkansas bar examination may have been told they failed when they had actually passed. Think about what that decision could have meant. A job offer might have disappeared. A graduate might have paid to sit for the exam again, taken a different job, or spent months explaining a failure that was never theirs. We do not yet know whose life followed that path, or how much anyone lost. The troubling fact is that Arkansas's own Supreme Court says it found evidence of score changes that could have produced exactly that result.

On November 5, 2026, prosecutors charged Nancie Givens, the former executive director of the Arkansas Supreme Court's Office of Professional Programs, with 65 felony counts of tampering with public records. The court described irregularities across at least eight bar-exam administrations between July 2020 and July 2025. Givens pleaded not guilty. These are allegations under criminal investigation, not findings of guilt. The court itself has acknowledged the potential harm and says it is reviewing the records.

The mechanism is unusually disturbing. According to a joint statement issued by six Arkansas justices, investigators found evidence that points were taken from some examinees' scores and added to others in the same examination session. Some altered scores were raised to the passing threshold of 270 or above. The justices also described at least one case in which a candidate who otherwise would have passed may have been given a failing score. In another, the person who actually earned the highest score allegedly lost that distinction because their score was reduced.

That is more than a spreadsheet problem. The bar examination is one gate to becoming a licensed lawyer. It is supposed to measure an applicant's performance under established rules, not the preferences of the person handling the records. Applicants invest years of study, tuition, exam fees and professional expectations in a result they have limited ability to audit themselves. A person denied entry to the profession cannot recover lost time simply because an administrator is later prosecuted.

The number circulating on social media needs care. Roughly 11,400 files were deleted or rendered unavailable, according to the justices. Most included applications, profiles and examination score sheets that court rules required the office to retain for 25 years. That is a count of files, not 11,400 people whose scores were altered. Reporting on the investigative affidavit describes discrepancies affecting at least 64 examinees. Neither figure establishes the final number of people who suffered a wrongful pass, wrongful fail, loss of rank or other damage. That number remains to be established by the official review.

The discovery timeline raises a separate institutional question. Givens resigned in February 2026. Robert Brech, who took responsibility for supervising the office, reviewed its records and found that large numbers of files and electronic communications had been modified or deleted in January. He notified a justice and examined the surviving records. State and federal investigators became involved; the Arkansas State Police and FBI began a criminal investigation in April. The score irregularities were reported as spanning years, while the records problem became visible after a change in office leadership. Why did existing controls not reveal the discrepancies earlier?

That question does not establish that other officials knew about the alleged alterations. There is no public evidence here proving a conspiracy or showing that particular candidates were favored because of race, money, family ties or political connections. But it is fair to ask who could access original scores, whether one official could change results without a second approval, how independent source data were preserved, and whether audits compared official outcomes against the examiners' underlying scores. An institution's responsibility for secure records exists even when criminal responsibility belongs to an individual.

The justices say that the vast majority of candidates previously informed they passed really did pass. That reassurance matters, especially to lawyers and clients worried about the legitimacy of existing licenses. It must not become a reason to overlook the smaller group whose results may have been wrong. Even one person who passed and was wrongly rejected has a claim to more than a corrected spreadsheet.

What would meaningful correction look like? First, an independent and documented reconciliation of original scoring records with reported results, using recovered data and external records where necessary. Next, confidential notice to affected candidates explaining what was found and how to contest the determination. Where a person was wrongly denied a passing result, the court should explain the available route to recognition, licensing and any appropriate retroactive relief. Financial compensation is a separate legal question: a candidate would need to establish losses and an available legal basis for recovery. The public record does not yet show a compensation scheme or establish that all potential losses are legally recoverable.

There is a harder problem on the other side. Some candidates may have been told they passed based on scores allegedly raised above the threshold. They may have built practices and represented clients in good faith, unaware of any alteration. The court must safeguard the public without automatically treating those individuals as knowing participants in wrongdoing. A careful review should distinguish an applicant's actual score, the administrator's conduct, the applicant's knowledge, and the consequences of any proposed licensing decision. Punishing people who did not cause the problem would create another injustice.

The court says new examination procedures and security measures were implemented for the February 2026 examination, and Givens was referred for professional-conduct investigation. Those are identifiable responses, but the public still needs to know what changed, how the safeguards will be tested, and who will independently check the outcome. A promise of transparency is not the same as a published accounting.

There are also two different forms of accountability. The criminal case asks whether prosecutors can prove that Givens committed the charged offenses. The institutional inquiry asks whether the court's licensing system protected applicants, detected discrepancies, preserved evidence, and corrected injury when it failed. An acquittal would not automatically answer the institutional questions. A conviction would not automatically restore anyone's career.

Arkansas's Supreme Court oversees the system that allegedly produced the wrong results. That makes independent scrutiny especially important. The institution should not be the only voice explaining whether its own safeguards were adequate or whether affected candidates have been made whole. The court can cooperate with outside investigators and publish a sufficiently detailed corrective record while protecting private applicant information and the integrity of the criminal case.

The central question is no longer only who changed the scores. It is who lost opportunities because of those changes, who received an advantage they had not earned, and who is responsible for putting the records and lives back in order. Arkansas may eventually establish that the number of wrongly failed applicants was small. That would limit the scale of the harm, not erase the duty to find them and address it.

For anyone who sat for the Arkansas bar between July 2020 and July 2025, the practical starting point is the official court notice, not speculation about social-media lists. Preserve your original result notices, exam correspondence, application receipts and evidence of any employment consequences. Follow the Office of Professional Conduct and Programs' announcements for verified contact and review instructions. The court has said it will notify affected candidates when their status is confirmed. The public should follow whether that promise leads to documented corrections and meaningful remedies.

### Pull Up A Chair
The Arkansas bar scandal is about access to a profession and the credibility of the institution controlling that access. Candidates could not independently change the records. They were required to trust the people who maintained them. The institution now has to demonstrate how it will identify mistakes and repair their consequences, not merely how it will prosecute alleged misconduct.

### Accountability ledger
- **What happened:** The Arkansas Supreme Court disclosed evidence of bar-exam score manipulation over multiple administrations and roughly 11,400 missing or unavailable files; prosecutors charged Givens with 65 felony counts.
- **What duty failed, if allegations are substantiated:** Accurate and secure examination scoring, independent verification, and preservation of applicant records.
- **Who may have benefited:** Applicants whose reported scores were raised, including at least one top-score designation; the identities, knowledge, and ultimate outcomes remain unconfirmed.
- **Who may have paid:** Applicants whose scores were reduced, especially any wrongly failed candidate; possible career delay, extra fees, lost income and reputational harm require individual evidence.
- **Who had authority:** The Office of Professional Programs administered the exam under Supreme Court oversight. Prosecutors and law enforcement investigate alleged criminal conduct. The court controls licensing and corrective procedures.
- **What remains unresolved:** Motive, total affected people, recovery and reconciliation of records, independent control failures, licensing consequences and any restitution.

### Seat Summary
A licensing decision can change a person's life. Arkansas must establish not just whether the exam records were altered, but whose opportunities were changed and what corrections are available. Criminal proceedings and individual restoration are separate tasks.

### How we checked this · Disclosure and limitations
This report relies on the Arkansas Supreme Court's November 5 statements, the public criminal docket, a public disciplinary record, and Reuters/Bloomberg Law reporting. The court's descriptions are evidence and allegations, not a judicial finding of guilt. Givens pleaded not guilty. The criminal case is open; the public docket listed a January 4, 2027 review hearing when checked November 11, 2026. The approximately 11,400 figure describes files, not proven victims. The report does not establish motive, a complete list of affected examinees, particular monetary losses, or a legal entitlement to compensation. Illustrations of possible career harm are hypothetical, not attributed to identified people. No affected candidate or accused person's attorney was interviewed for this draft.
