## The Short Version

**What happened:** Black Americans carry the stress of ordinary life plus the work of anticipating, absorbing and answering racism. Researchers have measured that load in the body, and it does not disappear with income or education.

**Who it affects:** Black adults and families, including the ones whose success makes people assume the burden is gone.

**What you can do:** Stop asking Black people to prove their exhaustion. Ask employers, health systems and lawmakers what they will change, and protect the complaint deadlines that keep discrimination answerable.

## Website editorial

I am sick and tired of being sick and tired.

Fannie Lou Hamer said that in 1964. More than sixty years later I am still saying it, and so is nearly every Black person I know.

I'm from Texas. I'm African American. I didn't grow up rich, and I found my way anyway. But this is not an against-the-odds story, and my story is not really the point. The point is the stress itself, the kind that comes with being Black whether anyone believes it, sees it or thinks about it at all. People keep describing Black life as an ordinary life with a few extra inconveniences. It is not.

"We can't hang up our color or blend in. All these years of stress, oppression and racism have us a little crazy and exhausted. I know I am."

Most people get to put their burdens down at some point. They go home, close the door and stop performing. A Black person can close the door, but the day comes inside too. You replay the meeting. You ask yourself whether you should have said something, and you remember what it cost the last time you did. You get ready for tomorrow's version of the same thing. Then someone asks why you look tired, and explaining it becomes one more job.

And then comes the part that makes me angriest. People say it is all in the past. They say we all have the same chances now. They point to how far I have come, as if getting past the obstacle proves the obstacle was never there. How far I have come is not evidence that the road was fair. It is evidence of how much I had to carry to get here.

### The body keeps count

This is not only a feeling. Researchers have spent decades measuring it.

In 1992, public health researcher Arline Geronimus proposed what she called the weathering hypothesis. [18] Her idea was that repeated social and economic adversity, combined with political marginalization, wears the body down early. In 2006, she and her colleagues tested the idea on national health survey data for adults aged 18 to 64. They used allostatic load, a combined score of biological strain markers such as blood pressure. Black adults scored higher than white adults at every age, and especially between 35 and 64. Poverty did not explain the difference. Black women who were not poor had the second-highest probability of a high score of any group studied. [1]

That finding is the heart of this article. Money did not switch it off.

Other research points the same way. Public health researcher Shervin Assari calls the pattern "diminished returns": education and income buy Black Americans less health protection than they buy white Americans. In national data on older Americans, highly educated Black adults remained at high risk for poor health, a risk their education would not predict. [2] A 2015 review of 293 studies found that reported racism was consistently linked to poorer mental health, including depression, anxiety and psychological stress. [3]

The science is still being worked out, and honesty requires saying so. A December 2025 review of five studies on discrimination and allostatic load in Black adults over 40 found the results inconsistent, and its pooled estimate was small and not statistically significant. [16] That does not erase the pattern above. It means researchers are still learning how racism gets under the skin, and no single blood test can measure what a lifetime of it costs.

Sleep is where much of this shows up at night. A 2016 systematic review found that discrimination is associated with worse sleep. [4] A study following college students over time found that discrimination predicted sleep problems getting worse, and helped explain why Black students slept worse than white students. [5] Poor sleep is in turn linked to heart and metabolic disease. The day does not stay at work. It follows you to bed.

The most painful numbers come at birth. In 2024, Black women in the United States died from maternal causes at a rate of 44.8 per 100,000 live births. For white women, the rate was 14.2. [6] The CDC does not attribute that gap to stress, and neither does this article. Researchers do study it as one place where a lifetime of strain may meet a health system that too often does not listen.

### Being strong is not the same as being supported

Black people, and Black women especially, get praised for strength. A 2019 University of California, Berkeley study of 208 African American women looked at what researchers call the "superwoman schema": the felt duty to appear strong, suppress emotion and keep going. The results cut both ways. Under heavy discrimination, some parts of that stance seemed to protect the women's bodies. Other parts made the damage worse. The lead researcher, Amani Allen, drew the conclusion plainly: "The problem is not that women need to learn to cope better with racial discrimination." [7]

The same is true of working harder. Epidemiologist Sherman James named the pattern "John Henryism," after the folk hero who beat the steam drill and died with the hammer in his hand. In his 1983 study of Black men in North Carolina, men with less education who met adversity with relentless effort had higher blood pressure. [8] Later studies are mixed, and the effect varies by income and sex. But the warning holds. Telling people to try harder is not a health plan.

Black people do not need another lecture on resilience. We have been resilient for four hundred years. What we need is for the conditions to change.

### It is documented outside the body, too

The people who say we all have the same chances are not stating an opinion that deserves equal time. The record says otherwise.

**Jobs.** In 2004, economists sent identical résumés to employers in Boston and Chicago. Applications with names like Emily and Greg got 50 percent more callbacks than the same applications with names like Lakisha and Jamal. In 2021, a larger team repeated the experiment with more than 83,000 applications to 11,000 entry-level jobs at large U.S. companies. The typical employer still contacted the presumed white applicants about 9 percent more often. [9]

**Wealth.** In 2022, the typical white family had about $285,000 in wealth. The typical Black family had about $44,900. The Federal Reserve calls that roughly six to one. [10] When something goes wrong, the difference is whether there is anything to land on.

### Not only in America

This is not only an American story. Black people carry versions of it in many countries. I live in Germany now, and it is not an escape. Germany's National Discrimination and Racism Monitor (NaDiRa) found that people who experience discrimination at least once a month are significantly more likely to report symptoms of depression and anxiety. [11] Black people surveyed reported especially frequent discrimination, most often because of their skin color. The 2023 health report found that about 14 percent of Black women surveyed had delayed or avoided medical treatment in the previous 12 months for fear of being taken less seriously or treated worse than others. [12] That is the same pattern: the burden follows people into the doctor's office, and some of them stop going.

### What would actually reduce the burden

The honest answer starts with a gap in the research. A 2022 systematic review of 29 studies found clear benefits from policies that raise income and opportunity. It also found that the remedies have been studied far less than the damage. [13] The burden is well documented. What institutions do about it is not.

So the questions belong to the institutions:

- **Employers:** Who investigates a discrimination complaint, by when, and in writing? What protects the person who raised it from retaliation? Does the manager named in the complaint still control that person's schedule? Praising someone's strength is not a substitute for staffing, flexibility or leave.
- **Health systems:** Do clinicians take reported pain and stress seriously the first time? Is care affordable? Do they measure who delays care, and why?
- **Lawmakers:** Do complaint deadlines give people time to act? In Germany, claims under the General Equal Treatment Act (AGG) must currently be asserted within two months, and a federal bill would extend that to four. [14] In the United States, most private-sector employment discrimination charges must be filed with the EEOC within 180 days, or 300 days where a state or local agency enforces a similar law. [15]

None of this asks Black people to endure better. It asks everyone else to stop making endurance the price of being Black.

We are tired. We are allowed to be tired. The point is to change what is making us this way.

## Pull Up A Chair

- **If it is happening to you at work,** write down each incident with the date, who was there and what was said. Ask for decisions in writing. Check your filing deadline early. An internal complaint does not stop the EEOC or AGG clock.
- **If you are someone's colleague or friend,** believe them the first time. Ask what you can take off their plate, then do it, instead of praising how strong they are.
- **If you run a team or an institution,** publish how complaints are handled and how long it takes, and track what happened to the person afterwards.
- **If you are carrying this right now,** you deserve care that takes your life into account. In the United States, call or text 988. In Germany, TelefonSeelsorge is free at 0800 111 0 111 or 0800 111 0 222.

## Seat Summary

Black Americans carry a stress load that home, money and success do not switch off, and researchers have measured it in blood pressure, sleep and early wear on the body. The seat at risk is the right to be tired without having to prove it. Institutions hold the remedies, and they have studied them least.
