## The Short Version

**What happened:** Racism against Black Americans keeps showing up in public life. Meanwhile, the country runs every day on things Black Americans invented, improved, built or created, from traffic signals and home security systems to blood banks, video game cartridges and nearly every kind of popular music.

**Who it affects:** Black Americans, who are told they don't belong in a country that relies on their work, and everyone who never learned where these things came from.

**What you can do:** Learn the names below and share them. Teach them to a child. And the next time someone says Black people contributed nothing, show them the receipts.

## Website editorial

Some people make it clear they do not want Black Americans here. They don't want to hear about us, live near us or share power with us.

They still use what we made every day.

They stop at the light. They lock the door and check the camera. They give blood and receive it. They play video games, ship cold food across the country, and turn up the radio. Much of that runs through the work of Black Americans, often people who were never paid what the work was worth, or never got the credit at all.

Here are the receipts.

### On the road and at home

**The three-position traffic signal.** Garrett Morgan, the son of a formerly enslaved parent, patented an improved traffic signal in 1923 after seeing a crash in Cleveland. Signals then only said stop and go. His added a third, all-stop position that cleared the intersection, the idea behind the caution light. [1] He also patented a breathing device, a safety hood that protected rescuers from smoke and gas. [1]

**The home security system.** In 1966, Marie Van Brittan Brown, a nurse in Queens, New York, designed a system with a camera, a monitor and a remote door lock, because police took too long to come to her neighborhood. She and her husband received the patent in 1969. Today's home security systems build on ideas from her design. [2]

**Refrigerated trucks.** Frederick McKinley Jones received more than 60 patents, including one for the roof-mounted cooling unit that keeps trucks cold on long hauls. [3] Think of that the next time you buy fresh food or a vaccine that traveled across the country.

**The light bulb that lasted.** Thomas Edison gets the credit for the light bulb, but many inventors worked on it. Lewis Latimer patented a method of producing carbon filaments in 1881 that made bulbs longer-lasting, more efficient and cheaper. [4]

### In medicine

**The blood bank.** Dr. Charles Drew showed that plasma could be safely separated from whole blood, stored and used for transfusion. [5] In World War II, he led the Blood for Britain program, which collected more than 14,000 donations and shipped about 5,000 liters of plasma overseas. [6] He then became the first director of the American Red Cross Blood Bank. [7] Modern blood drives grew out of that work.

**Cataract surgery.** Dr. Patricia Bath invented the Laserphaco Probe for treating cataracts and became the first African American woman doctor to receive a medical patent. [3]

**The cells that built modern medicine.** In 1951, doctors at Johns Hopkins took cells from Henrietta Lacks, a Black woman being treated for cervical cancer, without her knowledge. Her cells became the first human cells to keep growing in the lab, and they helped develop the polio vaccine, map human genes and develop COVID-19 vaccines. [8] Her family was never paid. In 2023, they reached a settlement with one of the companies that profited. [8] Nobody asked her. They just took.

### In technology

**The game cartridge.** Before 1976, home video games were built into the console. Jerry Lawson led the team at Fairchild that created the first home system with interchangeable cartridges, the Channel F. [9] Every game you ever bought separately follows that model.

**The personal computer.** At IBM, Mark Dean led the team that designed the ISA bus, the connection that let printers, keyboards and other devices plug into a computer. He holds three of the original nine patents on the IBM personal computer and is in the National Inventors Hall of Fame. [10]

**The Super Soaker.** Lonnie Johnson, an engineer who worked on NASA missions to Mars and Saturn, invented the Super Soaker. [11]

### In the music you play every day

The blues, jazz, gospel, rhythm and blues, rock and roll and hip-hop all grew out of Black American life. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture tells that story from the music enslaved Africans brought with them to today's hip-hop, which started at parties in the Bronx in the early 1970s. [12] Turn on almost any radio station in the world and you are hearing it.

### And the patents they never got

Some of this work was never credited because the law would not allow it. In 1857, a Mississippi enslaved man named Ned invented a new cotton scraper. His enslaver tried to patent it. In 1858, the U.S. Attorney General ruled that neither could hold the patent. Ned was enslaved, so he could not take the inventor's oath, and his enslaver had not invented it. [13] The opinion relied on Dred Scott, the Supreme Court ruling that Black Americans were not citizens. [14] It took the Reconstruction Amendments to end that rule. [15]

We will never know how many inventions were taken that way, with no name attached.

One more correction, because the truth is strong enough without myths: George Washington Carver did not invent peanut butter. He developed more than 300 uses for peanuts, in products from paint to baby products. [16] Every claim in this list is backed by a source below.

### Pay us back

So here is the deal. If you don't want us here, if you don't like what we stand for, give it all back. Give back the signal at the corner, the camera on the porch, the blood in the bank, the cold truck, the game cartridge, the plug on the back of your computer and the music on your radio. Then live with only what you have.

Nobody is actually going to do that. Nobody can. These things belong to the whole country now, because Black Americans built a share of it. Then treat Black Americans like people who belong in it.

## Seat Summary

The country uses Black American work every day: on the road, in the hospital, at home, on screens and on the radio. Much of it was underpaid, uncredited or, under the law before the Civil War, impossible to patent. The missing seat is simple: credit, and belonging, for the people who built it.
