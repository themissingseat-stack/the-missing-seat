## The Short Version

**What happened?** Black women's work and leadership predate slavery, helped sustain and resist the American slave system, shaped schools, labor protections and civil-rights movements after emancipation, and helped produce historic representation in the November 3, 2026 midterms. Yet institutions have repeatedly relied on their work while limiting their legal protection, earnings or access to authority.

**Who it affects:** Black women across generations; their families; the students and workers who deserve a fuller history; and the electorate and communities affected by the actions of newly elected officials.

**What you can do:** Start with the 1662 Virginia statute, the 1881 Atlanta washerwomen's strike and the 1935 Social Security coverage rules. Then use the 2026 election records and Congress.gov to track specific decisions, not merely the victory speeches.

## Website editorial

The first thing that caught my attention about the November 2026 midterms was the familiar praise. Black women showed up. Black women organized. Black women helped deliver major election victories. In Georgia, Black women did something else as well: they won offices with real authority.

That deserves celebrating. Keisha Lance Bottoms was elected governor of Georgia, becoming the first Black woman elected governor of any U.S. state. Juliana Stratton won a Senate seat in Illinois. Melat Kiros won a House seat in Colorado. When the new Congress convenes in January, three Black women are set to serve in the Senate simultaneously for the first time. [8, 9]

But I keep coming back to a different question. Why does the country seem so comfortable applauding Black women for saving everybody else, yet so often slow down when the conversation turns to protecting their labor, sharing authority or delivering what they were promised?

Before we talk about what Black women did in this election, we ought to talk about what has always been there.

### There was history before the plantation

Black women's history did not start in the United States, and it did not begin in bondage. In the ancient kingdom of Kush, in what is now Sudan, some women ruled in their own right. The kandake Amanirenas led resistance against Roman troops in the late first century BCE. Getty's account of Nubian art describes royal depictions that make female authority visible, including Queen Amanitore alongside King Natakamani. [1]

No, this does not mean every African woman was a queen, or that African societies were all equal or free from slavery. They were not. It means a popular history that gives Black women their first significant appearance only when Europeans enslaved them has already left out centuries of civilization, work and power.

The rulers have names because monuments and written sources preserved them. The daily work of ordinary women in farming, markets, households and communities was less likely to be recorded. Missing names do not mean missing contributions.

### America made a business out of their work and their children

During American slavery, Black women did not freely choose to build the wealth of plantation owners. They were compelled to work and exposed to sale, separation, punishment and sexual exploitation. Saying they "helped build America" without that distinction risks making coercion sound like volunteer service.

The law made the exploitation even more explicit. In December 1662, Virginia's General Assembly ruled that a child's enslaved or free status depended on the condition of the mother. The statute changed the ordinary English common-law presumption that a child's status followed the father. [2]

Consider the result. A slaveholder could benefit from an enslaved woman's forced labor and, under this law, claim a legal right over her children. This is not a metaphor about systemic racism. It is a preserved law, passed by identified lawmakers, making women's reproductive lives part of the machinery of inherited enslavement.

And still, women resisted. Some sued for freedom, others assisted escapees, passed down knowledge, preserved relationships or did whatever they could under conditions where even small acts could be punished. Harriet Tubman escaped and repeatedly returned to help others reach safety. During the Civil War she served as a scout, spy and nurse; a Union operation she helped guide at the Combahee River in June 1863 liberated more than 700 enslaved people. [3]

Tubman's story is extraordinary. It is also too easy to tell it as though freedom was delivered by a lone hero. The operation depended on Black soldiers, boat pilots, local knowledge and people willing to take enormous risks. That is what collective courage looks like.

### Freedom arrived. Equal protection did not.

The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery except as punishment for crime. It did not automatically supply land, money, safety, fair wages or equal political influence to the people whose forced work had enriched others.

Black women taught children, opened and maintained institutions, supported churches, worked in households and businesses, and organized for rights. Their labor was not invisible to the families who relied on it. Their authority and bargaining power were another matter.

In Atlanta in 1881, Black washerwomen organized a strike over their working conditions and pay. They understood their economic importance. Wealthy households could not simply wish their laundry clean; the workers had power precisely because the city depended on their work. The Smithsonian and Atlanta labor-history accounts record the women's organized challenge to that arrangement. [4]

That example matters because it corrects the sentimental version of the story in which Black women only cared for others. These women negotiated, organized and demanded fairer terms for themselves.

The same contradiction appeared decades later in federal policy. The original Social Security Act of 1935 left agricultural and domestic employment outside its main old-age insurance coverage. Black women were heavily represented in those occupations. A Social Security Administration historical analysis describes the unequal effects while also examining a serious dispute among historians about the exact motives and administrative reasoning behind the original exclusions. [5]

We should not flatten that disagreement. It is established that the occupational exclusions were written into the program and disproportionately affected Black workers and women. It is more contested whether the exclusion was primarily designed to produce a racial result, or arose from a mix of administrative, political and other choices. The documented effect is important even when the reason is disputed. Most excluded occupations were brought within coverage in later reforms, but that did not automatically replace years of foregone coverage for affected workers.

The people using domestic labor benefited from the services. The law left many of those workers outside a new national protection. Black women were being called essential long before anyone thought to use that word as a compliment.

### They helped make democracy work before democracy made room for them

During the struggle for civil rights, women did more than stand behind famous male leaders in photographs. Ella Baker emphasized grassroots organization. Septima Clark's citizenship-education work helped people navigate voting barriers. Fannie Lou Hamer exposed intimidation and violent disenfranchisement in Mississippi and challenged exclusion from party politics in 1964. [6]

Organizing was skilled political labor. It involved teaching, listening, building local networks, fundraising, transportation and the willingness to suffer consequences for public demands. Those are not small supporting tasks. Without them, much of what gets called a movement cannot function.

And the formal recognition came painfully late. Shirley Chisholm won election to the U.S. House in November 1968 and took office in January 1969, becoming the first Black woman in Congress. Think about the distance from emancipation in 1865 to her arrival in that chamber. Black women did not spend those years waiting to develop leadership. Their representation lagged behind leadership they had already demonstrated. [7]

### What the 2026 numbers actually tell us

The November 3 election added another chapter. ABC's National Voter Poll estimated that 93% of Black women who cast votes in U.S. House races supported Democratic candidates, compared with approximately 6% supporting Republicans. The same table estimates Black women made up about 5% of voters nationally. These are survey estimates of vote choice among participating voters, not a claim that 93% of all eligible Black women voted and not proof that they alone decided the national result. [10]

Other voters, candidates, issues, organizers and local conditions also helped produce the outcomes. Georgia offers a useful example: AP and local reporting describe changes in Black men's voting patterns as well as strong support among Black women. It would be poor analysis to turn an important constituency into a magical explanation for every race. [11]

But here is what is genuinely new: Black women also gained offices that control or influence real decisions. Bottoms as governor; Stratton as U.S. senator; Kiros as a member of Congress; additional Black women elected to statewide executive roles in Georgia, including its attorney general. [8, 9, 12]

The election is a milestone in representation. The work done after the election will tell us whether it becomes a milestone in policy as well. The newly elected officials will have to operate within the actual powers and limits of their offices, face opposition and public scrutiny, and account for promises with legislation, budgets, appointments and decisions.

### Calling women "saviors" can become another burden

I love giving people their flowers. And Black women have earned an enormous amount of recognition. But there is an uncomfortable side to the word "savior."

When we call Black women the people who always rescue the family, the workplace, the community or democracy itself, do we also quietly expect them to keep doing it? To be stronger? To forgive more? To volunteer more? To wait longer for a promotion, medical care, fair pay or political authority?

Nobody should have to be extraordinary to qualify for ordinary dignity. Black women are people with individual beliefs and disagreements, not a single political team or a bottomless supply of unpaid sacrifice. They deserve happiness, rest, safety, opportunity and influence whether or not they have just saved an institution.

And winning public office does not excuse anyone from accountability. Every official, including those whose elections broke historical barriers, must be judged by the same record of what they promised and what they actually did.

### Who gained? Who carried the costs? Who made it possible?

In the colonial period, Virginia lawmakers helped entrench an inherited status system that let enslavers extract labor across generations. The people enslaved and their descendants carried the direct damage. We cannot calculate from this single law every unreturned loss across centuries, but neither can we pretend the legal mechanism is unknowable.

In post-emancipation domestic labor, employers received services from workers with limited choices and often limited protections. In 1935, federal lawmakers and program designers adopted exclusions with disproportionately harmful consequences for women and Black workers, even though the specific intent remains a subject of historical debate. Later expansions changed coverage but did not by themselves erase all earlier economic costs.

In politics, organizations and candidates benefit from volunteer time, donations, organizing, and votes. That participation is not inherently exploitation. The legitimate public question is whether those institutions then offer meaningful access, listen to communities, protect voting rights and deliver measurable policy outcomes.

The benefits and burdens are not interchangeable across centuries. One is slavery, one is labor policy, another is democratic participation. What links them is worth examining: a society can rely on Black women's work without granting equal authority over the decisions that affect them.

### Pull Up a Chair

Start with the original documents instead of the polished slogans. Look at Virginia's 1662 statute. Read the record of the Atlanta washerwomen's strike. Follow the analysis of Social Security's early employment exclusions and ask what those choices did in practice.

Then treat 2026 like the beginning of an accountability file rather than the end of a celebration. On Congress.gov, follow the legislation and votes of the newly elected members after they take office. In Georgia, follow the governor's proposals and appointments, executive-office decisions and the actual state budget. Save campaign commitments with dates; compare them with actions taken and constraints encountered.

And when a museum, school, employer or political party thanks Black women for all they do, ask a simple practical question: Who received the credit, resources, protection and power?

Black women have been leading, teaching, building, caring, resisting and innovating for centuries. The 2026 elections deserve to be celebrated as real progress. But nobody should have to spend a lifetime saving everybody else before the world recognizes the value of her own life.

## Facebook copy: English (clean literal text)

Black women have been called the people who save the day so often that we ought to ask what happens to them after everyone else has been saved.

Look at the history. Before the transatlantic slave trade, women in ancient Nubia ruled kingdoms. During American slavery, Black women's labor was taken by force, and Virginia even passed a law in 1662 making children inherit their mother's enslaved status. Harriet Tubman later helped lead a Civil War operation that freed more than 700 people.

After emancipation, Black women built institutions and organized for better treatment. In 1881, washerwomen in Atlanta went on strike. In the civil rights movement, women like Fannie Lou Hamer challenged voter suppression. Shirley Chisholm entered Congress in 1969.

And now we have the November 2026 elections. Keisha Lance Bottoms is set to become the first Black woman governor of a U.S. state. Juliana Stratton won a Senate seat. Melat Kiros won election to Congress from Colorado. A national voter survey estimated that 93% of Black women who voted in House races supported Democratic candidates.

I am delighted to see these achievements recognized. But I also wonder why we keep expecting Black women to organize, sacrifice, rescue and carry everybody else without asking what they need and what authority they are given.

They deserve their flowers, no question. They also deserve the chance to live fully without having to earn basic dignity through extraordinary service.

The history is remarkable. The next chapter should be about what changes when more of that talent has real decision-making power.

Before slavery, women in Nubia ruled kingdoms. During American slavery, Black women resisted even as lawmakers turned their forced work and their children's status into tools of an economy. After emancipation they built schools, challenged unfair labor conditions and organized for civil rights.

Read the record. Give credit where it belongs. And watch what elected officials actually do next.
