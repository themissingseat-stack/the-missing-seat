# Regular Joe Final Website Editorial

Breast Cancer Survival Has Improved. Black Women Are Still Dying More Often.

October turns pink for a reason. Breast cancer is common, frightening and deeply personal. Almost everybody knows somebody who has had it, feared it, been screened for it, treated it or lost somebody to it.

Awareness has helped. Screening has helped. Treatment has improved. Millions of people in the United States are living after a breast cancer diagnosis.

But awareness is not the same thing as survival.

The latest federal cancer data show that breast cancer is the second most common cancer among women in the United States and the second leading cause of cancer death among women overall. For non-Hispanic Black women, however, breast cancer is the leading cause of cancer death.

That is where this story has to become more specific.

White women are slightly more likely than Black women to develop breast cancer overall. Yet Black women have the highest breast cancer death rate. Current national sources continue to put the mortality gap at roughly 40 percent: Black women are about 40 percent more likely than White women to die from breast cancer.

If the diagnosis rate is similar or somewhat lower but the death rate is higher, the question cannot stop at, "Did she know she was supposed to get screened?"

We have to follow the whole receipt.

## Screening matters. But what happens next matters too.

For women at average risk, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force currently recommends a mammogram every two years from ages 40 through 74. Other medical organizations use somewhat different schedules, and women at higher risk may need an individualized plan.

That is important. Early detection can save lives.

But here is a fact that makes the racial disparity harder to explain away: the USPSTF notes that Black women report mammography screening at rates similar to or higher than White women, yet Black women are still more likely to be diagnosed beyond stage I.

So telling Black women simply to "get your mammogram" cannot be the whole answer.

What happens after an abnormal image?

How long until the diagnostic mammogram or ultrasound?

How long until the biopsy?

How long until the pathology report?

Does the patient understand the result?

Does somebody explain what kind of breast cancer she has?

How quickly does she reach a surgeon or oncologist?

Can she get the recommended treatment without losing her job, her insurance, her transportation, her childcare or weeks of time navigating appointments?

Those are not side issues. That is the road between screening and survival.

## Black women are more likely to face an aggressive kind of breast cancer.

One part of the disparity is biological.

Black women are substantially more likely than White women to develop triple-negative breast cancer. Triple-negative means the cancer cells do not have estrogen receptors, progesterone receptors or excess HER2 protein. Those are three of the targets doctors can use against many other breast cancers.

Without those targets, some common hormone and HER2-directed treatments do not work. Triple-negative breast cancer also tends to grow and spread faster than many other invasive breast cancers.

Treatment options have improved, including chemotherapy, immunotherapy and newer targeted approaches. Triple-negative is not a hopeless diagnosis. But it remains a more difficult form of the disease.

That matters.

It does not explain everything.

The National Cancer Institute has explicitly noted that the higher frequency of aggressive subtypes among Black women does not fully explain the survival gap. The USPSTF has also called for more research into both tumor biology and differences in diagnosis and treatment.

That distinction is important because this story should not become another version of "Black bodies are just different."

Race is not a diagnosis.

Black women are not one biological type.

The outcome gap can reflect several things at once: tumor subtype, age at diagnosis, other health conditions, stage at diagnosis, access to specialists, treatment differences, delays, insurance, geography, income and the quality of care a patient receives.

The honest answer is not one cause.

The honest answer is that the gap remains.

## Do not blame Black women for a system-wide outcome.

There is a lazy version of health awareness that eventually turns every problem into a lecture for the patient.

Eat better. Exercise. Get screened. Make your appointments. Ask questions.

Those things can matter. People should know their family history, know what is normal for their own breasts and seek medical attention when something changes.

But personal responsibility cannot explain away a population-level survival gap.

If Black women are using mammography at similar or higher rates and still dying more often, the health system has to examine everything that happens after the screening appointment too.

That means measuring the time from an abnormal result to diagnostic imaging.

From diagnostic imaging to biopsy.

From biopsy to pathology.

From pathology to oncology.

From diagnosis to surgery, chemotherapy, radiation or other recommended treatment.

It means asking whether those timelines differ by race, insurance, neighborhood or hospital.

And if they do, it means finding out why.

## A diagnosis is not just "breast cancer."

A woman who hears the words "you have breast cancer" needs more information than that.

What stage is it?

What grade?

Has it spread?

Is it estrogen-receptor positive?

Progesterone-receptor positive?

HER2-positive?

Triple-negative?

Should genetic testing be considered?

What are the treatment options?

What happens first?

Is there a clinical trial that fits this diagnosis?

These are not questions patients are expected to answer alone. They are questions a cancer-care team should explain in language a frightened human being can actually understand.

The Black Health Matters breast-cancer resource page puts substantial emphasis on screening, early detection, breast-cancer types and information specifically for Black women. That work matters because a person cannot advocate for care she does not know exists.

But information has to meet access.

A perfect website cannot perform a biopsy.

A pink ribbon cannot schedule an oncology appointment.

Awareness cannot replace treatment.

## Black men are not completely outside this story.

Breast cancer in men is rare. The American Cancer Society estimates that fewer than one percent of U.S. breast cancers occur in men.

But rare does not mean impossible.

ACS research found that breast cancer incidence was 52 percent higher among Black men than White men in the study population, across the major tumor subtypes examined. Researchers said the reasons for the difference were not yet known.

That does not make male breast cancer a central Black-health crisis on the scale of breast cancer among women. It does mean that a man who notices a breast lump, nipple change or other persistent abnormality should not dismiss it because he thinks breast cancer is exclusively a women's disease.

## The strongest counterargument deserves an answer.

Not every racial difference in cancer outcome is evidence that a doctor discriminated against a patient.

Breast cancer is not one disease. Tumor biology differs. Triple-negative cancer is more common among Black women. Age, other illnesses, family history, genetics, stage and many other clinical factors affect prognosis.

That is true.

It is also why the right question is not, "Can we blame every death on racism?"

The right question is, "Which parts of this survival gap can we prevent?"

A health system cannot change a woman's race. It cannot guarantee that every cancer will be easy to treat.

It can make sure an abnormal mammogram is followed quickly.

It can make sure a biopsy does not disappear into scheduling limbo.

It can make sure the patient receives the right biomarker testing.

It can measure whether guideline-recommended treatment is delivered.

It can remove avoidable transportation, insurance, language, scheduling and referral barriers.

It can recruit Black women into research and clinical trials in meaningful numbers.

And it can publish the outcomes so the public can see whether the gap is actually narrowing.

That is accountability.

## What should an ordinary person do?

If you are due for breast-cancer screening, make the appointment. If you are at higher risk because of family history, a known genetic variant, previous chest radiation, a prior high-risk breast lesion or another reason, ask whether the standard screening schedule is enough for you.

Know changes that deserve medical attention. A new breast or underarm lump, swelling or thickening, skin dimpling or irritation, nipple changes, unusual discharge, changes in breast size or shape, or persistent breast pain should be evaluated. Many breast changes are not cancer. That is exactly why they should be checked rather than guessed about.

If an imaging result is abnormal, do not leave the appointment with only "someone will call you."

Ask what the next step is.

Ask when it should happen.

Ask whom to contact if you have not heard back.

Keep a copy of the report.

If cancer is diagnosed, ask the team to explain the stage and receptor or biomarker results in plain language.

And if somebody you love is going through this, practical support counts. Drive her to the appointment. Watch the children. Bring food. Sit in the room and take notes. Help her make the next call.

"Be strong" is not transportation.

"Stay positive" is not childcare.

Support should solve something.

## What should the system do?

Hospitals, insurers and public-health agencies should publish a breast-cancer follow-up receipt.

How long does it take, on average, to move from an abnormal screening result to diagnostic imaging?

From imaging to biopsy?

From biopsy to a completed pathology report?

From confirmed diagnosis to the start of treatment?

Where privacy rules allow, are those timelines and treatment completion rates different by race, insurance status or geography?

If the system cannot answer those questions, it cannot confidently say the survival gap is only about biology.

Breast cancer awareness has done important work.

Now measure what happens after awareness.

Measure who is diagnosed early.

Measure who waits.

Measure who gets the recommended treatment.

Measure who survives.

And when Black women are still dying more often, do not just hand them another pink ribbon.

Show the receipt.

## Who Is Missing?

Black women whose breast-cancer risk is discussed only during awareness month but whose treatment delays and survival outcomes disappear from the conversation the rest of the year.

Patients who completed screening but still face delays between an abnormal result and diagnosis or treatment.

Younger Black women and women with aggressive tumor subtypes whose risk may not fit the public's picture of a "typical" breast-cancer patient.

Black men who may dismiss breast symptoms because breast cancer is treated socially as a women's disease.

Families trying to keep jobs, children, transportation and bills together while helping somebody through cancer care.

## Recipes

**For readers**
- Check whether you are due for screening and ask a clinician whether your personal risk changes the usual schedule.
- Seek evaluation for a new or persistent breast change rather than trying to diagnose it yourself.
- After an abnormal screening result, get the next step, expected date and contact person before leaving the process.
- If diagnosed, ask for the stage, receptor/biomarker results and treatment plan in plain language.
- Ask whether genetic counseling/testing or a clinical trial is appropriate for your case.

**For families and allies**
- Offer concrete help: transportation, childcare, meals, appointment notes or help with calls.
- Do not make the patient manage everybody else's emotions while managing cancer.

**For hospitals and health systems**
- Track and publish time from abnormal screening to diagnosis and from diagnosis to treatment.
- Audit whether those intervals and completion rates differ by race, insurance status or geography.
- Provide patient navigation and practical support when transportation, scheduling or caregiving threaten continuity of care.
- Make clinical-trial recruitment representative and explain trial options consistently.

## Verification Notice

This editorial does **not** claim that every Black-White breast-cancer survival difference is caused by racism, discrimination or unequal treatment. Current authoritative sources describe a multi-factor problem involving tumor biology, stage at diagnosis, treatment, access, socioeconomic conditions and other health factors.

The phrase "about 40% more likely to die" reflects current national summaries from the American Cancer Society and USPSTF. NCI's current prevention summary describes mortality in Black women as about 38% higher than in White women using the cited period. The article therefore uses "about 40%" rather than presenting a false level of precision.

The USPSTF recommendation cited here applies to people at average risk and does not replace individualized medical advice for people at high risk, with symptoms, prior breast cancer, high-risk lesions, certain genetic variants or prior high-dose chest radiation.

Black Health Matters was used as the user-supplied starting resource. Current screening guidance and national disparity statistics were independently checked against CDC, ACS, USPSTF and NCI sources. Older or organization-specific screening claims from secondary material were not substituted for current national guidance.

## Strongest Counterargument / Fairness Check

A racial mortality gap alone does not establish discriminatory medical treatment. Black women are more likely to develop aggressive triple-negative tumors, may be diagnosed younger and can differ from White women in other clinical and socioeconomic factors that influence outcomes. The editorial therefore does not attribute the entire mortality gap to provider bias or health-system failure.

The accountability claim is narrower: because tumor biology does not fully explain the disparity, and because Black women report similar or higher mammography use while still experiencing later-stage diagnosis and higher mortality, health systems should measure and reduce preventable differences in follow-up, diagnosis, access and treatment rather than treating screening participation as the end of the inquiry.
