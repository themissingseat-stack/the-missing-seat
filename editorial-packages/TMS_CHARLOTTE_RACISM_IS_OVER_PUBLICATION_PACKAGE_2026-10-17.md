# Website editorial

## Racism Is Over. So Why Are Black Drivers Still a Competition?

For years, Black Americans have been told that racism is behind us. People don't see color anymore. Stop bringing up slavery. Stop making everything about race and, perhaps most insulting of all, stop playing the victim. Yet every so often something happens that makes you wonder whether the people repeating those claims have been paying attention to the same country the rest of us live in.

This time, it happened in Charlotte, North Carolina, where an investigation uncovered a disturbing private group chat involving six Charlotte-Mecklenburg police officers. According to investigative reporting, officers allegedly used racial slurs, discussed firearms and Black residents, and participated in a competition to see who could pull over the most Black drivers.

A competition. Not over solving violent crimes or helping residents, but over how many Black people they could stop.

On October 15, 2026, Police Chief Estella Patterson announced that three officers had resigned and three others faced termination proceedings. She confirmed racial, sexual, and religious bias in the messages and said the department had reviewed traffic stops, arrests, body-camera footage, and complaints. She sought federal assistance to evaluate possible civil rights violations.

Those distinctions matter. The existence of discriminatory communications and employment action is established. Specific descriptions of the alleged contest and violent remarks come from investigative reporting. As of the chief's announcement, the department said its initial review had not found evidence that the officers violated particular residents' civil rights. That is not proof that no one was harmed. It means the examination must continue.

A police officer's private prejudice does not stay private when that officer has the legal power to stop a car, search a person, make an arrest, or testify in court. The difference between an ugly personal opinion and discriminatory law enforcement is the authority the government gives that person over someone else's life.

## The history we're told to forget

North Carolina has a documented history of slave patrols. They enforced laws controlling enslaved people's movement, searching for people who were not permitted to travel freely, and punishing resistance. They were not simply private groups behaving badly; their powers were supported by law.

Modern American policing developed through several traditions, not a single direct institutional line from slave patrols. But the history remains relevant to the specific accusation in Charlotte. If officers compete to stop Black drivers because they are Black, the abuse echoes a long history in which government authority was used to regulate Black movement.

Recognizing that resemblance is not accusing every police officer of being a slave patroller. It is recognizing why the allegations strike so deeply and why the community has good reason to demand more than reassuring statements.

We cannot learn from a history we are constantly instructed to forget.

## Numbers must be investigated, not weaponized

Earlier reporting cited approximately 56 percent of stops and 83 percent of searches through May 31, 2026. Those reported percentages have not been independently reproduced here. An October 26 query of the city's current GIS dataset for January through May produced different results: 25,827 of 45,396 stop records had Driver_Race recorded as Black, and 2,501 of 2,969 records with a search recorded YES did so. That is about 56.9 percent and 84.2 percent respectively. Separating non-Hispanic Black records changes the numerators again; it still does not reproduce both reported percentages. These are current records, not a preserved May snapshot, and city/state datasets and category definitions may differ. The discrepancy must be reconciled before release. Those numbers alone do not prove discrimination. Investigators must consider who was driving in enforcement areas, reasons officers recorded for stops, search justification, search outcomes, and treatment of comparable drivers of other races.

But in a department where officers are accused of making Black drivers a contest, those disparities plainly demand examination. Their meaning cannot be decided by a headline, nor should they be dismissed without a serious audit.

The problem is also an imbalance of information. Residents seldom have access to all the footage, communications, dispatch records, or comparative stop data needed to establish what happened. The institution being questioned often controls the records necessary to answer the questions. That makes independent review, preservation of evidence, and disclosure particularly important.

## Canada shows why private police culture matters

This is not only an American concern. British Columbia's Police Complaint Commissioner arranged a public hearing concerning six current and former Nelson Police Department officers and alleged discriminatory WhatsApp content. A September 17, 2026 notice identified adjudicator Brent Hoy and scheduled hearing dates. Its October 8 update addressed a procedural adjournment; the notice said the hearings would examine investigative conduct and authority to obtain and rely on the messages. The current hearing index lists PH25-01 under current proceedings. That is not a final misconduct finding, evidence of a Charlotte-style contest or proof that a particular Canadian suffered an unlawful stop.

The parallel is more fundamental. What people in authority say when they believe the public cannot hear them can matter to how their institutions function. A workplace that tolerates degrading talk about the people it serves has to investigate whether those attitudes influenced conduct, supervision, decisions, or the treatment of complaints.

## The public pays. But who gets justice?

Taxpayers funded these officers' salaries, training, vehicles, and supervision. Public money now also supports investigations and disciplinary proceedings. These are legitimate public expenditures when misconduct is suspected, but they do not by themselves make a harmed resident whole.

Imagine someone who was improperly stopped on the way to work. They lose time, a shift, or money. Perhaps a search occurred, a citation was issued, or a case ended up in court. Perhaps an arrest record remains long after a charge was dismissed. Those are possible examples of injury, not verified findings about any specific Charlotte resident. Yet they illustrate why firing an officer and fixing the public's injury are two different tasks.

The institution may announce resignations, hearings, additional training, and policy changes. Each can matter. But the resident's financial loss, record, or sense of safety does not disappear when the employee leaves.

On October 15, WSOC reporter Evan Donovan reported that District Attorney Spencer Merriweather said his office needed to examine pending and past criminal cases involving the officers. The report said the office was still determining the scope and did not plan to publish the number affected. On October 22, Merriweather told Spectrum News that cases required individual review. Those statements establish a review commitment, not a verified count of completed reviews, overturned convictions or delivered relief.

Where rights violations are established, the government should provide clear and accessible pathways to appropriate remedies. Depending on facts and law, that may involve correction of a record, review of a conviction, reimbursement, civil relief, or other forms of restoration. None of these outcomes should be assumed in advance, and not every offensive text proves a traffic stop was unlawful. But neither should the absence of an earlier complaint automatically clear an encounter.

## Who benefited and who paid?

If officers could exchange biased messages for years without effective intervention, some may have benefited from a workplace environment that failed to challenge them promptly. Whether supervisors tolerated the conduct or whether discriminatory attitudes affected arrests and stops must be examined on evidence. The public has paid for the service and the investigation; any residents who were wrongly treated may have paid in freedom, wages, dignity, money, or lost opportunities.

Fair-minded officers also bear the damage to public confidence. But repairing the department's reputation is not the same thing as repairing injury to people.

This is why police accountability must not become a calculation of how many employees departed. It must be an account of decisions, the people affected, and whether actual relief followed.

## What Charlotte must do next

CMPD should preserve all relevant messages and enforcement records, provide meaningful access to independent reviewers, and explain how the traffic-stop and search audit was conducted. The district attorney should examine whether the information must be disclosed in criminal proceedings and whether it affects pending or concluded cases. Appropriate federal authorities should determine whether civil rights enforcement is justified. Certification authorities should independently assess whether the officers may work in law enforcement elsewhere.

City officials and oversight bodies should demand aggregated public results: number of encounters assessed, cases flagged, complaints reexamined, referrals, outcomes, and remedies. Privacy and fair-process protections matter, but they do not justify silence about overall results.

Residents should not be told that the matter is closed because officers resigned. Resignation is not restitution. Termination is not a remedy for a person who lost income or liberty. And an investigation that checks the conduct of employees but never accounts for anyone they may have harmed is incomplete.

## We are still asking to be left alone

There is something exhausting about being told racism is a thing of the past while fresh revelations keep making it a thing of the present. Black Americans are not asking to evade lawful investigation or receive special permission to break rules. We are asking to be treated by the same rules as everyone else.

We work, pay taxes, raise families, and contribute to the communities that police are supposed to serve. There should be no special courage required to drive to work without wondering whether someone wearing a badge sees your skin color as a game.

The response to Charlotte cannot stop with six employment files. It must follow the evidence to the people those officers encountered and determine whether anybody needs their rights or losses restored.

**If an institution can investigate its officers, discipline its employees, and close the file without determining what happened to the people those officers policed, it has answered only half the question.**

The other half is the one we keep asking: **Why can't you just leave us the hell alone and treat us like everybody else?**
