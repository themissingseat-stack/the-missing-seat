## Short Version

What happened: A child can have a credit file because of identity theft, an error or a legitimate account. Federal law gives families a way to freeze credit before a problem shows up.

Who it affects: Children in the United States and the parents or guardians handling their financial records.

What you can do: Start with the FTC’s child-identity guide. Follow each bureau’s instructions separately. If an account is already fraudulent, use the recovery steps too.

Based on: Federal law, FTC and CFPB guidance, and TransUnion’s published instructions.

## Website editorial

A bill addressed to a child should make an adult stop and check. It does not automatically prove a crime, but it is not something to dismiss because the child has never borrowed money. The record can exist before the child understands what borrowing means.

A child may not discover a wrong record until they need credit of their own. By then, they or their caregiver may have to explain that a debt in their name was never theirs.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau says children generally do not have credit reports. It also identifies several reasons one might exist: identity theft, a file created in error, or a legitimate connection such as being an authorized user on an adult's account. A file is a reason to investigate, not a verdict against a parent, a relative or a company. [1]

## The protection does not require a purchase

There is a useful protection families can request before a problem surfaces. The FTC tells parents and guardians of children under 16 that they can request a free credit freeze. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds may request and remove a security freeze themselves. The child's process differs from the ordinary adult process. [2]

A parent does not have to wait for the child to build a borrowing history. Federal law requires the bureau to create a limited record if it receives a qualifying request for a child under 16 who has no file. The bureau creates that record to apply the freeze; it cannot use it to judge the child’s creditworthiness. That is the important detail: having no previous credit account does not make the protection unavailable. [3]

The practical burden is that a family must deal separately with the three nationwide reporting companies. One request is not a request to all three. TransUnion's current child-identity page, for example, describes a written protected-consumer request supported by proof of authority and identification. Its procedure should not be assumed to be the procedure at every bureau. [4]

This is where a useful right can become another household project. A caregiver has to find the correct route, understand the documents, submit the request and keep track of the answer. We have not measured how much time that takes or how many families abandon it. But the steps themselves are visible in the instructions. A service can be free of a fee and still require work.

## Who handles the record

Credit reporting companies maintain the records. Creditors make decisions using them, subject to the rules that apply. Parents and guardians are asked to establish their authority to act for a child. That proof has a legitimate purpose: a person should not be able to take control of someone else's file simply by claiming to be family.

The fair question is whether the legitimate security check is explained clearly enough for an ordinary caregiver to complete. A company should be able to say which document is missing, where it belongs and how the family will know the request was completed. Asking for that clarity is different from asking a company to ignore identity verification.

The immediate beneficiaries of a properly placed freeze are the child and the family trying to prevent unauthorized borrowing. We are not alleging that any of the three companies refused a lawful request in a case investigated for this article. This is an explanation of an available protection and the responsibility attached to making it usable.

The burden of a false account can reach beyond a number on a screen. Someone must dispute the record, contact the company involved and establish what happened. For a family already handling work, care and school, that is another task competing for time.

## A freeze has limits

A freeze is useful for restricting access connected with new credit. It is not a repair order for an account that already exists, and it should not be presented as protection against every use of stolen information. Some access is still allowed under the law, including for certain existing account activity. [3]

An authorized-user record and a fraudulent account are not the same thing. The response should follow the evidence in the file. If there is an error, CFPB directs consumers to dispute it with both the reporting company and the company that held the account. If a reporting-company problem remains, its guidance links to the CFPB complaint route. [1]

## Pull Up A Chair

Start with the FTC's child-identity page. Use its official links to each bureau, find the current instructions for minors, and make a separate checklist for each request. Use the legitimate submission channels those pages identify. Keep confirmations and the instructions for later removal where the family can find them.

If a suspicious account already exists, follow the FTC's recovery guidance as well as considering a freeze. Freezing and correcting are separate jobs. 

Schools, family-service organizations and libraries can make this protection easier to find by sharing official instructions. They do not need to collect children's identification documents themselves. Helping a parent reach the right door is useful work; creating another store of sensitive family information is not a necessary part of it.

If you freeze a child’s credit, keep the confirmations with the family’s important papers and tell them where to find them when they’re old enough to apply for credit. That way they can use the protection now and remove it when they need to.
