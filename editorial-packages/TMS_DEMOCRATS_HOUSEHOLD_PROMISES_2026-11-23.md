## Website editorial

A family opening an electricity bill cannot pay it with an election result. A parent putting food back on the shelf has a practical question for every politician promising change. When will there be enough money to buy it? The incoming Democratic majority should expect that question from the people whose votes helped make it possible.

Hakeem Jeffries supplied commitments on November 17 that can be tracked. He promised to pursue Affordable Care Act tax credits, reverse Medicaid cuts and work on the cost of groceries, gas and housing. Those are his stated priorities. Their household effects depend on legislation, implementation and which people qualify. A press conference supplies a promise and a date for the record. [1]

The New Democrat Coalition's February affordability agenda gives additional substance. It proposes internet assistance, working-family tax relief, child care policies, housing supply and protection against electricity costs associated with major new loads. It also illustrates the time problem. Building homes over a decade addresses a different need from preventing an eviction next month. A family can require both. [2]

Congress should therefore publish a delivery account alongside its first affordability bill. The account should show the amount a household could save, the eligible population, the agency responsible, the money required for administration and the first plausible date of receipt. Workers without dependent children, caregivers, disabled adults and retirees belong in the examples. An average benefit can conceal a group receiving nothing.

This editorial proposes an additional standard for the opening months. Pair the first major legislative package with public oversight of assistance already authorized. Where an agency is missing application deadlines, investigate staffing, systems and wrongful denials. Where an assistance program needs funding, show the appropriation and the implementation plan. Constituents should be able to distinguish a new entitlement from improved delivery of an existing one.

The incoming majority will face other branches and its own procedural constraints. Passage in the House does not enact a law. Senate rules, the scope of budget reconciliation, presidential approval or a veto override can determine the route. A serious plan names the route and the likely obstacle. Negotiating over scope and financing is part of governing. Leaving the public unable to tell which stage a measure has reached makes accountability harder.

The outgoing Congress still holds its present authority before the new Congress begins. A time-sensitive measure should be examined for what can happen in the current session as well as the next. Each bill needs a refreshed record of its actual status. This draft does not claim that a particular unfinished measure has passed.

Investigations should also produce a public benefit that can be described. Recovering diverted funds, documenting illegal conduct and stopping an unlawful denial of services are meaningful objectives. The committee should explain its question, findings and correction. Working families have an interest in honest government and in food on the table, and deserve an agenda capable of pursuing both.

Readers can choose a commitment and follow it from statement to bill text, committee action, floor vote and implementation. Record who moves it, who blocks it and what each actor says. Then compare the promised household benefit with the actual result. After an election, the people who were asked to wait patiently should be able to see the calendar.
