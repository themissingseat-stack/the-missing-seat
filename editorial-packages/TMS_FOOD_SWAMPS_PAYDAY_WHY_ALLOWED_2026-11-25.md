## The Short Version

**What happened:** In many low-income and Black neighborhoods, the main street is fast food, corner stores, check cashers and payday lenders instead of grocery stores, banks and businesses that build jobs. Research shows these "food swamps" predict obesity better than the absence of grocery stores, and that payday lenders and check cashers cluster in Black neighborhoods.

**Who it affects:** Families who pay more for worse food and worse financial services, and kids raised on ads aimed straight at them.

**What you can do:** Find out who decides what opens on your block, usually the city's zoning and planning office, and show up when the next permit comes up. Ask what your city does to bring in grocers, credit unions and local businesses.

## Website editorial

Drive through a wealthy suburb and look at what lines the main road: a grocery store, a bank branch, a sit-down restaurant, maybe a bookstore. Now drive through a poor neighborhood in the same city. Fried chicken, a dollar store, a liquor store, a check casher, a payday lender, another fried chicken.

That is not an accident of taste. It is what gets built, what gets financed, what gets advertised, and what city rules allow. All of those are choices, and all of them can change.

### It's the swamp, not only the desert

For years, the problem was described as a "food desert," a place with no grocery store. Researchers at the University of Connecticut's Rudd Center found something more telling. A "food swamp," a neighborhood where fast food and junk food outlets outnumber healthy options, predicted adult obesity rates better than a food desert did, even after controlling for food deserts. [1] The lead researcher put it simply: fast food and corner stores are not bad in themselves, but when they become most of what a neighborhood can rely on, that's a problem. [2]

### The ads are aimed at our kids

The food doesn't only sit on the corner. It is sold to children. Fast-food companies spent $5 billion on advertising in 2019, and Black youth saw 75 percent more fast-food ads than their white peers. [3] In 2017, unhealthy products made up 86 percent of food advertising spending on Black-targeted TV programming. [4] Only about 1 percent of fast-food advertising promoted healthier menu items. [5]

### The money businesses follow the same map

It isn't only food. Payday lenders and check cashers charge far more than banks, which some call a "ghetto tax." A national study of these businesses found big gaps between white and non-white neighborhoods, largest in the most segregated cities. The gap between Black and white neighborhoods was widest among high-income neighborhoods, so even affluent Black areas are targeted. [6] In ZIP codes where at least half of households are Black, there are nearly 20 percent fewer banks and credit unions than the national average. [7]

So the same neighborhood that gets the fryer instead of the grocer gets the payday lender instead of the bank. Both take money out of the community and send it somewhere else.

### Why is it allowed?

Because in most places, nothing stops it. If a lot is zoned for commercial use, a drive-through or a check casher can often open without a public hearing. Meanwhile, grocers and banks decide where to open based on profit and risk, and decades of redlining and disinvestment taught them to avoid the same neighborhoods. Fast food and payday lending fill the gap.

### What works, and what doesn't

Banning the bad isn't enough on its own. In 2008, Los Angeles banned new stand-alone fast-food restaurants in South LA. [8] RAND researchers later found that South LA actually had fewer fast-food chain restaurants per person than wealthier West LA. The real gap was the shortage of grocery stores and the flood of small food stores. Obesity rose anyway. [9][10] The lesson: you can't zone your way to health only by saying no. You also have to bring something in.

That points to fixes that build:
- **Bring in the good on purpose.** Use city incentives, land deals and financing to recruit grocers, sit-down restaurants, credit unions and Black-owned businesses, the way cities compete for a stadium or a factory. Los Angeles paired its ban with incentives for grocery stores and table-service restaurants. [11]
- **Spread out what drains.** Many cities have passed zoning rules that limit how close payday lenders can cluster together. [12] They can do the same for liquor stores and other businesses that harm more than they help.
- **Give neighbors a say.** Require a public hearing before a drive-through, payday lender or liquor store opens near schools and homes.
- **Stop targeting kids.** The Rudd Center has called on fast-food companies to end their disproportionate marketing of unhealthy food to Black and Hispanic youth. [13]

### The bottom line

Nobody is saying people can't buy a burger. The question is why some neighborhoods get a choice and others get the same five storefronts on every corner. Rich neighborhoods get businesses that build wealth. Ours get businesses that drain it. That is a decision made in zoning offices, bank boardrooms and marketing departments, and decisions can be changed.

## Pull Up A Chair

- **Find your city's planning or zoning office** and sign up for notices of new permits in your area.
- **Ask your council member** what the city does to recruit grocers, credit unions and local businesses to your neighborhood.
- **Support community-owned options:** credit unions instead of payday lenders, local markets, Black-owned restaurants.
- **Talk to kids about ads.** Ask them who the ad is for and what it's selling.
