# Germany Promised a Full School Day. Families Need the Hours to Match.

A school place and a working day do not automatically fit together. A parent can get a child safely to class, turn up for work and still face a gap in the afternoon that nobody else has agreed to cover. The usual answer is to find a relative, buy additional care or change the working day. Each answer assumes the family has something available to give.

Germany has taken an important step toward changing that arrangement. The new school-age care entitlement starts with children entering first grade in the 2026/2027 school year. That deserves recognition. It also deserves a question more useful than whether the government made a good announcement: can a family actually organize its day around the provision it receives?

Under the current wording of section 24 of the Social Code’s child and youth welfare book, the covered cohort has a care entitlement from school entry until the start of fifth grade. It covers eight hours from Monday to Friday, excluding public holidays. Lessons and all-day school activities count toward those hours. It is not eight additional hours after school.

The holiday rules matter too. Qualifying public or recognized youth-work provision can fulfill the holiday entitlement, and state law can permit up to four weeks of closure during school holidays. Families need the actual calendar, not an assumption that the school gate will stay open whenever a parent has to work.

The same provision also calls for needs-based provision beyond those eight hours. That is a further planning responsibility to ask about, not a promise that every requested shift or preferred provider is guaranteed. That is the legal starting point, refreshed against the current statute on October 26. It is not a finding that every municipality has delivered every place, nor evidence that every family has been refused. The distinction matters because a right and its delivery are two separate things we should be able to examine without dismissing either.

## Who is missing?

The missing seat belongs to the child whose afternoon is treated as a scheduling problem and to the adult expected to solve it privately. A child needs more than somewhere to wait. Rest, play, relationships and support should matter alongside the hours on a timetable. Parents need an arrangement they can understand before accepting shifts or changing their work.

Think about a possible family situation, rather than an invented case. A parent has an early shift, a child has a school day ending before that shift does, and the care offer covers some afternoons but leaves uncertainty around holidays. The parent might have an excellent provider and still lack a usable plan. What matters is the whole arrangement, including the transition from class to care and the dates when the arrangement changes.

This affects people beyond parents. Employers benefit when staff can plan reliably. Colleagues benefit when coverage is agreed rather than rearranged at the last minute. Children benefit when adults have time to make decisions without a daily scramble. These are reasons to support dependable provision, not measured outcomes that this article claims the reform has already achieved.

The burden of a gap is uneven. Someone with flexible hours, spare income or nearby relatives has options that another household may lack. That is a practical inference, not a claim about a newly measured national rate. We should ask who can use the offer, including single parents, families with limited German, and children needing disability support, rather than assume that publishing the same information gives everyone the same access.

There is also a missing seat for the people providing care. A timetable cannot tell us whether staff have enough support to do their work well. Expanding a promise without listening to educators would be a poor way to protect children. The answer should not be to expect workers to cover every gap through goodwill.

## Make the promise usable

The power sits with the public bodies arranging provision and the institutions delivering it. Families can state their needs and choose among available arrangements, but they cannot individually create staffing, premises or a coordinated local system. The statute also requires the responsible youth-welfare bodies or their delegated offices to inform and advise parents about local provision. That creates a concrete place to begin.

There is a fair counterargument. A nationwide entitlement has to work through different local arrangements. Schools, care providers and youth-welfare services do not all operate in the same way. A reform beginning with one cohort is not a promise that every older primary-school pupil immediately receives the same new entitlement. Clear reporting should respect that phased scope.

But local differences should be explained to families before they become a lost afternoon. A child covered by the law needs a clear answer about the available arrangement. When the proposed hours do not meet the family's understanding of the entitlement, the responsible office should explain how it has counted them and what the parent can do next.

For an ordinary reader, the principle is familiar. If your work contract says eight hours, the start, finish and breaks matter. Public care needs the same practical clarity. Calling something an all-day offer is not enough to tell a parent whether it covers a particular working schedule. Eight covered hours may still leave commuting or unusual shifts to arrange. That limit should be visible too.

The immediate action is to ask the school and local Jugendamt, the youth-welfare office, for one written account of the arrangement: the daily start and finish, which lesson hours are included, where the child goes afterward, holiday dates, closures, costs and the contact responsible for unresolved gaps. State any accessibility or communication needs. Ask which registration deadlines apply locally.

If an answer is incomplete, keep the request and response. Ask for the applicable procedure for a written decision and independent advice about review options. This article cannot promise a particular place, provider, free service or outcome in an individual dispute. It can insist that a family should not have to guess what the public offer means.

The wider action belongs with local councils and youth-welfare committees. Ask them to publish aggregate figures for the covered cohort: requests, offers, unresolved requests, hours provided and holiday coverage. Ask them to explain what they measure as fulfillment and let families and staff test whether that description matches daily life. Keep children's names and private circumstances out of public campaigning.

Germany's new entitlement is a meaningful opportunity to make education and working life fit together better. The next test is ordinary and demanding: a child has somewhere appropriate to go, a parent knows the hours, and somebody accepts responsibility when the plan does not connect. That is where a promise begins to become a service.
