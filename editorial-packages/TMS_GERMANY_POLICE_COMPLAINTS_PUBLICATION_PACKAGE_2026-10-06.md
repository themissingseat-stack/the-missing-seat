## Website Editorial

Germany did something worth recognizing. It created an independent place where people can report possible misconduct and structural problems involving the federal police. That matters. Police officers carry public authority that most people will never have. They can stop, question, search, detain and use force. The public needs somewhere outside the police chain of command that can examine whether that authority was used properly. Police employees also need somewhere to report serious internal problems without first going through the same hierarchy they may be questioning.

The Federal Parliamentary Commissioner for the Police Authorities is supposed to provide that seat. The office sits with the Bundestag, is independent and is not bound by instructions. It can request statements, question federal police employees, inspect files and visit federal police premises without advance notice when the matter falls within its legal authority.

That is more than a suggestion box. But the existence of a seat does not prove that everybody can reach it, knows it exists or receives a meaningful result.

## GESCHICHTEN

The office's activity report covers July 1, 2025 through June 30, 2026. It recorded 421 submissions, up from 267 in the previous reporting period. Of the 421 submissions, 323 came from members of the public and 98 from employees of the federal police authorities.

Fifty submissions reported concrete indications of discrimination or racial profiling during federal police activity. The commissioner opened investigations in 33 of those cases. Most reportedly concerned controls at Germany's borders, including airports, railway stations and trains.

Those numbers do not prove that racial profiling occurred in all 50 cases. They are reports made to the commissioner, and only 33 led to investigation. An opened investigation is not a finding of misconduct. The Bundestag’s fuller July 8 account also reports that the commissioner criticized police conduct in ten cases, while some investigations remained underway. That is an official oversight assessment, not a criminal conviction.

The figures still tell us something important. People are trying to use the office, and discrimination during police controls is not an imaginary subject invented by social media. It is a category serious enough to appear in the official report of the Bundestag's independent police commissioner.

The report also describes pressure on officers working at border controls. The commissioner said federal police employees were under substantial strain and called for more workable arrangements. Some infrastructure had improved, but officers at certain control points still faced inadequate protection from heat or cold and unacceptable sanitary conditions.

This is not a choice between the rights of the public and the working conditions of police. Badly designed systems can injure both. Exhausted officers working in poor conditions may be asked to carry out politically important controls at high volume, while travelers experience decisions that can feel unexplained, inconsistent or discriminatory. Accountability has to examine the whole system, not merely pick a side.

## BELEGE

Germany's 2024 Police Commissioner Act gives the office meaningful powers. It may investigate structural failures involving the Federal Police, the Federal Criminal Police Office and the Police at the German Bundestag. It may also examine possible individual misconduct, especially conduct suggesting a violation of fundamental rights, including the equality guarantee in Article 3 of the Basic Law.

Police employees may contact the commissioner directly without following the normal chain of command. The law says they may not suffer employment disadvantages because of a submission. Members of the public may submit a complaint orally or in writing. They must have been personally affected, and the information must indicate a possible structural problem or broader failure involving one of the three federal police authorities. A complaint must generally arrive within six months after the person learned of the events.

That sentence contains both the promise and the barrier. The promise is an independent route with access to police files, employees and premises. The barrier is that many people do not know whether the officer who stopped them belonged to the Federal Police or a state police force. Germany's policing structure may be clear to a lawyer and invisible to the traveler standing on a train platform.

The commissioner does not cover every police officer in Germany. Most everyday policing is performed by the police forces of the Länder. Complaints about them belong in state systems, where available complaint bodies, powers and procedures vary.

The six-month deadline can also matter. A person may spend weeks deciding whether an experience was improper, seeking legal advice, finding a translator or learning that this office exists. Someone who fears immigration consequences or another encounter with police may hesitate longer. The law allows oral submissions and says identity can be kept confidential from the police authority on request, but the complainant must still provide a name, address, account of what happened and when they learned of it. Confidentiality is not absolute: section 3 provides for witness identification in relevant proceedings and possible disclosure after consultation when needed for investigation.

Those rules may be defensible for investigation and fairness. They are still access conditions, and the Bundestag should measure who they exclude. The commissioner can investigate, seek an agreed resolution, publish important findings and refer possible criminal or disciplinary matters to the responsible authorities. The office is not a court. It does not replace a criminal complaint, a civil claim, an administrative challenge or legal deadlines elsewhere. It cannot personally convict an officer or award damages.

A new accountability office should be judged neither as useless because it cannot do everything nor as successful because it exists. The proper test is whether people can find the right door, receive an independent examination and see institutions change when patterns emerge.

## WER FEHLT?

The missing seats include people most likely to face confusing control situations and least likely to understand the complaint architecture: Black Germans, migrants, refugees, Muslims, Roma and Sinti, tourists, people who speak little German, disabled people, young travelers and anyone uncertain whether the officer was federal or state.

Police employees also belong in this picture. An officer who sees discrimination, unsafe conditions, broken procedures or pressure to carry out an unworkable policy may need an independent route as much as a civilian does.

Who benefits when the office works? The person whose complaint is taken seriously benefits. Officers benefit when false assumptions can be corrected and structural problems are not quietly left on individual employees. Parliament benefits from evidence it can use for oversight and legislation. The wider public benefits when trust rests on examination rather than slogans.

Who holds power? The commissioner has investigative power. The Bundestag controls the law and resources. Federal police authorities control many of the records and the implementation of changes. Prosecutors, disciplinary authorities and courts control separate legal consequences.

Who carries the burden when the system is hard to use? The person who was stopped must identify the correct police body, understand the six-month rule, document the encounter and decide whether speaking up is worth the risk. Community groups and lawyers often become the unpaid map through the complaint system.

## Why an Ordinary Person Should Care

You do not have to believe that German police are generally abusive to support independent accountability. In fact, a credible complaint system protects good policing. It can separate mistakes from misconduct, individual behavior from structural failure and evidence from rumor. It can show whether a troubling experience was an isolated event or part of a repeating pattern.

Without that channel, the public is pushed toward two bad choices. Either believe every accusation immediately or trust the institution to investigate itself behind closed doors. A parliamentary commissioner offers a better possibility: independent access, evidence, explanation and public reporting.

But that possibility works only if ordinary people know where to go.

## Strongest Counterargument

Federal police authorities conduct millions of interactions, many under pressure and in legally complex situations. Fifty submissions mentioning discrimination or racial profiling cannot establish a systemwide rate, and the rise from 267 to 421 submissions may reflect growing awareness of the new office rather than worsening police conduct.

That is correct. Complaint figures alone cannot measure prevalence or prove guilt. The answer is not to ignore them. The answer is to publish better outcome data: how many submissions were outside jurisdiction, how many arrived too late, how many investigations found individual misconduct or structural problems, what recommendations followed and whether the responsible authorities implemented them.

An accountability office should not inflate complaints into convictions. It should not allow complaints to disappear into a count either.

## HANDELN

The Bundestag and the commissioner should publish a plain-language access and outcome dashboard in German, English and the languages most often needed during federal police contacts. It should explain:

1. which police forces the commissioner covers;
2. how a person can identify whether an officer belonged to a federal or state force;
3. the six-month submission deadline;
4. what confidentiality can and cannot protect;
5. what the commissioner can investigate and what requires another legal route;
6. how many submissions were accepted, rejected, redirected or closed;
7. how many investigations found misconduct or structural failure; and
8. which recommendations were implemented.

The office should publish a simple referral map for every Land so that a person who reaches the wrong door is directed to the correct complaint body instead of being left to begin again. Parliament should use the law's required evaluation to ask whether the personal-effect requirement, six-month limit and federal-only jurisdiction make the office less usable for people most exposed to discrimination. The evaluation should include anonymous feedback from complainants whose cases were accepted, rejected and redirected.

## One Realistic Action

Save the Bundestag's official complaint page before you need it. If you experience or witness questionable conduct involving the Federal Police, the Federal Criminal Police Office or the Police at the Bundestag, record the date, place, agency, officer identifiers if visible, witnesses and what was said or done.

If you were personally affected, check the commissioner's jurisdiction quickly because the submission deadline is generally six months. Contact details and instructions are published by the Bundestag. If the matter may involve injury, detention, criminal conduct, immigration status or another legal deadline, seek qualified legal advice because a submission to the commissioner does not automatically replace other remedies.

Then ask one public question of the Bundestag:

> Of the 421 submissions received in 2025-2026, how many were accepted, redirected, rejected as late or outside jurisdiction, substantiated, and followed by an implemented institutional change?

That answer would tell the public whether Germany created only a place to complain or a route to accountability.

## GEMEINSAM

Germany created a seat for police complaints. That deserves credit. Now the country must show who can reach it, what happens after someone sits down and whether verified problems change the institution that caused them. Trust is not built by telling people to trust the police. It is built when police power can be questioned fairly, investigated independently and corrected openly.

## Verification Notice

**Official report:** The 421 submissions, the division between 323 public and 98 employee submissions, and the 50 discrimination or racial-profiling reports come from the commissioner's 2025-2026 activity report as summarized by the Bundestag.

**Investigation, not finding:** Thirty-three investigations were opened from the 50 discrimination or racial-profiling submissions. An investigation does not establish misconduct. This package does not claim that all 50 allegations were true.

**Scope:** The federal commissioner covers the Federal Police, Federal Criminal Police Office and Police at the Bundestag. The office does not generally cover the police forces of Germany's Länder.

**Legal powers:** The Bundestag’s July 8 account also reports criticism of police conduct in ten cases. This is an oversight assessment rather than a court judgment.

**Legal powers:** The Act authorizes document requests, questioning, access to premises, reporting and referrals. The commissioner is not a criminal court and does not personally impose convictions or award damages.

**Access limits:** The six-month deadline, personal-effect requirement and identity information are stated in the Act. The conclusion that these conditions may deter or exclude some users is an editorial inference that should be tested through the law's formal evaluation.

**Trend limitation:** Increased submissions may indicate visibility and trust in a new office, increased problems, or both. The count alone cannot determine the cause or measure the overall frequency of misconduct.

**No generalized accusation:** This article does not allege that German police as a whole discriminate or that border controls are inherently unlawful.

Germany created an independent federal police commissioner so citizens and police employees have somewhere outside the police chain of command to report possible misconduct and structural problems.

The new Bundestag report shows that people are using it. From July 2025 through June 2026, the office received 421 submissions, up from 267. Fifty reported concrete indications of discrimination or racial profiling. The commissioner opened 33 investigations.

Those are complaints and investigations, not 50 proven cases. But they are also not nothing.

The office has real powers. It can question federal police employees, request files, visit premises and publish important findings. Yet its reach is narrow. It covers the Federal Police, Federal Criminal Police Office and Police at the Bundestag, not every state police force. A civilian complainant must be personally affected, identify themselves and generally submit the complaint within six months.

The missing seat is access. Does an ordinary traveler know whether the officer at a station was federal or state police? Does a person who speaks little German know where to go? If the wrong office receives the complaint, is the person redirected before the deadline expires?

Ask the Bundestag to publish what happened to the 421 submissions: how many were accepted, redirected, rejected, substantiated and followed by an implemented change.

Germany created a seat for police complaints. Now show us who can actually use it.
