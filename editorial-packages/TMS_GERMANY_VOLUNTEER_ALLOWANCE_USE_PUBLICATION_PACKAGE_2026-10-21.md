## Editorial

Germany runs on work that does not always look like a job. Somebody coaches the children after school. Somebody keeps a local club's accounts. Somebody trains new volunteers, helps at a cultural association, supports a rescue organization, or teaches a community course after finishing the work that pays the rent.

That labor is often described as “voluntary,” which is true in one sense and incomplete in another. It still takes time, skill, travel and responsibility. A modest payment does not turn community service into a commercial career. It can make the difference between being able to keep showing up and having to stop.

Germany made a useful adjustment. From 2026, the tax-free annual allowance for eligible instructors, trainers, educators and comparable activities rose to €3,300. The broader allowance for eligible voluntary work rose to €960. Parliament approved those changes in the 2025 tax amendment law, which took effect for the new tax year.

A seat was made. The people doing quiet civic work can keep a little more of modest eligible compensation without income tax. That deserves credit.

The job is not finished when the number changes.

## The good idea behind the allowance

The Übungsleiterpauschale is commonly associated with coaches, but its legal scope is more specific than the nickname suggests. It covers certain part-time teaching, training, educational, artistic and care activities performed for public-law bodies or qualifying nonprofit, charitable or religious organizations. The separate Ehrenamtspauschale can cover other eligible part-time work serving qualifying tax-advantaged purposes.

These are tax exemptions, not government grants. They do not guarantee that an organization pays a volunteer, and they do not turn every payment for community work into tax-free income. The activity, organization, purpose and amount all matter. Social-insurance, expense-reimbursement, employment and minimum-wage questions can also require separate analysis.

That distinction is important because simple political language can create a false impression. “Volunteers can earn €960 tax-free” sounds as if every person who helps a neighbor or receives money from any club automatically qualifies. The law is narrower. A person should check the role and the organization instead of relying on a slogan.

Still, the basic policy makes sense. Parliament recognized that civic participation has costs and that modest compensation should not be treated as if it were ordinary commercial profit from the first euro.

## Who is missing?

The missing seat belongs to the volunteer who does not have a tax adviser, and to the small club whose treasurer is learning the rules after work. It belongs to the parent who could coach one evening a week if travel and equipment costs did not swallow the payment. It belongs to people with lower incomes, care duties or disabilities who may have less room to donate time and money at the same time.

Large organizations can buy professional advice. Small associations often depend on another volunteer to understand payroll, tax and documentation. The federal government writes the rule. Tax authorities interpret and administer it. Organizations decide whether they pay anything and how clearly they explain the arrangement. The volunteer carries the risk of misunderstanding what was tax-free, what was reimbursement and what needed to be declared.

The immediate beneficiaries are eligible volunteers and organizations that can use the larger allowance to recruit or retain them. Communities benefit when sports, education, culture, care and emergency support remain available. The public carries the cost through reduced tax revenue, although the volunteer allowance was only one part of a much larger tax package. This editorial does not assign a stand-alone national cost or claim that the increase created more volunteering. The published official sources do not establish those outcomes.

## What success should look like

A higher allowance is measurable on paper. Its social result is not automatic.

Success would mean that more eligible people can continue useful civic work, especially people who could not otherwise afford to donate both labor and expenses. It would mean that small organizations understand the rules well enough to use them without fear. It would also mean that the relief does not mainly reward arrangements that would have existed anyway while leaving lower-income volunteers outside because their organization cannot pay them at all.

There is a fair counterargument. Tax exemptions add complexity, favor people who receive eligible payments, and reduce public revenue. Unpaid volunteers receive no direct benefit from a larger tax-free ceiling. A simple allowance can also hide a harder question: whether essential services are leaning too heavily on people who are called volunteers because institutions do not want to fund proper jobs.

Those objections do not erase the value of the change. They tell us what the receipt should measure. How many people use each allowance? What kinds of organizations reach them? Are rural groups and small associations able to participate? Does the change help recruit or retain volunteers? Are people replacing paid staff under the cover of civic service?

A positive story becomes public-interest journalism when we ask whether the good idea reached the people it was meant to help.

## Why an ordinary person should care

Even if you never receive either allowance, you probably know the work. It is the training session, neighborhood event, youth team, first-aid group, cultural program or local association that still exists because someone accepted responsibility after their regular workday.

Communities lose more than an activity when those people burn out. They lose relationships, safe places, practical knowledge and the habit of solving problems together. A small tax rule will not rebuild civic life by itself. It can remove one unnecessary penalty from people already carrying part of it.

The useful question is not whether government should praise volunteers. Governments are good at praise. The question is whether the rules respect their time and whether the result can be seen.

## The action

If you receive money for part-time civic work, ask the organization to identify in writing which allowance it believes applies, the qualifying purpose, the amount paid, and whether any separate expense reimbursement is included. Keep the agreement and annual payment record. Do not assume the label “volunteer” decides the tax treatment.

If you help run an association, publish a one-page explanation in plain German. State which roles qualify, what records are kept, whom volunteers can ask, and when professional tax advice is needed. Do not promise tax exemption for a role until the actual legal conditions have been checked.

The federal and state finance authorities should publish usage data that protects privacy but shows whether the increased allowances reach small organizations, rural communities and people across income groups. After two tax years, Parliament should ask for a public evaluation of participation, administrative burden and unintended substitution of paid work.

Germany did something sensible by leaving more modest civic compensation in the hands of eligible volunteers. Now it should show whether the seat can be used by people without a tax department behind them.

## German social version

Deutschland hat 2026 die steuerfreien Pauschalen für begünstigtes ehrenamtliches Engagement erhöht: bis zu 3.300 Euro für bestimmte nebenberufliche Tätigkeiten als Übungsleiterin, Ausbilder, Erzieherin, Betreuer oder in vergleichbaren Bereichen und bis zu 960 Euro für andere begünstigte ehrenamtliche Tätigkeiten.

Das ist eine sinnvolle Anerkennung. Aber es ist ein Steuerfreibetrag, kein Zuschuss, und nicht jede Zahlung mit dem Etikett „Ehrenamt“ ist automatisch steuerfrei.

Wer eine Vergütung erhält, sollte sich schriftlich erklären lassen, welche Pauschale angewendet wird. Kleine Vereine sollten ihren Ehrenamtlichen eine verständliche Ein-Seiten-Erklärung und eine feste Ansprechperson geben.

Der Platz wurde geschaffen. Jetzt muss sichtbar werden, wer ihn tatsächlich nutzen kann.
