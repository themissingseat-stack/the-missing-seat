## Website editorial

Raúl Krauthausen and the Sozialhelden team helped turn a question about getting through a doorway into information other people could use. Wheelmap lets contributors describe wheelchair access to public places, rather than leaving each visitor to start the search again. It is a practical example of people making a missing seat easier to find.

Krauthausen is an inclusion activist and founder of Sozialhelden. His organization works on participation by people with disabilities, including mobility and digital information. The people whose access is at stake are helping shape the tool, rather than appearing only as the subject of someone else's good intentions. [1]

That deserves attention. Planning a visit can mean checking an entrance, the spaces inside and a toilet, not simply an address and opening time. Better information can help someone choose where to go or identify what still needs confirming. This article does not claim to have interviewed a particular visitor or measured how many journeys the project saved.

## What the map makes possible

Wheelmap uses a traffic-light rating system for wheelchair access, including a category for places not yet rated. Its documentation explains how people can contribute ratings and photographs and how the map uses OpenStreetMap. A person can share an observation instead of keeping useful knowledge inside one household or group of friends. [2]

The project's current overview says information is available on more than 3.2 million publicly accessible places. That is the operator's reported information coverage, not a count independently audited for this editorial. It is certainly not proof that those places are all accessible. A record describing a barrier is still a record in the map. [3]

The distinction is important to a positive story. Building a shared information resource is a concrete output worth recognizing. Claiming that the resource has already removed millions of barriers would turn that achievement into something the evidence does not show.

A map entry also does not describe every person's requirements. An entrance rating may be useful while leaving another question unresolved. Germany's national tourism organization describes Wheelmap's basic step-free-access and toilet information and recommends obtaining access information while planning. A category on a screen should not be mistaken for a complete assessment of a particular person's visit. [4]

## A local partnership puts names beside the work

The district of Olpe provides a useful example of how a tool can become organized community work. Its official account says the district pays for a Wheelmap Pro licence, with the Sozialhelden team providing the technical implementation. The IKO inclusion project coordinates participation and training. The same account identifies Anna Elberg, Thomas Heckel and Sylvia Müller among the people organizing that work. [5]

This is more than a vague invitation to care. There is a local public partner, a technical provider and people responsible for helping contributors take part. The district describes collecting information beyond wheelchair access, including information relevant to people with sight, hearing or learning difficulties. Those are features of the local extended project; they should not be assumed to exist in every ordinary Wheelmap record. [5]

The district's account establishes an arrangement and a way to participate. It does not provide an evaluation demonstrating how many residents gained access, which buildings were altered or how many contributors remained active. Those are the next results to request. A paid licence and a training programme are resources and activities; their value should eventually be visible in usable information and better access.

Giving the team credit does not require pretending those questions have been answered. The useful achievement is that responsibility for gathering information has been organized and made public. The stronger result would connect an identified problem to a decision and a change a person can actually use.

## Who benefits, and who still has the work?

The missing seat belongs to someone whose visit requires information other people can usually take for granted. Contributors can help distribute the research, and venues may benefit when potential visitors can understand their access arrangements. Those are plausible benefits of the method, not quantified revenue or participation effects measured here.

Power over the physical place usually remains elsewhere. A contributor can record a step, but the owner or responsible public body controls decisions about altering the entrance. A software team can improve a description without having authority over the building. Volunteers should not become the people expected to compensate indefinitely for a barrier they did not create.

There is a fair concern about relying on contributed information. An entry may be incomplete, circumstances may change, and an image may not show the detail a particular visitor needs. A large dataset does not remove that concern. For an important visit, check the available description and confirm critical details directly with the venue.

There is also an institutional limit. Mapping can help identify barriers and make them visible, but officials should not use the existence of a map as evidence that the access problem is solved. The measurement has to distinguish places recorded, information kept current and barriers actually removed.

## Add an observation, then follow the response

The immediate action is small enough to complete. Read Wheelmap's current rating instructions, choose a public place you know and contribute accurate access information through the project's official route. Describe what you can establish, rather than guessing. Any photograph should focus on the relevant entrance or facility without exposing identifiable visitors or private information. This editorial did not submit ratings or test the contribution process.

A venue can help by publishing clear, current access information and explaining whom a visitor can contact about a mismatch. Where a barrier can be addressed, identify the responsible person, the proposed change and the date for checking it. Obtain qualified access advice where necessary; a map category is not a building specification or a legal compliance certificate.

For councils and community partners, the next action is to publish a simple account of what followed the mapping. How much information was checked, what problems were referred, what changed and who still could not use the place should remain separate. That would allow residents to recognize useful progress without losing sight of unfinished work.

Wheelmap offers a constructive idea that other communities can examine: people can gather knowledge together, use a shared method and make the result available to someone they have never met. The German initiative and its local partners deserve credit for that practical contribution. The next seat is made when the information leads to a change at the actual doorway and the person who needed it can get through.
