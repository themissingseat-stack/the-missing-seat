## The Short Version

What happened: Federal rules tie the tax exemption of nonprofit hospitals to a written financial assistance policy, and the regulations also set minimum timing rules before a hospital may take extraordinary collection actions such as lawsuits, liens, wage garnishment or credit reporting. A May 2026 investigation of Minnesota hospitals found that eligibility limits and paperwork vary widely, and its reporters wrote that similar variation exists nationwide.

Who it affects: Patients and caregivers with a hospital bill they cannot comfortably pay, including people who have health insurance.

What you can do: Before agreeing to a payment plan, ask for the hospital's financial assistance policy, application and list of covered providers, ask for a written eligibility decision, and note the date of your first post-discharge bill.

Based on: IRS section 501(r) guidance reviewed by the IRS in July 2026, the federal regulations as published on eCFR through November 12, 2026, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance last reviewed October 28, 2026, Stanford Health Care's current public policy, and a May 11, 2026 KFF Health News and Minnesota Star Tribune investigation.

## Full editorial

The envelope arrives after the treatment, when a person is trying to get back to work, recover at home or help someone else recover. The amount is more than the household can comfortably pay, and a monthly payment may look like the only practical answer.

Sometimes a payment plan is the right tool. But dividing a bill into installments and deciding whether a patient qualifies for free or discounted care are different decisions, and a hospital that offers the first has not necessarily made the second. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance, last reviewed October 28, 2026, puts the order plainly: make sure you owe the amount, check the bill for errors and see whether you qualify for financial assistance or charity care; if you still owe part of the bill, ask about payment plans or other options. [6]

### Assistance is a condition of a federal tax status

Federal rules for hospitals recognized under section 501(c)(3) include a written financial assistance policy. The IRS explains that section 501(r) applies facility by facility, and that failing to meet its requirements can put a hospital organization's exemption at risk. This is not a claim that every hospital is a nonprofit or that every patient receives the same discount. To find out whether your hospital is covered, ask its billing office whether the hospital facility is a 501(c)(3) tax-exempt organization. Hospitals that are not 501(c)(3) organizations, such as for-profit hospitals, are outside these rules, though state law or their own policies may offer help. In this article, a covered hospital means one that must follow these rules. [1]

The policy has to say who is eligible and whether help means free or discounted care, how charges are calculated and how to apply. It must also list any providers other than the hospital that deliver emergency or medically necessary care in the facility, and say which of them the policy covers. [2] That list matters because one hospital stay can produce several bills. Stanford Health Care's policy is one example: it states that services from a physician who is not on its covered-provider list are not covered by the policy. [9] An approval from the hospital therefore should not be assumed to erase every separately billed professional charge, and a bill from an uncovered provider is not proof that the hospital's decision meant nothing.

The public has a stake because these are conditions of a federal tax status. Under the regulation, a hospital has established its policy only if an authorized body of the hospital facility adopted it and the facility implemented it, and implementing means consistently carrying it out. [3]

### Having insurance does not settle the question

Some people assume assistance is only for the uninsured. Stanford's public page describes help for uninsured patients and for insured patients with high medical costs. For insured patients it requires family income at or below 400 percent of the federal poverty guidelines (for a household of four, Stanford's table shows $132,000) plus annual out-of-pocket costs at Stanford above a set share of income, which the policy defines as 10 percent. The policy document shows a March 2025 approval date. [4][9] Those are one California system's terms, not a national standard or a promise that any applicant will qualify.

Eligibility rules differ sharply elsewhere. A KFF Health News and Minnesota Star Tribune investigation published May 11, 2026 found that some Minnesota hospitals give free care to households earning up to $47,000 a year while others cap it near $15,000, and that many require detailed documents such as bank statements, retirement accounts and mortgage papers. The reporters wrote that similar variation exists nationwide, citing KFF Health News's earlier reporting and other researchers. [7] That is regional reporting, not a measurement of every state or of Stanford. It does explain why a patient should read the actual criteria at their own hospital instead of assuming the answer from an income level or an insurance card.

### Information should arrive before the decision

The regulation requires a covered hospital to make the policy, the application form and a plain-language summary widely available on a website, to provide paper copies on request without charge by mail and in public places including the emergency room and admissions area, and to print a conspicuous notice about assistance on billing statements, with a phone number and web address. [3] A separate definition in the regulations says that documents posted online must be viewable without paying a fee, creating an account or providing personally identifiable information. [8] So a bill from the hospital facility itself should point you toward the policy. A bill from a separate physician group or other uncovered provider need not.

Reading the rules is different from applying. A hospital may need personal and financial information to decide an application; the point is that a patient can learn what is required before handing it over.

The IRS says a covered hospital must make reasonable efforts to determine eligibility before taking extraordinary collection actions. Those actions include selling the debt, reporting adverse information to credit agencies, and legal steps such as liens, lawsuits and wage garnishment. [5]

Two clocks matter, and both start with the first billing statement you receive after discharge. The hospital must wait at least 120 days from that statement before starting those actions, and before it does it must send a written notice with a deadline no earlier than 30 days out. You, in turn, have at least 240 days from that statement to apply for assistance. If you send an incomplete application within that period, the hospital must tell you how to complete it and give you a reasonable chance to do so. [5][10]

An application also stops the clock on collections. If a covered hospital receives your application during the application period, it must not start, or take further action on, extraordinary collection actions until it has decided whether you are eligible or, if your application is incomplete, until you fail to answer its request for the missing items within a reasonable time. That is the main reason to apply early. [10] These are federal minimums for covered facilities, and a hospital's own policy or state law may offer more. Applying does not freeze every ordinary bill or collection letter, and a court deadline keeps running independently. If legal papers arrive, do not wait for the hospital's decision.

A fair objection is that hospitals need revenue, verification takes staff time and not everyone who asks will qualify. Hospital officials quoted in the Minnesota investigation said it is unfair to expect them to solve an affordability problem when many facilities are financially strained, and the Minnesota Hospital Association said no amount of charity care from hospitals will ever fully meet the need. [7] In our view, clear instructions also serve the hospital: they can cut incomplete applications and keep a request for help separate from a dispute over whether a service was billed correctly.

### Who holds the power

Patients who qualify benefit when the amount they owe reflects the policy. Hospital boards and billing departments hold the authority to make that happen within the rules. Patients and caregivers carry the work of applying and responding, often during recovery. Our view, as a recommendation rather than a finding, is that hospitals should put the assistance route beside payment instructions, explain a denial against the actual criteria, and publish privacy-protected counts of applications, decisions and relief applied.

## Pull Up A Chair

Start with the hospital's financial assistance office or billing page. Ask for the current policy, the plain-language summary, the application and the list of covered providers. Ask which of your bills the application would cover, what documents are required, what the submission date is and how you will receive a written decision. Keep copies of everything you send.

Find the date of the first bill you received after you left the hospital, and count 240 days from it. That is the federal minimum window for a covered hospital to accept your application. Write both dates down, and apply well before the second.

Check the bill itself. CFPB guidance says to confirm that you owe the amount, ask for an itemized list if something looks wrong, and review how insurance was applied. Check a billing error and an assistance decision separately. [6]

If the answer is a payment plan, ask whether assistance eligibility has been assessed first and what balance would remain if you are approved. The CFPB suggests asking for an interest-free plan and warns that using a credit card or medical credit card may carry high interest and reduce your room to negotiate. [6] If a lawsuit, wage garnishment notice or other legal paper arrives, seek qualified legal help promptly while you pursue the hospital's process.

## Seat Summary

A nonprofit hospital's written assistance policy is a condition of its federal tax status, and covered hospitals must follow minimum steps before extraordinary collection actions. What a patient qualifies for depends on the hospital's published criteria, the provider that sent each bill and the patient's documents. A 2026 Minnesota investigation found those criteria vary widely there, and its reporters say similar variation exists nationwide, so the policy in front of you decides.

## Final Thought

Before you commit a strained budget to a monthly payment, get the hospital's written answer on assistance. If it says yes, the balance you are paying down may be smaller than the one on the bill.
