## Website editorial

Here is how the publisher describes the opening of Tiffany Hammond's new picture book.

It is a rainy day, which makes it a good day for a museum. Aidan is excited to learn. But rain brings long lines and loud rooms, and the noise and the waiting become too much. His brain gets fuzzy. He grunts, shouts and slaps his chest. That is how he tells the world what he is feeling.

The people around him don't understand. So they stare at him and whisper, and Aidan starts to feel that he does not belong there.

That is the opening of *How Do You Spell Belong?*, a new picture book by Tiffany Hammond, illustrated by Ken Daley, coming from Bloomsbury Children's Books on January 26, 2027. According to the publisher, Aidan has the help of his father and a new friend, and with them he is able to be himself.

The title asks a question. The story asks a bigger one: who has to change so a child can belong?

### Who Tiffany Hammond is

Hammond is an autistic storyteller from Texas raising two autistic sons. She is the voice behind Fidgets and Fries, a social media platform about autism, disability and family. Her oldest son, Aidan, is nonspeaking. He is the real boy behind both of her books.

Her first picture book, *A Day with No Words* (2023), shows a Black mother and her nonspeaking son going through a day using a tablet to talk to each other and the world. It became a #1 New York Times bestseller. Bloomsbury reissued it in November 2025.

Her official biography says she began documenting her family's life "not as inspiration, but as truth." The world often missed Aidan's voice because he didn't speak, but she saw it everywhere: in how he moved, in his eyes, in his laughter, in his silence. Today Aidan is 19. He still uses his iPad to communicate, and he is learning to spell. His mother calls it a long journey, maybe years.

The new book follows that journey. Hammond describes it as the story of a nonspeaking boy and his father spending a day at a museum, "spelling, connecting, and reminding each other they belong."

### Why this book matters now

Autism is not rare. In the CDC's 2025 report using 2022 records, about 1 in 31 eight-year-olds across 16 U.S. surveillance sites had been identified with autism.

The report also showed how the picture has changed. Across those sites, Black, Hispanic and Asian or Pacific Islander children were more likely than white children to be identified, continuing a pattern first seen in 2020. Among Black children it was about 1 in 27; among white children, about 1 in 36. The CDC says the change is consistent with more access to identification services for groups that were underserved, after years in which children of color were identified less often and later.

Many of those children will be like Aidan. Researchers estimate that about 25 to 30 percent of autistic children do not develop functional speech and remain minimally verbal past age 5. Definitions vary from study to study. The point does not.

A child who does not speak is not a child with nothing to say.

Still, a Black autistic boy who communicates without speech has rarely been the hero of a children's book. Hammond's work puts him there. A child who sees himself on the page learns he is a story worth telling. A child who does not usually see a boy like Aidan learns something just as important: what she is seeing in public may be communication, not misbehavior.

### What society usually does, and what it could do

Most people have seen this happen. A child melts down in a store, on a train, in a museum line. The adults around him decide quickly what kind of child he is and what kind of parent he has. They stare. They whisper. Sometimes they say something.

For an autistic child, that moment is often sensory overload: too much noise, light, waiting or crowd, all at once. The grunting or chest-slapping that looks alarming from across the room may be the clearest way he has to say "this is too much."

The book's title answers the question of who should change. Aidan doesn't need to change to belong. The room needs to make space for him. That can mean:
- a quieter corner;
- a shorter line;
- a sensory-friendly hour;
- a stranger who looks away instead of staring;
- a parent who doesn't have to apologize for her son existing in public.

Some museums, theaters and stores now offer sensory-friendly hours or quiet spaces. Before a visit, check the venue's current access information and booking requirements. Where nothing like that exists, it is worth asking.

TMS has written before about the pressure on autistic people to hide who they are to make others comfortable ("You Don't Look Autistic"). Hammond's book comes at the same truth from the other side, through a child who cannot hide it and should never have to.

## Pull Up A Chair

- **Preorder from a local or independent bookshop,** or through Bookshop.org, which supports independent stores.
- **Ask your public library or your child's school library to order it.** Libraries buy books patrons request. One request matters.
- **The next time a child is overwhelmed in public, don't stare.** Look away, give space, and if it feels right, ask the parent quietly whether they need anything.
- **Ask places you visit whether they offer sensory-friendly hours or a quiet room.** If they don't, ask them to consider it.
- **In Germany,** ask your bookshop to order the English edition, or request it through your city library's acquisition suggestion form (Anschaffungsvorschlag).
