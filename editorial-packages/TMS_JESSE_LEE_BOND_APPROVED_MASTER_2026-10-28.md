# His Death Was Called an Accident. His Great-Granddaughter Became a Supreme Court Justice.

**Topics:** Race & Equal Humanity · Justice & Law · Government Accountability
**By Jansen | October 29, 2026**

### The Short Version · 20-second read

**What happened?** In 1939, Jesse Lee Bond, a 20-year-old Black farmer in Arlington, Tennessee, asked a white store owner for a receipt. He was lynched that day. The coroner wrote that he accidentally drowned, and the two men tried for his murder were acquitted. In 2023, his great-granddaughter, Kyra Harris Bolden, became the first Black woman on the Michigan Supreme Court.

**Who it affects:** Bond's family, who have spent more than 80 years fighting to get the truth on the record, and every family whose loss was officially written down as something it wasn't.

**What you can do:** Read Justice Bolden's own account, support the Lynching Sites Project of Memphis, which works with Bond's family, and follow the National Park Service study of Memphis-area lynching sites, which includes Bond's and is still underway.

*Based on: Kyra Harris Bolden's 2021 published account, Associated Press and WMC Action News 5 reporting, the Equal Justice Initiative, the Lynching Sites Project of Memphis, and the National Park Service.*

**Seat Affected:** Equal protection of the law, and a truthful public record
**Seat Status:** Seat Denied

### Read the full story

**A receipt was a threat to the ledger**

On April 28, 1939, Jesse Lee Bond went to the S.Y. Wilson Store in Arlington, Tennessee, to buy supplies for planting. He was 20 years old and farmed cotton. Like many Black farmers in the South, he bought on credit, and the store's owner, Charles Robert Wilson, kept a ledger of every purchase, every loan and the interest each customer owed.

That ledger is the key to this story. A Black farmer of that era generally could not see or challenge what a white merchant said he owed. A family member told Memphis reporters that Bond's total didn't match what the store had written down, and that back then you couldn't question it: whatever the ledger said, you owed.

Bond asked the cashier, the owner's son Sam Wilson, for a receipt. They argued, and Sam Wilson handed it over reluctantly. When his father heard about it, he sent Sam to bring Bond and the goods back to the store.

Bond returned with his aunt, Luanna Bond. According to the account his great-granddaughter later published, Charles Robert Wilson and his friend William "Bud" Johnson opened fire inside the store. Bond ran, and other men helped catch him. They brought him back to the town square and shot him to death in front of the store, in daylight. His body was castrated, dragged behind a truck to the river and staked to the riverbed.

Five days later, local authorities "found" him. The coroner's death certificate says he "fell into the Hatchie River and was accidentally drowned."

**The second injury came from the government**

The mob killed Jesse Lee Bond. What happened next was done by public officials acting with public authority.

The coroner signed a document that turned a public killing into an accident. Two men were tried for murder and quickly acquitted; *The Commercial Appeal* reported the outcome on January 23, 1940. No one was ever convicted. Bond was buried at Grays Creek Church, where a large crowd came, many from neighboring counties to protest what the white authorities had done. His grave has no stone to this day.

**Washington had a chance to act, and chose not to**

One year before Bond was killed, a federal anti-lynching bill reached the floor of the U.S. Senate. Southern senators held a month-long filibuster against it in early 1938, and its sponsors set it aside so the Senate could pass a relief appropriation. Bills like it died again and again for decades. Congress did not make lynching a federal crime until the Emmett Till Antilynching Act in 2022, eighty-three years after Bond's death.

That doesn't mean federal law offered nothing in 1939. Older civil rights statutes from Reconstruction were still on the books and could sometimes reach officials who abused their power. But their reach was narrow, especially against private mobs, and they were rarely enforced in cases like this one. Congress knew that and declined to close the gap. Whether federal prosecutors could have brought a case against anyone involved in Bond's killing is a separate question this review can't answer. The choice in the Senate is on the record either way.

**The family kept the true record**

The official record said drowning. The family said otherwise and never stopped saying it.

Bond's brother, Charlie Morris, spent most of his life pushing to bring the killing into the open; his son, Dr. Ronald Morris, says his father fought for justice for more than 83 years. The family helped propose a Tennessee cold-case bill to investigate unsolved civil-rights-era killings. With the Lynching Sites Project of Memphis, they have held vigils at the site and worked toward a historical marker. An attempt to start a conversation with descendants of the known perpetrators went nowhere.

In February 2026, Dr. Morris stood in Arlington and told reporters that justice wasn't done there and still isn't. That same month, the National Park Service opened public comment on a study Congress ordered in its 2023 federal spending law. The study covers eight lynching sites within about 100 miles of Memphis, including Bond's, involving the killings of 16 Black men between 1868 and 1940, and will tell Congress whether any of them should become part of the National Park System. The comment period closed on April 3, 2026, and the study is still underway.

That study is real federal recognition, and it matters. But it came 87 years after the killing, and only after the family and local volunteers had spent decades keeping the truth alive themselves. The local and state institutions that wrote "accident" on Bond's death certificate have not, as far as this review found, corrected it.

**Who benefited, and who paid**

The record shows who walked away. The two men tried for murder left court without a conviction, and no one else was ever charged. Charles Robert Wilson's ledger went on unchallenged. Bond's cousin told reporters that in 1939 a Black customer could not question what the store said he owed. A killing in the town square, in daylight, over a receipt, sent that message to every other customer more clearly than any rule could. That last point is our inference from the timing and the family's account, not a documented statement of anyone's motive, but it is the plain effect of what happened. The coroner's finding meant the official record named no killer at all. In Washington, the senators who blocked the 1938 bill kept lynching a matter for local officials like the ones who handled Bond's case.

The costs fell on the other side. Bond lost his life at 20. His aunt, Luanna Bond, was with him when the shooting began. His daughter, Justice Bolden's grandmother, grew up without her father, and Bolden has written about the economic toll that followed the family for generations. His brother spent more than eight decades trying to get the truth acknowledged. None of those losses has been compensated, and the false cause of death, as far as this review found, still stands.

**Then his great-granddaughter became a justice**

Kyra Harris Bolden learned her great-grandfather's story from a relative while she was in college, and it changed how she understood the justice system. She went to law school, practiced law and served in the Michigan House of Representatives.

In November 2021, while still a state legislator, she published her family's account in Bridge Michigan, writing against a bill that would have limited how race and history could be taught in Michigan schools. She described what the killing cost her family: the trauma, a grandmother who grew up without her father, and an economic toll that lasted for generations.

On January 1, 2023, Bolden was sworn in as the first Black woman ever to serve on the Michigan Supreme Court, and its youngest member. In November 2024, Michigan voters elected her to stay. She earned that seat through her own work and record. Her great-grandfather's death shaped why she chose the law, not whether she deserved to be there.

**Representation is not restitution**

It is tempting to end the story here, with a family that went from a lynching to the state's highest court. That journey is real, and it should be celebrated.

But look at what it did not change. Bond's death certificate still records an accident. The acquittals stand. The men who killed him faced no consequence. His grave is still unmarked.

A descendant reaching the bench is progress for her, for Michigan and for everyone who had never seen someone like her there. It is not repair for Jesse Lee Bond. Those are two different debts, and paying the first doesn't settle the second.

**What you can do**

- **Read the family's account in their own words.** Justice Bolden's 2021 essay is short and direct. Start there, not with a viral repost.
- **Support the Lynching Sites Project of Memphis.** They work directly with Bond's family on the marker and on remembrance.
- **Follow the National Park Service study.** Public comment closed in April 2026, but the study is still in progress and its findings will go to Congress. The project page posts updates.
- **Ask your own county.** If your area has a documented lynching, ask the county or state what its official records say about the cause of death, and whether anyone has ever corrected them.

**The missing seat**

There are two seats in this story. One belongs to Kyra Harris Bolden, on the Michigan Supreme Court. The other belonged to a 20-year-old farmer who asked to see the numbers, was killed for it, and was recorded as having drowned.

One seat was made. The other is still missing.

### How we checked this

- **Documented:** The date and place, the receipt dispute, the coroner's accidental-drowning finding, the acquittal of two men, Bolden's family relationship, and her 2023 appointment and 2024 election, confirmed by the family's published account and by independent reporting. The National Park Service study and its inclusion of Bond's case are confirmed by the agency itself.
- **From the family's account:** The names of the shooters, Luanna Bond's presence, and the details of the mutilation come from Justice Bolden's 2021 essay. AP and WMC reporting is consistent with it.
- **Where sources differ:** Bolden's account and the death certificate name the Hatchie River; AP, EJI and WMC name the Loosahatchie. Northeastern University's Civil Rights and Restorative Justice archive describes the body being found in a drainage canal and the accused being released by a judge; most sources, including the 1940 newspaper report, describe an acquittal. We use "acquitted" and name the river as the death certificate does.
- **Federal law:** Older federal civil rights statutes existed in 1939. We did not determine whether any could have applied to the people involved in Bond's killing.
- **Not established:** We did not review the original coroner's file or trial record. We found no record that the death certificate was ever corrected, which is not proof that it wasn't. We could not confirm the current status of the Tennessee cold-case bill.
- **Not used:** A 2026 statement by Rep. Steve Cohen circulated with this story. The statement we located is his April 2026 public comment to the National Park Service study; it does not document the trial records, so it is not cited for the killing itself.
- No new interviews were conducted.
