## The Short Version

What happened: A Government Accountability Office (GAO) report, published January 21 and publicly released February 20, 2026, found that job centers it visited had trouble arranging sign-language interpreters and making outside training accessible. It also found that the Labor Department does not routinely analyze its monitoring reports together. As of November 15, all three GAO recommendations remain open.

Who it affects: People with disabilities who use publicly funded job centers and training under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA). GAO visited twelve centers in four places, so the findings do not measure how often problems happen nationwide.

What you can do: Before an appointment or training, describe the access you need, ask in writing who will arrange it, and keep a record. If it is not resolved, ask for the program's Equal Opportunity Officer and written complaint procedure.

Based on: GAO-26-107473 and its July 2026 agency-response update, the Labor Department's Civil Rights Center guidance, 29 CFR 38.35, and a June 2026 Labor Department final rule.

## Full editorial

A job center can have a screen reader, a wheelchair-accessible workstation and an interpreter on call, and still be unusable on the day a particular person walks in. That is the gap the Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congress's nonpartisan auditor, documented in a report on Labor Department workforce programs.

GAO visited twelve job centers in four places: Arkansas (four, in person), the District of Columbia (one, in person), Oregon (four, virtually) and Virginia (three, in person). It also held discussion groups with 18 local workforce board directors from ten other states. GAO chose the sites to vary geography, population and the share of participants with disabilities. The visits show what can go wrong. They do not show how often it does.

What went wrong in those places was specific. One site reported waits of up to three days for a sign-language interpreter, which delays services. Another lost its interpreter video equipment during building renovations. GAO describes a deaf participant who could not join a training because no interpreter was available during the required hours, and a participant with a physical disability who could not attend truck-driver training because modifying a vehicle was too expensive. Some outside training providers lacked accessible restrooms or elevators, and some had strict attendance rules that did not allow for medical appointments. At one center the designated accessible workstation was out of service during renovations; at another, staff could not operate the screen reader during a demonstration.

GAO also found real effort. The centers offered screen readers and magnifiers, wheelchair-accessible workstations, video interpreting and communication devices. The problem it describes is less a lack of equipment than a service that depends on everything working at once.

The broader numbers give the stakes. About 7 percent of participants in the programs GAO reviewed, for July 1, 2023 to June 30, 2024, reported a disability, and those participants had worse employment outcomes. This article does not claim the barriers above explain that gap.

## Inspecting a center is only the first step

The Labor Department does inspect. Its Employment and Training Administration monitoring reports from 2022 through 2024 identified accessibility problems, including a job center that did not meet physical access requirements and a state policy on training providers. What the department lacked, GAO found, was a written procedure for routinely analyzing those reports together. Staff had reviewed reports for program years 2021 to 2023 by hand and found common physical accessibility problems, but nothing required the comparison to happen.

That matters because one inspection can find a local problem, while many inspections together can show whether the same obstacle keeps appearing. Without the second step, an agency can treat a series of similar barriers as separate incidents. GAO also found that officials in several local areas did not know about the department's accessibility guidance or did not use it, and that the department had not evaluated either. GAO identified this as an oversight risk. It did not find that every center failed, and nothing here suggests officials meant to exclude anyone.

A fair manager can say that no single accommodation works for everyone, and that is true. A workstation that suits a wheelchair user does nothing for an interpreter request, and an interpreter does not make an inaccessible course usable. Staff need room to respond to the individual. But that judgment works better when someone is clearly responsible for the arrangement and the person knows where to go if the first answer fails.

## What Labor has agreed to, and what it has not

GAO made three recommendations, and its report page, checked November 15, lists all three as open. The page shows no update date; the latest entries are the department's July 2026 responses.

The department agreed to the second: a written procedure for routinely analyzing state monitoring reports, including the associated local reports. It says it will add the procedure to its next update of an employment and training order on grants management, expected by the end of fiscal year 2027, which is September 30, 2027. In the agency comments printed with the report, the same plan carried the end of fiscal year 2026, so the stated date appears to have moved by a year. It agreed to the third as well, evaluating how well state and local agencies know and use its guidance, including by increasing how it distributes resources.

It disagreed with the first, which asks for more complete data on participants' disability types. GAO found that 43 percent of participants who reported a disability did not report the type, and that completeness varied widely by state. In July the department said it could take some steps but could not commit to raising completeness, because people may choose to withhold sensitive information. In the report it added that it does not require disclosure and worried that more outreach in states with complete data could pressure people. GAO maintains the recommendation is warranted, and says it points at states with less complete data. It also says the department can encourage better data without requiring disclosure, for example by helping states understand what their privacy rules allow, or by intake forms that prompt for an answer while offering "prefer not to disclose."

Both concerns are reasonable. Health information is sensitive, and no one should be pushed to name a diagnosis to get help. But without some reliable picture, the department cannot easily see where access breaks down.

## One rule change worth knowing

On June 23, 2026, the Labor Department published a final rule rescinding 29 CFR 38.40, which had required WIOA Title I recipients to take reasonable affirmative outreach steps toward groups that included people with disabilities. The department said Section 188 does not give it authority to mandate that outreach and that the requirement raised equal protection concerns. Recipients may still do outreach voluntarily, and the rule says the nondiscrimination standards for disability are unchanged. It took effect 30 days after publication. The change concerns outreach, not accommodations inside a center, so it does not answer or explain what GAO found. It does mean one older tool for reaching disabled job seekers is no longer required.

## The person should not become the access department

The missing seat belongs to the disabled job seeker whose qualifications cannot be judged until the service itself is usable. It also belongs to people who tried to enter a program and gave up before they became a counted participant. No source here measures that second group, so this article makes no claim about its size.

State and local workforce agencies, center operators and training providers run the services. The Labor Department sets guidance and monitors them. The job seeker controls much less and carries the delay, the repeated explanation and the risk of losing an opportunity. When access works, employers meet more qualified applicants and the public gets employment services that do what they were funded to do.

## Pull Up A Chair

If you need a service, contact the center before your appointment where you can. Say what you need to take part, such as an interpreter, a screen reader, materials in another format or a different time. You do not need to share your medical history to make that request. Ask for a written reply, ask who is responsible for arranging it, and note the date you asked.

If a referral sends you to a training provider, ask before you enroll who checks that the training is accessible, and what happens if the arrangement fails. Training outside the center is where GAO heard some of the hardest problems.

If the problem is not resolved, ask for the program's Equal Opportunity Officer and its written complaint procedure. Under the Labor Department's rules, a complaint can be filed with the recipient's Equal Opportunity Officer or directly with the department's Civil Rights Center. The Center's guidance says complaints must be filed within 180 calendar days of the alleged discrimination, and only its Director can extend that deadline for good cause. A recipient's procedure should lead to a written Notice of Final Action within 90 days, and a person who is dissatisfied can then go to the Civil Rights Center within 30 days. These deadlines are why a dated record helps. A request for access is not a promise that every arrangement will be approved, and this is general information, not legal advice.

If you want to look beyond your own case, ask your local workforce board how it tracks unresolved access requests. A simple record of the barrier, the response and the time taken, with no identifying details, would tell the public more than a general assurance that accessibility matters.

## Seat Summary

Job centers can be open and still hard to use. GAO documented interpreter delays and inaccessible outside training at the sites it visited, and found the Labor Department does not routinely compare its own monitoring reports. All three recommendations remain open, with the written procedure the department agreed to expected by the end of fiscal year 2027. Ask for the access you need before you go, put it in writing, and keep the dates.

## Final Thought

Ask your local center two things this month: who arranges access when I request it, and where do I go if that fails. If it cannot answer both in plain words, that is the part of the service still unfinished.
