## The Short Version

What happened: A Government Accountability Office (GAO) report, published January 21 and publicly released February 20, 2026, found that job centers it visited had trouble arranging sign language interpreters and making outside training accessible. It also found that the Labor Department does not routinely analyze its monitoring reports together. As of the report page's July 2026 status entries, all three GAO recommendations were open.

Who it affects: People with disabilities who use job centers and training funded under the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA), the federal law behind the public job-center system. GAO visited twelve centers in four places, so the findings do not measure how often problems happen nationwide.

What you can do: Before an appointment or training, describe the access you need, ask in writing who will arrange it, and keep a dated record. If it is not resolved, ask for the program's Equal Opportunity Officer, the person each program must name to take discrimination complaints, and for its written complaint procedure. The filing clocks are short.

Based on: GAO-26-107473, the Labor Department's Civil Rights Center guidance, 29 CFR Part 38, and a June 2026 Labor Department final rule.

## Full editorial

A job center can have a screen reader, a wheelchair-accessible workstation and an interpreter on call, and still be unusable on the day a particular person walks in. That is the gap the Government Accountability Office (GAO), Congress's nonpartisan auditor, documented in a report on Labor Department workforce programs.

GAO visited twelve job centers in four places: Arkansas (four, in person), the District of Columbia (one, in person), Oregon (four, virtually) and Virginia (three, in person). It also held discussion groups with 18 local workforce board officials from ten other states. GAO chose the sites to vary geography, population and the share of participants with disabilities. The visits show what can go wrong. They do not show how often it does.

What went wrong was specific, and most of it comes from local officials GAO interviewed. Officials at three job centers and a disability organization said centers have limited access to sign language interpreters, which can delay services. One local official said interpreter technology was no longer available at the center because of building renovations. Another said participants had to wait up to three days for an interpreter, and that the center was trying to hire staff who sign. Local officials at a different center told GAO about a deaf participant who could not join a training because no interpreter was available for the hours it required. Others described a participant with a physical disability who could not attend truck-driver training because modifying a vehicle was too expensive.

Training outside the center drew similar accounts. Local workforce officials told GAO that some training providers lack accessible restrooms, and one said a provider's second-floor classes had no elevator, which would shut out wheelchair users. Officials at one job center said some programs have strict attendance rules that do not allow for medical appointments. GAO also saw problems itself. At one center, the workstation designated as accessible was not running; the local official said a renovation was the cause. At another, staff could not operate the screen reader during a demonstration.

GAO also found real effort. Officials described screen readers and magnifiers, accessible workstations, video interpreting and communication devices, and six of nine participants with disabilities GAO spoke with said they received accommodations. The problem GAO describes is less a lack of equipment than a service that depends on everything working at once.

The broader numbers give the stakes. About 7 percent of participants in the programs GAO reviewed, for July 1, 2023 to June 30, 2024, reported a disability, and those participants had worse employment outcomes. This article does not claim the barriers above explain that gap.

## Inspecting a center is only the first step

The Labor Department does inspect. Its Employment and Training Administration (ETA), the agency that oversees these programs, reviewed about 20 percent of its grants, covering 18 to 20 states, in fiscal year 2024, and aims to review each state once every three years. States, in turn, monitor their local agencies and centers. ETA's reports from 2022 through 2024 identified accessibility problems, including a job center that did not meet physical access requirements and a state policy on training providers.

What the department lacked, GAO found, was a written procedure for routinely analyzing those reports together. Staff had reviewed reports for program years 2021 to 2023 by hand and found common physical accessibility problems, but nothing required the comparison to happen. One inspection can find a local problem; many together can show whether the same obstacle keeps appearing. Without the second step, a series of similar barriers can look like separate incidents.

Guidance is a second gap. Of the nine local areas GAO visited, seven said they knew the department's accessibility guidance and six had used it. But officials in five areas said they were not aware of or had not used its promising practices guide, a document of recommended approaches for job center staff. As of January 2026, the department had also taken that guide off its website to review it against the administration's priorities, with a return it had estimated for summer 2025, and had not put it back. The department does not evaluate how widely any of its accessibility guidance is known or used.

GAO identified this as an oversight risk. It did not find that every center failed, and nothing here suggests officials meant to exclude anyone. A fair manager can say that no single accommodation works for everyone, and that is true: a workstation that suits a wheelchair user does nothing for an interpreter request. Staff need room to respond to the individual. That judgment works better when someone is clearly responsible for the arrangement and the person knows where to go if the first answer fails.

## What Labor has agreed to, and what it has not

GAO made three recommendations. As of the report page's July 2026 status entries, all three were open.

The department agreed to the second: a written procedure for routinely analyzing state monitoring reports, including the associated local reports. It said it would add the procedure to its next update of an employment and training order on grants management policy. In the agency comments printed with the report, that update was scheduled for the end of fiscal year 2026. The July 2026 response gives the end of fiscal year 2027, September 30, 2027, so the date appears to have moved by a year. The department also agreed to the third, assessing how well state and local agencies know and use its guidance and increasing how it distributes resources.

It disagreed with the first, which asks for more complete data on participants' disability types. GAO found that about 80,000 participants, 43 percent of those who reported a disability, did not report the type, and that completeness ranged from 2 percent to nearly 100 percent by state. The department said it does not require participants to disclose their disability type, that disclosure is voluntary, and that more technical assistance in states with nearly complete data could lead states to pressure people who would rather not answer. GAO replied that it does not propose requiring disclosure, that its recommendation points to states where data are less complete, and that the department could help states understand what their privacy rules allow. It added that, according to DOL officials, the department does not plan to target assistance to those states.

Both concerns are reasonable. Health information is sensitive, and no one should be pushed to name a diagnosis to get help. But without some reliable picture, the department cannot easily see where access breaks down.

## One rule change worth knowing

On June 23, 2026, the Labor Department published a final rule (91 Federal Register 37309, effective July 24, 2026) rescinding 29 CFR 38.40, which had required WIOA Title I recipients to take reasonable steps to reach out to groups that included people with disabilities. The department said Section 188 of WIOA, the law's nondiscrimination provision, does not give it authority to mandate that outreach, and that outreach aimed at racial and ethnic groups raised equal protection concerns. It said nondiscrimination and outreach are distinct, and that recipients can comply with Section 188 without the old outreach steps. Recipients may still do outreach voluntarily.

The department received 84 comments on the proposal. Eighty-three opposed rescission, including national and local disability rights organizations. The department also said that since 2016 it had not started a single enforcement action over a recipient's failure to do the outreach, so it did not expect the change to disrupt services.

The change concerns outreach, not accommodations inside a center, so it does not explain what GAO found. It does mean one older tool for reaching disabled job seekers is no longer required.

## The person should not become the access department

The missing seat belongs to the disabled job seeker whose qualifications cannot be judged until the service itself is usable. It also belongs to people who tried to enter a program and gave up before they became a counted participant. No source here measures that second group, so this article makes no claim about its size.

State and local workforce agencies, center operators and training providers run the services. The Labor Department sets guidance and monitors them. The job seeker controls much less and carries the delay, the repeated explanation and the risk of losing an opportunity. When access works, employers meet more qualified applicants and the public gets employment services that do what they were funded to do.

## Pull Up A Chair

If you need a service, contact the center before your appointment where you can. Say what you need to take part, such as an interpreter, a screen reader, materials in another format or a different time. According to Labor Department officials, these programs do not require participants to disclose a disability, and job center officials told GAO they can arrange accommodations more readily once they know about a need. What you share is your choice. Ask for a written reply, ask who is responsible for arranging it, and note the date you asked.

If a referral sends you to a training provider, ask before you enroll who checks that the training is accessible, and what happens if the arrangement fails. Training outside the center is where local officials described some of the hardest problems.

If the problem is not resolved, ask for the program's Equal Opportunity Officer and its written complaint procedure. A complaint can go to the recipient's Equal Opportunity Officer or directly to the Labor Department's Civil Rights Center, the office that handles discrimination complaints about these programs. Generally it must be filed within 180 days of the alleged discrimination, and only the Center's Director can extend that deadline, for good cause.

Two further clocks catch people. A recipient's procedure must lead to a written Notice of Final Action, its decision on the complaint, within 90 days of filing. If you disagree with the decision, you have 30 days from the notice to take the complaint to the Civil Rights Center. If the 90 days pass with no decision, you have 30 days after that, 120 days from the original filing, to go to the Center. Waiting on the local process can therefore use up time you need, which is why a dated record helps. A request for access is not a promise that every arrangement will be approved, and this is general information, not legal advice.

If you want to look beyond your own case, ask your local workforce board how it tracks unresolved access requests. A simple record of the barrier, the response and the time taken, with no identifying details, would tell the public more than a general assurance that accessibility matters.

## Seat Summary

Local officials told GAO about interpreter delays and inaccessible outside training at the sites it visited, and GAO found the Labor Department does not routinely compare its own monitoring reports. The procedure the department agreed to write is now dated for September 30, 2027. Until then, the dated request is the reader's best tool.

## Final Thought

Ask your local center two things this month: who arranges access when I request it, and where do I go if that fails. An answer you can write down is the service working.

## What this article does and does not claim

Barriers are attributed to local officials GAO interviewed, or to GAO's own observation where it says so. Open recommendations are not a finding that any center broke the law. GAO's twelve site visits cannot be generalized, so no national rate is claimed. The disclosure disagreement is presented from both sides. No exclusion count, hiring loss or racial disparity is asserted. Advice to readers is a practical request for access, not a promise of approval.
