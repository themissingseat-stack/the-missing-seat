## Full editorial

If a public program helps someone get a job, keeping the support connected should be part of the job. The person should not have to stop and manage a dispute between agencies while trying to learn a shift, understand a supervisor or build confidence at work. Getting hired is an important beginning. It is a poor place for the government to lose track of who is responsible next.

That matters especially when a worker needs support to participate. We should not confuse needing help with being unable to contribute. Nor should we treat someone’s willingness to work as permission to make them prove their patience with every office involved. A system designed to support employment should be organized around the person’s working life, including the transitions that its own rules create.

On September 23, 2026, the Government Accountability Office released a review of disability employment funding in Georgia, Pennsylvania and Washington. Its selected sample cannot establish national prevalence. Vocational Rehabilitation and Medicaid home- and community-based services were the main federal funding sources examined. Providers reported service gaps linked to administrative burden or confusion. One Pennsylvania provider described uncertainty over a rehabilitation closure letter delaying Medicaid-funded support. These are reported experiences, not findings that every transition fails. The agencies also described coordination efforts. [1]

The issue deserves attention without making the evidence larger than it is. We do not need to claim that every state behaves the same way, that every counselor is careless or that every interrupted service caused someone to lose a job. We need to recognize the practical problem the report raises: support can be available through public programs while the connection between those programs remains difficult to use.

The Department of Labor identifies interagency coordination as part of improving employment opportunities. Its resources include ways to use separate funding streams together and guidance for agreements between agencies. The aim of competitive integrated employment includes comparable pay and benefits, interaction with colleagues without disabilities, and opportunities to advance. [2] Those are substantial goals. They deserve an administrative process that helps people reach them.

This is where a phrase like “agency coordination” needs translating. For the worker, it should mean knowing whether the coach will be there, whom to call when something changes, and whether an approval has actually been completed. It should mean that the office ending its part of the support has checked that the next office is ready. A document sent is not the same thing as a service received.

Consider the risk, rather than an invented account of a particular person. A worker may need help adjusting to a new task. If that help stops during a funding transition, a manageable difficulty could become a problem at work. A relative might rearrange their own day to help. A provider might face pressure to continue without clear payment. An employer might misunderstand the absence of support as a change in the worker’s ability. These are possible consequences, not outcomes established for every person in the study. They explain why continuity is worth protecting before a crisis develops.

The missing person in the administrative design is the worker who has to live through the handoff. Their schedule, goals, communication needs and preferences should help determine how it works. Family members and supporters can assist when the worker wants them involved. They should not automatically become the unpaid project managers of a process that public agencies created.

Who holds the power is clearer. Agency leaders control procedures, staffing priorities and escalation routes. Program administrators decide what information moves between offices and how responsibility is recorded. Providers must work within those arrangements. The worker has the least ability to redesign the machinery, yet is the person whose working life may be disrupted when it stalls. Responsibility should follow the authority to prevent the gap.

There is no reason to invent a villain or claim an agency is profiting from a delay. The potential institutional convenience is more ordinary: an office can finish its own task while the unresolved transition becomes somebody else’s problem. If performance is measured only within each office, the person’s experience between offices can be overlooked. That is an accountability risk. It should be tested with records rather than assumed from a frustrating encounter.

The benefits of a reliable transition are also ordinary. A worker can concentrate on work. A provider can plan the support it is authorized to deliver. An employer can make reasonable arrangements with clearer expectations. The public can see whether the programs it funds are producing usable help. None of those benefits requires pretending that all workers need the same amount of support or that support must last forever. The plan should respond to the individual, including continuing needs and changing goals.

Program boundaries still matter. Public money needs eligibility rules, lawful payment and safeguards against paying twice for the same service. “Keep the support connected” is not an instruction to ignore those requirements. It is a demand that agencies build the requirements into a workable transition. Where law or resources prevent continuity, the agency should explain the obstacle plainly and identify who can resolve it. Sending a person back and forth does not make the obstacle disappear.

For an ordinary reader, the principle is familiar. You would not accept two offices saying that each completed its part if the service you needed never arrived. Disability employment support deserves the same standard. It is public spending intended to help a person participate in working life. Taxpayers should be able to ask whether that purpose survives the administrative process, rather than accepting paperwork as proof of delivery.

The immediate action is practical. If you receive this support, ask your counselor, service coordinator and provider for a shared written transition plan before one program closes its part. Request the last date of the current authorization, the intended first date of the next one, any documents still needed, and a named person responsible for resolving a delay. Ask the agencies to explain what happens if the dates do not connect. This is a request for clear planning, not a promise that a particular service must be approved.

The worker should receive that plan in a form they can understand and have a real opportunity to correct it. If you are helping someone, get their permission, follow their preferences and keep private information out of public posts. If support is interrupted, document the dates and the service affected. Ask for a written explanation and the relevant review or complaint route. Different programs have different procedures, so an agency should identify the route that applies instead of offering a general assurance.

The public action belongs with state agency leaders and legislators. Ask the rehabilitation and disability-service agencies to publish aggregate information about transitions: how many were completed without interruption, how long gaps lasted, why they occurred, and which corrective steps worked. Do not ask for workers’ names or medical files. Request a deadline for reporting improvements and ask that workers who use the services help assess whether those improvements are real.

A good handoff is not an impressive slogan. It is a worker knowing that the next step is ready and whom to contact if it is not. If public agencies want credit for helping people work, they should accept responsibility for connecting the support that makes work possible. The person trying to keep a job should not have to hold the government together first.
