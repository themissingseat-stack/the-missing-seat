## Website editorial

Khagisara Karki counted her care work and reached a figure of 18 hours a day. UN Women published her account from Dailekh, Nepal, describing a woman beginning to question why the responsibility was treated as hers. That is her reported experience, not a measured average for women across the country.

The same account describes Hemanti Chaudhary in Belauri saying that greater sharing at home gave her time to use her tailoring skills. These women are not Kathmandu residents in the report, and their experiences are not an evaluation of a city programme. They show why an apparently administrative subject can be personal: care arrangements help determine whose time is available for anything else.

In Kathmandu, UN Women reports working with the municipality since 2024 to map childcare, older-person care and disability services against demand. Its account identifies a gap between services existing and families knowing where or how to reach them. That is a useful place for a city to begin. It makes the route into care part of the service, rather than leaving each household to discover it alone.

## Information is part of access

A list of providers can be a start. A usable route needs more. A family needs to know whether a service fits the person's needs, whether it has space, what it costs, when it operates and how to get there. Someone should be responsible for helping when the first answer does not fit.

Those are questions for assessing the model, not a finding that Kathmandu has failed each one. Mapping can identify problems that deserve a new service. It can also reveal that an existing service needs a clearer entrance. The remedies differ, and understanding that difference can prevent public money being spent on the wrong answer.

There is an ordinary household test here. A parent should be able to follow the published route without already knowing somebody inside the system. A person seeking disability support should not need to guess which office has the right list. An older person should have a way to ask for help that does not depend entirely on a relative's ability to navigate online information.

That last point matters because a well-designed page can still leave the work with the same unpaid person. Information should reduce a burden, not simply describe it more elegantly. A city can test that by asking users to follow the route and recording where they get stuck, with consent and without collecting unnecessary personal details.

## People providing care need a seat too

Nepal's cooperative approach offers another piece of the discussion. In July, the International Labour Organization described a national initiative with the National Cooperative Federation to train facilitators for community care services. The work includes assessing needs and helping cooperatives design services. Training is a capacity-building step; it does not establish that new services are operating or that families have received affordable places.

The idea deserves attention because local participation can inform what a service needs to do. It also deserves scrutiny. Calling work community-based should not mean expecting women to provide another layer of unpaid labour. A service that frees one caregiver's time by exhausting another worker has left part of the problem out of its accounting.

WHO's October 20 policy document places care workers within broader workforce planning. Its published overview identifies education, working conditions, fair wages and social protection as priorities. That is guidance about building a workforce, not an impact evaluation of Kathmandu. It supports a practical question for any proposed expansion: who will do the work, under what conditions and with what support?

Municipal leaders control planning and service arrangements. National authorities and programme partners influence resources and policy. Providers control day-to-day access. Families and care workers carry much of the consequence when those pieces do not connect. Giving them a voice should mean more than inviting them to endorse a finished plan.

## Credit the progress and check the result

There is a fair objection to demanding final results from work still being developed. Mapping, consultation and training take time. A new programme should not have to claim instant transformation to justify a careful first step. Recognition can help useful work continue.

The answer is to describe each stage accurately. Credit a map for identifying services and gaps. Credit training for preparing people. Attribute personal reports of change to the people reporting them. Then establish a way to learn whether the next stage happened. That approach supports promising work without turning an institutional success story into proof of results it has not measured.

An ordinary reader can apply this well beyond Nepal. Ask your municipality for one practical care-service route covering eligibility, price, hours, physical access and a person to contact. Ask how it handles a full service or an unsuccessful referral. Request aggregate information about whether people actually obtained support, alongside the working conditions of those providing it. Families should not have to expose private medical details to make an accountability question legitimate.

At home, shared responsibility can begin with making the work visible and agreeing who takes responsibility for particular tasks. Public services still matter. A household conversation cannot create an affordable place or make a building accessible, and a new service cannot decide how every family shares its time. Both levels deserve attention.

Kathmandu's reported mapping is worth learning from because it starts with a concrete connection between people and services. The next useful story is about the people who can follow that connection and receive care. A city earns the word caring through what residents can actually use, and through how it treats the people doing the work.
