# BACKGROUND DOSSIER — KEISHA LANCE BOTTOMS

Keisha Lance Bottoms is an Atlanta-born attorney and former judge who served two terms on the Atlanta City Council before becoming Atlanta's 60th mayor in January 2018. The City of Atlanta's official biography says she is a graduate of Florida A&M University and Georgia State University College of Law and had also worked as executive director of the Atlanta-Fulton County Recreation Authority. Atlanta records describe her as the only Atlanta mayor to have served in all three branches of city government: as a judge, councilmember and mayor.

Bottoms' family history is part of the significance of her current candidacy. In her 2018 inaugural address, she described her maternal grandparents as grandchildren of formerly enslaved people who came to Atlanta from East Georgia seeking opportunity. The Associated Press reported in October 2026 that Georgia has never elected a governor who was not a white man and that Bottoms would become the first Black woman elected governor in United States history if she wins.

Her mayoral term was unusually turbulent. Atlanta suffered a major ransomware attack shortly after she took office. Her administration later confronted the COVID-19 pandemic, the 2020 protests after George Floyd's murder, the police killing of Rayshard Brooks, a sharp rise in violent crime and a political movement to split Buckhead from Atlanta. Those events remain central to the 2026 campaign.

The record is not one-dimensional. City records credit her administration with creating Atlanta's first fully staffed Office of Equity, Diversity and Inclusion, eliminating cash bail for certain city ordinance offenses, ending the use of the city detention center for ICE detainees, expanding financial transparency through Atlanta's Open Checkbook and pursuing an Office of Inspector General. Her administration also increased public-safety spending and backed plans that eventually developed into Atlanta's public-safety training center.

Her critics focus heavily on public safety and the unrest of 2020. Atlanta Journal-Constitution reporting found that Atlanta homicides rose 59% from 2019 to 2020, compared with a 33% rise among other large-city agencies in the comparison set. The dispute over whether police were told to "stand down" near the burned Wendy's where Rayshard Brooks was killed remains contested: later testimony indicated officers had been instructed to pull back, while former interim Police Chief Rodney Bryant told the AJC there was no stand-down order and that police remained in coordination with Bottoms' administration. Eight-year-old Secoriea Turner was killed near the site on July 4, 2020.

Bottoms did not seek a second mayoral term in 2021. Opponents characterize that as abandoning the city. Bottoms has said she believed that chapter of her life had ended and has rejected the claim that she left because she could not win. She later served as a senior adviser in the Biden White House.

In the May 19, 2026 Democratic gubernatorial primary, Georgia's official audit recorded 608,262 votes for Bottoms out of 1,093,349 Democratic gubernatorial ballots cast in the audited count, enough to win the nomination without a runoff. She now faces Republican Rick Jackson in the November 3 general election.

Her current campaign emphasizes affordability, Medicaid expansion, housing and homeownership relief, education, lower taxes on certain household costs and a $15 minimum wage. In an October guest column, Bottoms said her affordability agenda would target healthcare, groceries and family essentials, gas and auto insurance, housing and education. Those are campaign proposals, not enacted policy, and several would require legislative cooperation.

The most consequential institutional issue now surrounding her candidacy is not one of those policy proposals. It is what could happen after voters cast their ballots.

The Atlanta Journal-Constitution has reported that Republican leaders could consider a post-election special session that might redraw congressional boundaries and, if Bottoms wins, attempt to reduce powers of the governor before she takes office. No such move is certain as of October 31. Gov. Brian Kemp has not publicly committed to calling such a session, legislative leaders have not announced a bill curtailing the next governor's powers, and the precise legality of any proposal would depend on what the legislation actually says.

That uncertainty must stay explicit.

# WEBSITE EDITORIAL

## Georgia Is Choosing a Governor. Will the Winner Get the Office Voters Chose?

Georgia voters are being asked a straightforward question on November 3: who should be their next governor?

But another question is beginning to form behind it.

If voters choose Democrat Keisha Lance Bottoms, will she take office in January with the same governorship Georgians believed they were filling when they cast their ballots?

That is not a hypothetical question invented by her campaign. The Atlanta Journal-Constitution has reported that Republican officials could consider a lame-duck special session after the election. One possible purpose would be another round of redistricting. Another, if Bottoms wins, could be legislation reducing some powers of the governor before she is sworn in.

Nothing about that is certain yet. Gov. Brian Kemp has not announced such a session, legislative leaders have not produced a public bill stripping powers from Bottoms, and Republicans would be entitled to argue for legislation they believe improves Georgia government.

But the possibility is serious enough to deserve scrutiny before the votes are counted, because the office Georgia voters are choosing is not ceremonial.

The Georgia Constitution vests the state's chief executive powers in the governor. The governor may veto legislation, enforce state law, fill certain vacancies, make authorized appointments and call the General Assembly into special session. Georgia's own public-information site describes the governor as the state's chief executive and explains that the office oversees the executive branch and has substantial appointment, veto and emergency powers.

That is the office voters are choosing.

## Before the governor's race, there was Atlanta

Bottoms is not arriving in this race without a record.

She is an Atlanta native, lawyer and former judge who served on the Atlanta City Council before becoming the city's 60th mayor in 2018. She became only the second woman elected mayor of Atlanta.

Her term included events that would have tested almost any city administration: a crippling ransomware attack, the COVID-19 pandemic, protests following George Floyd's murder, the police killing of Rayshard Brooks, a sharp increase in violent crime and a campaign to break Buckhead away from Atlanta.

Her supporters point to actions on transparency, equity, affordable housing, first-responder pay and public investment. City records from her administration credit her with creating a fully staffed Office of Equity, Diversity and Inclusion, expanding financial transparency, eliminating cash bail for certain city offenses and eventually pushing for an Office of Inspector General.

Her opponents point to the disorder of 2020 and the rise in violent crime.

That criticism is not invented.

An Atlanta Journal-Constitution review found Atlanta's reported homicides increased 59% from 2019 to 2020, compared with a 33% rise among other large police agencies in the comparison data. Eight-year-old Secoriea Turner was killed near the Wendy's where Rayshard Brooks had been shot by police, after the site became a volatile protest area.

There is also a disputed question about whether Atlanta police were told to stand down around that site. Court testimony later indicated officers were instructed to pull back. Former interim Police Chief Rodney Bryant has said there was no stand-down order and that officers remained in coordination with Bottoms and her aides.

Both facts belong in the story.

So does Bottoms' own public response. When demonstrations after George Floyd's murder turned into looting and destruction, she went on television and told people to go home. She defended the legitimacy of protest while condemning violence. After Turner was killed, she again publicly condemned the gun violence.

Readers should not have to choose between pretending Bottoms' mayoral record was flawless and pretending one of the most chaotic years in modern American city government can be explained by one mayor.

The record should be examined, not cleaned up.

## The history is real even if she does not campaign on it every day

Bottoms' candidacy carries another fact Georgia cannot avoid.

The Associated Press reports that Georgia has never elected a governor who was not a white man. If Bottoms wins, she would become the first Black woman elected governor anywhere in the United States.

Her own family history reaches directly into the history that makes that milestone possible. In her 2018 inaugural address, Bottoms described her grandparents as descendants of formerly enslaved people who came to Atlanta from East Georgia seeking opportunity.

That does not mean anyone should vote for her because of her race or sex.

History is not a substitute for a governing record.

But history should not be erased simply because acknowledging it makes people uncomfortable. A Black woman becoming governor of a state that once enforced slavery and Jim Crow would be historically significant whether a voter supports her policies or opposes them.

The proper democratic response to that history is not to lower the standard by which Bottoms is judged.

It is to apply the same standard to everybody.

## Voters are supposed to choose the office too

This is where the story becomes larger than Keisha Lance Bottoms.

Georgia voters know they are electing a governor with a veto.

They know the governor leads the executive branch.

They know the governor has appointment authority and participates in the budget and legislative process.

Those powers affect the practical value of the vote.

A voter may support Bottoms because they want Medicaid expansion. Another may oppose her because they dislike her record on crime. Someone else may support Jackson because they prefer his tax policies. Another may vote against him because they dislike his politics.

Those are democratic choices.

But if an outgoing government waits until after voters have made their choice and then changes the balance of power before the winner takes office, another question appears:

Were voters choosing the office they were told they were choosing?

Georgia lawmakers unquestionably possess legislative authority. The General Assembly can pass laws, and the governor can call it into special session. The Constitution itself allows a special session to enact laws related to the purposes stated in the governor's proclamation.

The existence of that authority, however, does not answer whether every possible use of it is democratically wise, legally valid or consistent with the voters' understanding of the office.

Those questions depend on the actual legislation.

There is also American precedent for the political maneuver. North Carolina Republicans passed legislation reducing some authority of incoming Democratic Gov. Roy Cooper after his 2016 victory. Wisconsin Republicans used a lame-duck session after the 2018 election to limit powers of incoming Democratic Gov. Tony Evers and the incoming Democratic attorney general; litigation followed and parts of the changes survived court challenges.

Georgia does not have to repeat another state's fight to learn from it.

## What went wrong?

Nothing has legally gone wrong yet simply because politicians discussed their options.

That distinction matters.

The democratic problem would arise if officials waited until citizens completed the election and then materially changed the powers attached to the office because they disliked who won it.

If lawmakers believe the Georgia governorship has too much power, they are entitled to make that argument.

They can introduce legislation.

They can campaign on it.

They can tell voters before the election that they intend to redistribute executive authority regardless of which candidate wins.

The uncomfortable part is timing.

A reform announced before an election can become part of the choice.

A reform designed after the result can look like an attempt to revise the choice.

That is the accountability line worth watching.

## Who benefits?

If the General Assembly reduced the next governor's authority, the immediate institutional beneficiary would be whichever institutions received that authority instead.

That could mean the legislature, separately elected executive officers, boards, commissions or other state bodies, depending on the actual bill.

Politically, lawmakers opposing a Bottoms administration could also gain leverage by limiting tools she would otherwise possess.

But until legislation exists, TMS should not pretend to know the exact beneficiary.

The power transfer must be traced from the text of the bill if one appears.

## Who pays?

The first people who could lose something are Georgia voters.

Not because every governor is entitled to unlimited authority. Governors are not monarchs.

The problem is simpler.

Voters make decisions partly based on what an office can do.

If the job changes after the vote, the public may discover that the candidate they elected cannot exercise authority voters reasonably understood to belong to that office.

Bottoms would also bear the political consequence if she won and entered office with fewer tools than her predecessor.

And the cost would not stop with Democrats. Once a legislature establishes that an incoming governor's powers can be reduced in the lame-duck period because the majority dislikes the winner, the same logic can be used against a future Republican governor.

Rules created for an opponent do not stay owned by one party.

## Who made it possible?

Georgia's Constitution itself creates the relevant structure.

It vests chief executive power in the governor but also gives the General Assembly broad legislative power. The governor can call special sessions, and the legislature can pass laws within the scope of the proclamation. The governor may veto legislation, while two-thirds of each chamber can override that veto.

Brian Kemp remains governor until his successor takes office.

The current General Assembly remains capable of legislating during that period if properly convened.

That is why the timing matters.

If Kemp called lawmakers into session after the election but before January, legislation could reach his desk rather than the desk of the person voters had just selected to succeed him.

That is the mechanism.

It is not a conspiracy theory.

It is also not proof that it will happen.

## The Receipt

Here is what we can actually document as of October 31, 2026.

Georgia's official primary audit shows Bottoms won the Democratic nomination outright, receiving more than 608,000 votes in the May gubernatorial primary.

The November 3 general election pits Bottoms against Republican Rick Jackson.

The Georgia Constitution gives the governor chief executive authority, veto power, appointment functions and authority to call special sessions.

The Atlanta Journal-Constitution reports that Republican officials have discussed the possibility of a post-election special session that could include redistricting and, in the event of a Bottoms victory, efforts to limit gubernatorial authority.

No public legislation doing that has yet been identified in this review.

That last sentence is just as important as the others.

## The Recipe

There is a clean way to handle this.

If Georgia lawmakers believe the powers of the governorship need reform, publish the proposal.

Show the text.

Explain which powers would move, where they would go and why.

Explain whether the change would apply permanently to every future governor or whether it is designed around the person who just won.

Hold hearings.

Let legal scholars, affected agencies and the public examine the proposal.

Record the votes.

And give Georgians enough information to decide whether lawmakers are reforming government or reacting to an election result.

That is what accountability looks like.

## What can ordinary people do?

The immediate action is not to assume something happened that has not happened.

Watch the actual record.

Georgia residents can follow the General Assembly, read introduced bills, identify their state representative and senator, contact those offices, attend or watch committee proceedings and ask a very specific question if a special session is called:

**Would this change have been proposed if the other candidate had won?**

Then ask for the answer in writing.

If a bill is introduced, compare the powers of the governorship before and after the proposed change.

Do not argue about personalities first.

Follow the authority.

That is how voters can tell whether the office itself is being redesigned.

## The Wait. What? fact

Here is the fact worth remembering:

Georgia voters could elect a governor on November 3 while the outgoing governor and current legislature would still have a window before inauguration in which a special session could pass legislation affecting the powers available to the incoming administration.

That does not prove abuse.

It does explain why the public should be watching.

## The larger seat

Keisha Lance Bottoms should be judged on her record.

Her handling of Atlanta's violent-crime surge deserves scrutiny.

Her policy proposals deserve scrutiny.

Her accomplishments deserve scrutiny.

Rick Jackson deserves the same scrutiny.

But once Georgia voters make their decision, another principle becomes more important than either candidate:

The government should be very careful about changing the meaning of a vote after it has been cast.

If an office is too powerful, reform it openly.

If authority is misplaced, prove it.

If the structure needs changing, tell the public before the election whenever possible and defend the change as a rule that should apply no matter which party controls the office.

The seat belongs to the public before it belongs to the person who occupies it.

That is why this is not only a story about whether Keisha Lance Bottoms becomes the first Black woman elected governor in American history.

It is also a story about whether the institution respects the choice that put someone in the seat.

# COUNTERARGUMENT / FAIRNESS CHECK

Legislatures have the constitutional role of making laws, and gubernatorial powers are not frozen forever. A legislature may have legitimate institutional reasons to alter executive authority. A post-election law is not automatically unconstitutional or illegitimate merely because it affects an incoming governor.

The accountability issue is therefore not "Republicans are forbidden to legislate after an election." It is whether a specific change is lawful, permanent, institutionally justified, transparently debated and defensible regardless of the identity or party of the incoming governor.

No intent finding should be made without evidence from the actual legislation, statements, chronology and institutional record.

# ACTION PATH — WHAT CAN A GEORGIA READER DO?

**5 minutes:** Use the Georgia General Assembly website to identify your state representative and senator and save their contact information.

**If a special session is called:** Read the governor's proclamation first. It defines the permitted subjects of legislation in that special session.

**If a bill changes executive power:** Save the introduced text and every substitute. Identify exactly which authority is removed, where it goes, when the change takes effect and whether it is temporary or permanent.

**Ask your legislators:** "Would you support this same transfer of power if your preferred candidate had won the governorship?"

**Follow the vote:** Committee votes, floor votes, amendments, the governor's action and any lawsuit should become part of the public record.
