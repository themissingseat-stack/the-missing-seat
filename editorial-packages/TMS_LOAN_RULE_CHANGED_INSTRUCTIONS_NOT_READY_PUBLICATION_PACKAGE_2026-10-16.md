## Website editorial

When a borrower calls about a student loan, they should not need to know which part of the answer came from the Education Department and which part came from its contractor. They need to know what applies to their account, what they must do and when. That is a reasonable expectation from a system managing a financial obligation.

A rule can be announced before the people implementing it understand every practical step. That does not make the rule meaningless or the staff dishonest. It makes the preparation important. The person on the receiving end should not become the place where government instructions are tested for the first time.

In a report publicly released August 6, 2026, the Government Accountability Office examined Education's instructions to student-loan servicers. All four servicers interviewed raised concerns about clarifying questions. One instruction generated six rounds of questions over two months. The review covered 68 change requests issued between March 2020 and December 2024. It was not a review of every borrower's bill. [1]

## A government instruction becomes somebody's account

The department communicates changes through documents called change requests. In ordinary language, those tell the companies servicing federal loans what to change in their systems and communications. The practical issue is whether the instructions are clear enough to implement accurately and on time.

GAO found inconsistent early coordination. It also recorded improvement reported by Education in December 2025, including consultation that changed draft instructions. The department disputed GAO's recommendation for formal criteria, arguing that it could hinder flexibility. GAO maintained its recommendation, which remained open when checked October 16. [1]

That disagreement deserves an honest hearing. Policy can change quickly, including because of court orders. A rule requiring the same lengthy consultation for every minor revision could slow necessary action. But GAO is asking for criteria to decide when early coordination is needed, not a meeting for every sentence. [2] Complex changes and tight deadlines are exactly when someone should decide how implementation questions will be answered.

The measure should be whether the borrower receives a usable answer. A press release, a finished instruction document and an updated account are different stages. A department can complete the first without knowing that the last is ready. An accountable process connects them before the obligation reaches a person expected to act on it.

## Who is missing?

The missing seat belongs to the borrower trying to make a correct decision with incomplete or conflicting information. A person balancing rent, food and a loan payment has limited room to absorb uncertainty. This article does not claim that GAO measured how often such uncertainty caused fees, delinquency or loss. Those consequences need account-level evidence.

Education holds authority over program instructions and its servicing contracts. Servicers control important parts of implementation and borrower communication within those requirements. Borrowers control neither, but they are the ones who must respond to a notice and preserve records when answers differ.

Clearer preparation benefits borrowers, frontline staff and contractors trying to carry out the policy. Government may gain speed by issuing a change immediately; whether that is worth the implementation risk depends on the circumstances. There is no finding here that a servicer profited from deliberately confusing anyone. The accountability question is whether the work was organized around delivering a reliable result.

This matters beyond a single repayment plan. Public administration often separates deciding a policy from making it usable. When the two do not connect, the individual can be told the rule is clear while the organizations applying it are still exchanging questions. A taxpayer should be able to ask whether the contract includes a working path from instruction to accurate service.

## Ask for the answer in writing

Education should identify which changes need early coordination and how unresolved questions will be handled. For major changes, it should explain who is responsible for confirming that account systems, staff instructions and borrower messages agree. Urgent action may require a different process, but the reason for that choice should be recorded and problems corrected quickly.

That is not a demand for a guarantee that every account will always be error-free. It is a demand that foreseeable confusion have an owner before it becomes a borrower's problem. Nor should contractor consultation give companies the power to decide federal policy. They need to understand how to implement it; the public agency remains responsible for the decision.

If you receive conflicting information, save the notices, account screenshots and dates of contact. Ask the servicer to identify in writing the instruction applying to your account, its effective date and the correction you are requesting. If the issue remains unresolved, use Federal Student Aid's official feedback channel and keep the case reference. [3] A complaint alone does not change your payment obligation or suspend a deadline; ask specifically what applies while the issue is reviewed.

The public action is equally concrete. Send GAO-26-107780 to your congressional representative and ask what evidence Education uses to confirm a major servicing change is ready for borrowers. Ask for the criteria, not another promise to communicate better.

The person who owes the money should not have to coordinate the organizations collecting it. When government changes the rule, making the instructions usable is part of the work.
