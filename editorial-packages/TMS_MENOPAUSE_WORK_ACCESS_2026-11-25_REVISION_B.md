## The Short Version

**What happened:** On January 1, Illinois becomes one of the first places in the U.S. to require workplace accommodations for menopause. There is still no federal law that names it. Research shows symptoms can last for years, and longest for Black women. Many workers with symptoms say they get no support at work.

**Who it affects:** Women in midlife, especially those in jobs where they cannot change their hours, their breaks or the temperature, and anyone whose career depends on not looking "past it."

**What you can do:** Ask your employer in writing who handles health-related adjustment requests and how the information is kept private. Ask your state lawmakers whether your state protects menopause at work.

## Website editorial

Picture a woman who has done the same job for twenty years. Now she is sleeping three hours a night, soaking through her shirt in meetings, and losing words she has used her whole career. She knows what it is. The question is whether saying so at work will get her help, or get her quietly written off.

For millions of women, that is the real cost of menopause. The symptoms are ordinary. The silence around them is a choice workplaces make.

### What the research shows

Menopause is a normal stage of life. Symptoms range from mild to severe. They include hot flashes, night sweats, broken sleep, mood changes and trouble with memory and concentration. [1]

They do not pass quickly. The Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN) followed more than 3,000 women at seven U.S. sites for up to 17 years. For more than half of the women with frequent hot flashes and night sweats, the symptoms lasted more than seven years. [2] Black women had the longest median duration, about 10 years. Japanese and Chinese American women had the shortest, around five. [3] The researchers told doctors to warn women that symptoms may last longer for African American women. [2]

Ten years is not a phase. It is a large share of a working life, arriving at the age when many women are in line for their most senior jobs.

### What it costs at work

A 2023 Mayo Clinic study surveyed women aged 45 to 60 who were in primary care. Among the 4,440 who were working, 13.4 percent reported at least one adverse work outcome tied to menopause symptoms, and 10.8 percent had missed work because of them in the past year. Some cut back hours. Some were laid off, quit or retired. The authors estimated the missed work costs about $1.8 billion a year in the United States. [4] The study has limits: only 16 percent of those invited responded, and 93 percent of respondents were white. If symptoms last longest for Black women, a mostly white sample may understate the burden.

A 2025 study at one U.S. drug company found that only 9 percent of employees with current symptoms said they received any support at work. Some said they no longer recognized themselves on the job. The study was funded by Pfizer, and it covers one employer. [5]

### Why women hide it

Two things meet in midlife: a woman's body changes, and her workplace starts deciding whether she still has a future there.

Workplaces have long been designed around a worker who does not menstruate, get pregnant or go through menopause. When a woman's health needs show up, they are treated as a private inconvenience, and she is expected to absorb the cost. That is how patriarchy works at the office. It shows up in rules about who gets a break, who controls the thermostat, and whose sick days count against them, not in anyone's stated intentions.

Ageism adds a second pressure. A manager who assumes menopause makes a woman unreliable may stop sending her to training or passing her name up for promotion. She may never know why. So many women say nothing, and the problem stays invisible to the people who could fix it.

The answer is not to make women announce their symptoms. A good workplace offers help without requiring anyone to explain intimate details. Someone can ask for a fan, a cooler desk, a flexible start time or a break to change clothes, and the request goes to one named person, stays confidential and gets an answer in writing. The 2025 study's authors made the same point: support should be available without disclosure. [5]

### What the law says

No federal law names menopause. Workers rely on overlapping protections. Title VII bans sex discrimination, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act bans age discrimination, and the Americans with Disabilities Act can cover severe symptoms. Courts have been mixed on whether menopause itself is covered. [6]

States are moving. Rhode Island became the first state to require workplace accommodations for menopause in 2025. Illinois passed the Menopause Equity and Care Act in 2026, and its workplace rules take effect January 1, 2027, along with a Philadelphia ordinance. [7] At least 16 menopause bills were introduced in state legislatures in the first months of 2026. [8] Not every one survives. California Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed one bill in September, and Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger vetoed another in May. Both said existing state civil rights law already covers it. [9] In Congress, Representatives Debbie Dingell and Yvette Clarke introduced the Menopausal Workers' Fairness Act in July. It would create a federal right to reasonable accommodation, but its chances are slim. [10]

### Treatment is easier to get than it was. Ask anyway.

For two decades, hormone therapy carried the FDA's strongest "boxed" warning, based on a 2002 study. After it was published, prescriptions dropped among women of all ages. In November 2025, the FDA removed the warnings about heart disease, breast cancer and dementia from the boxed label. The warning about uterine cancer stays for women who still have a uterus and take estrogen alone. [11] Some doctors welcomed the change. Others criticized the FDA for skipping its usual public advisory process. [12]

The label change does not make hormone therapy right for everyone. It means a woman can expect a real conversation with her doctor about her symptoms, her health history and her options, including non-hormonal treatments. "It's natural" is not an answer when she cannot sleep or do her job.

### The test

A menopause policy is only as good as what happens when someone uses it. Did she get the adjustment? Did she keep her shot at the next promotion? Did anyone count how many women left before they had to?

She is still doing her job. She should not have to hide what it costs.

## Pull Up A Chair

- **At work:** Ask HR in writing who handles health-related adjustment requests and how the information is kept confidential. You do not have to give your diagnosis to ask for a fan or a schedule change.
- **Keep a record:** Write down how symptoms affect your sleep and work, and bring it to your doctor. Ask about all of your options, including non-hormonal ones.
- **If you manage people:** Name one confidential contact and track whether requests get answered.
- **Check your state:** Ask your state legislators whether your state protects menopause at work. Illinois rules take effect January 1, 2027.

## Seat Summary

Menopause symptoms can last a decade, and longest for Black women. They arrive just as careers peak. Most workplaces offer no route to help without exposure, and federal law does not name the condition. A few states are starting to fill that gap. The missing seat is a woman's right to keep her career and her privacy at the same time.
