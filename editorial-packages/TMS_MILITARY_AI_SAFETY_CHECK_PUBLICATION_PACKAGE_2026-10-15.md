# WEBSITE EDITORIAL

The United States military wants artificial intelligence faster.

That is not hard to understand. Other countries are investing in AI. Military leaders do not want American service members sent into danger with slower information, weaker defenses or outdated tools. Used well, AI might help maintain equipment, analyze large amounts of information, improve logistics, strengthen cybersecurity and support decisions under pressure.

But there is another question, and it matters just as much: who is responsible for checking whether the technology is ready, trustworthy and being used as promised?

The Defense Department already knows this question matters. It adopted ethical principles for military AI. Those principles say people must remain responsible, systems should minimize unintended bias, methods should be traceable, performance should be reliable, and systems should be governable when they behave in unintended ways.

Those are serious promises. They are also only as strong as the people, rules and institutions responsible for carrying them out.

On October 14, 2026, the Government Accountability Office reported that the Defense Department had taken real steps toward responsible AI. Most of the ten Defense components GAO surveyed said they had begun establishing oversight and applying responsible-AI principles in the selected uses reviewed.

That is the part that deserves to be recognized. This is not a story claiming the Pentagon has no safeguards. It is not proof that a military AI system harmed anyone. GAO did not conclude that Defense AI is generally unsafe.

The problem is that the accountability structure has not kept pace with the ambition.

## The office had ten positions. Three were filled.

The Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office has a Responsible AI Division. Its job includes helping Defense components put the department's principles into practice.

According to GAO, officials said that only three of the division's ten authorized positions were filled as of February 2026. They said the shortage limited work the division could do.

That number deserves more attention.

The military is not discussing a small software upgrade. AI can influence how information is sorted, which risks receive attention, how systems are tested and how people make decisions. Some applications may be routine. Others may affect service members, civilians and decisions with life-changing consequences.

If the department wants rapid adoption, the people assigned to test, coordinate and question that adoption cannot be treated like optional overhead.

The people pushing a system forward will usually have a clear mission, budget and deadline. The people asking whether it is fair, reliable, explainable and controllable need authority and capacity too.

Otherwise, speed wins by default.

## The working council stopped meeting

The department created a Responsible AI Working Council so components could share problems, practices and lessons. GAO reported that the council did not meet from late 2024 until August 2025.

Officials from four Defense components told GAO that they faced implementation problems during that period that could have benefited from collaboration. Six of the ten surveyed components said sharing responsible-AI resources and practices would help their work.

The council resumed meeting, but GAO found another weakness. It lacked a charter defining roles, responsibilities and how often it should meet. Without that structure, GAO warned that collaboration could lapse again.

This sounds bureaucratic until something goes wrong.

If different military components interpret the same safety principle differently, who decides which interpretation controls? If one office discovers a testing problem, who makes sure another office learns from it? If a vendor says its system meets the standard, who has the authority and expertise to challenge that claim?

A council meeting will not answer every question. A charter will not make an algorithm trustworthy. But named responsibility matters because responsibility that belongs to everyone often belongs to no one when the pressure rises.

## Who is missing?

The missing seats belong first to the service members expected to rely on these systems. They may be asked to trust an output while carrying the consequences if it is wrong.

Civilians are missing too. Military technology does not operate in an empty room. Decisions involving surveillance, targeting, logistics, intelligence and cybersecurity can affect people who never chose to participate in the system.

The public is also missing. Taxpayers fund the technology, but much of military AI development cannot be fully public for legitimate security reasons. That makes independent oversight more important, not less.

Finally, the safety staff themselves are missing. A department cannot say responsible AI is central while leaving most authorized positions in its responsible-AI division unfilled.

## Who benefits, and who carries the burden?

Technology companies can benefit from military contracts. Defense leaders can benefit from faster tools and a reputation for innovation. Military units can benefit when AI genuinely improves information, readiness and protection.

Those benefits are real.

But the burdens are not evenly distributed. Service members may have to rely on systems they did not choose. Civilians may experience consequences without knowing how a decision was made. Oversight staff may be asked to cover more systems than their capacity allows. The public may be told to trust a process it cannot fully see.

That does not mean military AI should stop. It means accountability must grow at least as fast as deployment.

## The strongest counterargument

The military cannot publish every technical detail or move at the speed of a civilian committee. Adversaries are not waiting. Some secrecy protects operations, capabilities and lives. More review can also become paperwork that delays useful technology without making it safer.

That is fair.

But GAO did not recommend exposing classified systems or freezing AI development. It recommended basic management: define roles, keep the working council functioning and staff the division responsible for helping the department implement its own principles.

Those steps can support faster adoption because commanders and service members are more likely to trust systems that have passed a credible process. Responsible AI is not the brake. Done properly, it is part of the engine.

## Why should an ordinary person care?

Military AI may sound distant from everyday life, but the bill is public, the authority is public and the consequences can extend far beyond a military base.

The standards developed inside Defense can influence federal purchasing, private contractors and the wider technology market. A weak accountability model can become a template. A strong one can show that speed and responsibility do not have to be enemies.

This is also a basic government question. When an agency announces principles, does it fund and staff the work required to follow them? Or do the promises stay on paper while the technology moves ahead?

The Pentagon has already written the values. Now it needs to show who owns them.

## What action looks like

Congress should require the Defense Department to report, in an unclassified form, whether the Responsible AI Division's authorized positions are filled, whether the Working Council has an adopted charter, how often it meets and how unresolved responsibility gaps are being closed.

That request does not demand classified operational details. It asks whether the accountability system exists, has people in the seats and is functioning.

If the military wants the public to trust AI at battlefield speed, it has to build oversight at the same speed.

# WHO BENEFITED / WHO PAID

**Who may benefit**

- Service members and commanders when AI improves readiness, information, maintenance or protection.
- Defense leaders seeking faster adoption of useful capabilities.
- Technology companies receiving military AI contracts.
- The public when responsible systems improve national security without abandoning legal and ethical safeguards.

**Who holds power**

- Congress controls legislation, oversight and appropriations.
- The Secretary of Defense and the Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Officer control departmental responsibility, staffing and implementation.
- Defense components decide how principles are applied in specific programs.
- Contractors help design and supply systems, but government remains responsible for public authority.

**Who carries the burden**

- Service members asked to rely on AI-enabled systems.
- Civilians affected by military decisions or system failures.
- Oversight personnel working without the capacity or authority needed to match deployment.
- Taxpayers funding technology whose safeguards cannot always be inspected publicly.

## Receipt 2: Three of ten authorized positions were filled

GAO reported that Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office officials said only three of ten authorized Responsible AI Division positions were filled as of February 2026. Officials said staffing shortages hindered additional implementation work.

Source: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-107837

## Receipt 3: The Working Council had lapsed

GAO reported that the Responsible AI Working Council did not meet from late 2024 until August 2025. It later resumed, but lacked a charter defining roles, responsibilities and meeting frequency.

Source: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-107837

## Receipt 4: Defense already has ethical principles

The Defense Department reaffirmed five AI ethical principles in 2021: responsible, equitable, traceable, reliable and governable. The memorandum says they apply to Defense AI capabilities of any scale.

Source: https://media.defense.gov/2021/May/27/2002730593/-1/-1/0/IMPLEMENTING-RESPONSIBLE-ARTIFICIAL-INTELLIGENCE-IN-THE-DEPARTMENT-OF-DEFENSE.PDF

## Receipt 5: Defense released implementation tools

The Chief Digital and Artificial Intelligence Office released a Responsible AI Toolkit in 2023. Defense described it as a voluntary process for identifying, tracking and improving alignment with responsible-AI practices.

Source: https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3588743/cdao-releases-responsible-ai-rai-toolkit-for-ensuring-alignment-with-rai-best-p/

# VERIFICATION NOTICE

Verified October 15, 2026 against GAO-27-107837 and Defense Department primary materials. GAO's survey covered ten selected Defense components and ten selected AI use cases; its findings should not be generalized as a complete audit of every military AI system. The three-of-ten staffing figure reflects what officials reported as of February 2026 and should be refreshed before later publication. The Working Council resumed meetings in August 2025; the article does not say it remains inactive. GAO's recommendations were open when checked. The Defense Department concurred with GAO's two new recommendations. No claim of an unsafe system, unlawful conduct or proven harm is made.
