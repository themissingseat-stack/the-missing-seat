## The Short Version

Who it affects: Families, students, teachers and researchers looking for records of Black women's work and civic life.

## Website editorial

The materials for a 1939 Washington exhibit on Black women's history came on loan from Howard University. Dorothy Porter, who supervised the library collection that grew into the Moorland-Spingarn Research Center, helped organize the exhibit. The next year, she asked women around the country for materials for an exhibition in Chicago. Sue Bailey Thurman went to Chicago to set it up. Each exhibition had an end date. Keeping the record meant finding somewhere it could stay. [1]

Mary McLeod Bethune had founded the National Council of Negro Women in 1935. Some of its members worked with Mary Beard's World Center for Women's Archives. The partnership gave them a place to exhibit, but it didn't produce a lasting home for the Chicago loans. The Park Service's history says lenders were unwilling to donate those materials to the World Center and connects that reluctance to Black women's limited inclusion in the organization. The same account says the center disbanded by 1941 for lack of funds. That last fact matters: the record gives us both a problem of representation and a problem of sustaining an institution. [1, 2]

## The work of keeping things

In 1941 the council started its own archives committee. Porter chaired it first. The committee asked for documents, diplomas and photographs from women across occupations and walks of life. Thurman had founded the Aframerican Woman's Journal in 1940 and became committee chair in 1944. The journal carried appeals asking people to send material. This was a practical choice about what could count as history: a photograph or a diploma that looked ordinary at home might be one of the few records of what somebody did. [1]

The women doing the collecting also had to raise money and make the case for keeping it. In 1946 Thurman's mother, Susie Ford Bailey, donated $1,000 toward a museum. That did not buy an institution overnight. The council opened its museum and the National Archives for Black Women's History at the Council House on November 11, 1979. The council's own records now form the core of the archive, alongside personal papers and records from other organizations. The people who did the collecting left a record of their decisions, too. [1, 3]

The Park Service offers a selection of documents online. Its dedicated archives page currently says it is not accepting research requests. The collections page still describes appointment requests, so confirm access directly before planning research. The online selection remains a way to begin. The surviving material gives students and descendants evidence they can use. It takes staff time, storage and workable access to keep that benefit available. [3, 4, 5]

## Pull Up A Chair

Start with the online selection and note who made a document, what it records and what it leaves unanswered. If you have photographs or minutes from a club or family, label one item with names, a place and a date or honest estimate. Keep the original and a copy; get permission before putting private material online. If you plan to offer records to an archive, ask what it collects and how it handles access before sending anything. Keep the label with the item so the next person can make sense of it.

## Seat Summary

The council and its collaborators organized the exhibits, paid and raised money for collection work, and decided which records to ask for. Lenders decided whether to donate. The Park Service now stewards the holdings and controls research access. Families and scholars benefit from a record they can consult, though a sample online does not replace full access.
