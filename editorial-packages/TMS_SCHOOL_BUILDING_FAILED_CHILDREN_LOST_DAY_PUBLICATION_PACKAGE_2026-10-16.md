## Website editorial

We tell children that showing up matters. Then a pipe breaks, the heating fails or a classroom becomes too hot to use, and the building cannot show up for them. The child did not choose to miss school. The public institution responsible for providing it could not keep the room open.

A school closure can become a parent's work problem within minutes. Someone has to arrange care, change a shift or find another place for a child to spend the day. Those are foreseeable consequences, not a claim that investigators counted every lost paycheck. They belong in the discussion because a repair budget is not the only place where the cost lands.

The Government Accountability Office estimates that 19% of public school districts canceled school because of facility problems during 2024-25. About 2 million students were affected, with an estimated 3.5 million student-days of learning lost. Those are days added across students, not 3.5 million days on a calendar. The report was publicly released September 14, 2026. [1]

These are survey estimates, not a count of every incident. GAO gives a range of 1.5 million to 2.4 million students affected. Nearly half of districts, an estimated 47%, reported chronic facility problems that often disrupted learning. The study does not establish that every such building was unsafe or that every closure was preventable. [1]

## The budget does not contain the whole bill

There are good reasons to close a building when conditions become dangerous. Keeping children inside so the attendance record looks better would be a terrible response. The accountability question comes before and after that decision: what was known, what could reasonably have been prevented, and what happened to the children while repairs were made?

An emergency notice tells a family what happens today. It rarely explains the earlier decisions. Was the problem newly discovered? Had staff reported it repeatedly? Was there money for repair but no available contractor, or no money at all? Did a temporary patch become the plan because nobody could authorize something better? Those questions require local records. A national report cannot answer them for every district.

District leaders face real financial limits. GAO estimates that 55% considered their latest facilities budget insufficient. It also found that districts with less preventive-maintenance capacity had more chronic problems and instructional disruption. That association supports attention to maintenance; it does not prove that any particular repair expense would have prevented a particular closure. [1]

We should be careful with the word savings. Postponing a repair can leave money available for something else this year. Whether that is a responsible choice depends on the condition, alternatives and risks. But if the consequence is repeated disruption, the cost has not disappeared. Some of it has been transferred to children, teachers and families.

## Who is missing?

The missing seat belongs to the student whose learning time is treated as an unfortunate side effect of an infrastructure decision. It also belongs to the caretaker who cannot rearrange paid work at short notice and the teacher expected to recover lost time without being given more of it. Families with fewer alternatives have less room to absorb a sudden closure. That is the equity question to investigate locally, not a racial or income disparity measured by this report.

School boards and administrators control important maintenance and planning choices. State lawmakers and funding authorities shape what districts can afford. Their responsibilities differ, so naming the responsible office matters more than blaming a vague government. A district should not be blamed for refusing to spend money it does not have. A funding authority should not disappear from the story just because the district sent the closure message.

No evidence here establishes that a contractor or official deliberately benefited from a failure. The possible short-term institutional benefit is avoiding an immediate expense or difficult budget choice. The people carrying disruption should not be left out when that choice is assessed.

An ordinary taxpayer has a reason to care even without a child in school. A public building should deliver the service funded inside it. Maintenance is part of that service, not decoration added after the educational work is finished.

## Receipts should lead to a repair date

GAO describes practical approaches, including preventive maintenance, facilities planning and state assistance with projects. There is no single national fix that removes the need for local judgment. [1] A useful response would connect the condition of each building to an affordable plan, a responsible decision-maker and a public explanation of what must wait.

Ask your school board for its latest facilities condition assessment, unresolved urgent repairs and facility-related closure days. If no assessment exists, ask when one will be completed. Then request a short public schedule identifying the repair, funding status, responsible office, expected completion and arrangements for students during disruption. Personal student and staff information does not belong in that public record.

The strongest counterargument is that districts already spend too much time reporting and too little time repairing. That concern is fair. The answer should be a usable summary built from maintenance records, not another elaborate dashboard that consumes the maintenance budget. Public reporting earns its place when it helps a board fund a repair, lets a family plan or exposes a delay someone has authority to resolve.

We can tell children attendance matters and still ask whether the adults responsible for the building have made attendance possible. A canceled school day should come with more than a message explaining the failure. It should come with a plan for ending it.

## Receipts, verification and distinctness

1. GAO-26-107970, published September 9 and publicly released September 14, 2026: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107970
2. Full report, methodology, survey tables and practices: https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107970/index.html

Verification: estimates describe school year 2024-25, not current national conditions in October 2026. The nationwide survey had a 62% weighted response; nine district visits in three states are illustrative. The 19% estimate has a 15-24% confidence interval; affected students 1.5-2.4 million and lost student-days 2.5-4.5 million. Budget insufficiency is district-reported. No nationwide dollar loss, race-specific effect, legal violation or universal preventability is claimed. Family/work consequences are explicitly identified as foreseeable risks. The action is TMS's recommendation.

Distinctness: the published absenteeism editorial examines unreliable federal rates and discontinued ESEA monitoring (GAO-26-107920). This article examines physical building failures, closure time, maintenance responsibility and a local repair plan. The German full-school-day package concerns a different country and legal care entitlement. Neither substitutes for this reporting.
