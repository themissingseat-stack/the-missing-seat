## Editorial

A woman showed up ready to prove she could repair pallets.

According to the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, she did not get the chance. She said she was told the company only hired men.

That sentence is the whole problem in plain English. Not that she failed a skills test. Not that another applicant had more experience. Not that the company eliminated the position. She was allegedly stopped before her ability could settle the question.

On October 6, 2026, the EEOC announced that Birchstone Management had agreed to pay $500,000 to resolve a federal sex-discrimination charge. Birchstone is a warehousing and logistics management company based in Dallas. The agreement provides $55,000 to the woman who filed the charge and $445,000 for a class settlement fund.

This was a settlement reached through the EEOC's pre-litigation conciliation process. It was not a court judgment, and the agreement does not give us permission to describe every allegation as a proven fact at trial.

What we can say is still serious. The EEOC reported that its investigation found that Birchstone turned away a qualified applicant seeking pallet-repair work in Spokane Valley, Washington, because she was a woman. The agency also found that, since at least May 2020, other qualified women were rejected at facilities in Spokane Valley; Amarillo and Houston, Texas; Lakewood, Washington; and Las Vegas, Nevada. According to the agency, the company hired men with similar or fewer qualifications or kept looking for applicants who were not women.

If that finding is accurate, the missing seat was not an abstract promise of equality. It was a paying job.

### Seat Summary

- **Who is missing?** Women who were qualified to compete for pallet-repair jobs but allegedly never received a fair evaluation.
- **Who was affected?** The named applicant, possible class members and families depending on access to steady work.
- **Who benefited?** Men who faced a narrower field of applicants, and an employer that allegedly allowed a sex-based shortcut to replace individual assessment.
- **Who held power?** Hiring managers and company leaders who controlled applications, qualifications, records and access to the job.
- **Who carried the burden?** Women who lost wages and experience, applicants who must prove a hiring process excluded them, and enforcement agencies trying to reconstruct decisions after the fact.

## The Receipt

Federal law has not been unclear about this for sixty years. Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 prohibits covered employers from treating applicants or employees unfavorably because of sex. An employer may define the skills a job requires. It may test applicants fairly. It may reject someone who cannot perform the work. What it may not do is substitute “woman” for “unqualified.”

The national employment numbers help explain why the alleged conduct matters beyond one company. Bureau of Labor Statistics data for 2025 show that women were 47.1 percent of employed people overall but 21.5 percent of workers in transportation and material-moving occupations. Women were 24.6 percent of laborers and freight, stock and material movers and 8.1 percent of industrial truck and tractor operators.

Those figures do not prove discrimination at Birchstone or anywhere else. They do not tell us why each person chose a job, left a job or never applied. BLS also cautions that its 2025 annual figures are based on eleven months because October data were not collected during the federal shutdown.

But low representation makes the hiring door more important, not less. When an employer sees few women in a field and treats that scarcity as evidence that women do not belong there, yesterday's exclusion becomes tomorrow's excuse.

The EEOC also reported a recordkeeping problem. Its investigation found that Birchstone failed to maintain employment records required by federal law. Records matter because discrimination rarely arrives on company letterhead. Applications, interview notes, qualification comparisons and hiring decisions can show whether the same standard was applied to everyone. When those records are missing, the burden of proving what happened can fall harder on the person who was already denied the job.

The settlement does more than divide money. Birchstone must appoint a qualified human-resources professional to revise hiring and recruitment policies and procedures. It must train personnel involved in hiring at locations in Amarillo, Houston and Lakewood, post an equal-employment notice, audit its recordkeeping annually and report to the EEOC for two years.

There is also a claims process. According to the EEOC, women who applied for Birchstone pallet-repair positions and were not hired because of sex between May 15, 2020, and July 31, 2025, at the five identified locations may contact the settlement administrator.

That matters because a settlement fund does not repair anything if the people it was created for never learn that it exists.

## Why an Ordinary Person Should Care

Most people will never repair a pallet. Almost everyone has had to convince somebody that they were capable of doing a job.

Hiring discrimination does not only take away one paycheck. It can take away overtime, health insurance, retirement contributions, training, promotions and the experience needed for the next job. The loss compounds while the employer continues operating as if the applicant was never there.

It also hurts employers and coworkers. A company that rejects qualified applicants for reasons unrelated to the work shrinks its own talent pool. Existing workers carry vacancies longer. Customers and contractors depend on a hiring system that selected by stereotype instead of ability.

And then there is the message sent to the next woman. Do not bother applying. This is not for you. Even if nobody says those words publicly, a hiring pattern can say them just as loudly.

## The Counterargument

Employers are allowed to require that workers meet the physical and technical demands of a pallet-repair job. Safety is real. Productivity is real. Experience can matter. Nothing in equal-employment law requires a company to hire someone who cannot perform the essential work.

That is not the allegation here.

The EEOC said the applicant was qualified and that men with similar or fewer qualifications were hired while women were rejected or the company continued looking for non-female applicants. A fair defense of job standards cannot answer an allegation that the standard changed with the applicant's sex.

Birchstone also chose settlement rather than litigating the charge. Settlement can avoid cost, delay and uncertainty; it does not automatically establish that every agency finding would have been proven in court. That is why this article uses “allegedly” and identifies the EEOC as the source of the findings.

The company nevertheless accepted money, policy, training, audit and reporting obligations. Those remedies should be judged by whether the hiring process actually changes.

## Who Was Missing From the Decision?

The most obvious missing person was the applicant standing at the door with qualifications the company allegedly did not evaluate fairly.

Also missing were the other women whose applications may be buried in files or whose files were not preserved. Missing were workers who might have recommended qualified women if the job had been presented as open to everyone. Missing were people responsible for compliance before the government had to intervene.

Good hiring systems do not wait for a discrimination charge to ask whether one group is being screened out. They compare who applied, who advanced, who was rejected, why the decision was made and whether the explanation matches the records.

That is not a quota. It is management knowing what its own hiring system is doing.

## The Recipe

Birchstone should publish a plain-language notice at every affected facility explaining the claims process, eligibility window and deadline. The notice should reach former applicants, not merely current employees. The company should preserve the information necessary for the settlement administrator and EEOC to identify possible class members.

During the two-year reporting period, the company should track applicants, minimum qualifications, interview decisions, offers and documented reasons for rejection for pallet-repair positions. Any public summary should protect individual privacy while showing whether women are receiving real consideration.

Other employers in male-dominated fields should not wait to see their company name in an EEOC release. They can audit job descriptions for requirements that are not actually needed, train hiring managers to evaluate ability rather than assumptions, preserve records and compare rejected applicants with the people hired.

## One Action

If you or someone you know applied for a Birchstone Management pallet-repair position between May 15, 2020, and July 31, 2025, at the Spokane Valley, Amarillo, Houston, Lakewood or Las Vegas locations and believe sex affected the decision, review the official EEOC release and contact the settlement administrator listed there. The EEOC identifies **info@BirchstoneSettlement.com** and **(877) 788-4952** for claims information.

For everyone else, ask your employer one direct question: **Do we preserve enough hiring records to show that applicants with similar qualifications are evaluated by the same standard?**

That question will not fix every barrier. It can make a hidden shortcut visible before another qualified person is sent away.

## Return to the Seat

This story is not about giving a woman a job because she is a woman.

It is about letting her compete because she is qualified.

An employer gets to ask whether an applicant can do the work. It does not get to answer that question with a stereotype before she begins.

She showed up ready to demonstrate her ability. The missing seat was the chance to be judged on it.

## Verification Notice

**Official agency finding:** The EEOC reported that its investigation found that Birchstone rejected a qualified woman because of sex and similarly rejected other qualified female applicants at five facilities while hiring men with similar or fewer qualifications or continuing to seek non-female applicants.

**Settlement posture:** The parties resolved the administrative charge through pre-litigation conciliation. This was not an adjudicated court judgment. The article does not state that a judge or jury found Birchstone liable.

**Company position:** The EEOC release does not provide a quoted response from Birchstone or state that the company admitted liability. The absence of a response in the release should not be treated as an admission.

**Settlement terms:** The $500,000 agreement consists of $55,000 for the charging party and $445,000 for a class settlement fund, plus policy, training, notice, audit and two-year reporting obligations.

**Labor statistics:** BLS reported that women were 21.5 percent of transportation and material-moving workers in its 2025 annual table. The figure is descriptive context, not proof of discrimination or a measure of Birchstone's workforce. The 2025 table uses eleven monthly observations because October data were not collected during the federal shutdown.

**Inference:** The conclusions that sex-based screening can compound lost earnings and that missing records can make discrimination harder to prove are editorial inferences grounded in the documented hiring and recordkeeping issues.

**Not established:** This article does not claim that every woman who applied was qualified, that every male employee benefited knowingly, or that low female representation in a job category proves unlawful discrimination.

**Freshness check:** Sources were reviewed October 7, 2026. The EEOC release identifies the claims contact and eligibility dates. Recheck the claims deadline and administrator information immediately before publication.
