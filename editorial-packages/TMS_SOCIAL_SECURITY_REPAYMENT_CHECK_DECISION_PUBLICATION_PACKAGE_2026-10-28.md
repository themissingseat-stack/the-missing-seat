## Website editorial

One person received a Social Security overpayment notice meant for somebody else. The agency then used the wrong beneficiary's benefits to recover the money. That is a case described by Social Security's own inspector general in a September 2026 audit. It is not an invented example of what might go wrong.

Think about what that means at a kitchen table. A person can budget carefully, open a government letter and still face a reduction that does not belong to them. The letter carries institutional authority. The person has to find out whether the number is right, what caused it and which request will actually address the problem.

The answer should not depend on already knowing the agency's vocabulary. Reconsideration, waiver and a change in recovery rate are different routes. Choosing among them is easier when someone explains what each one does before the money leaves the household budget.

## Three questions, three different requests

Start with whether the debt is correct. If you believe you were not overpaid, or the amount is wrong, SSA directs you to request reconsideration, using Form SSA-561 or its non-medical appeal process. Ask for the period involved, the amount paid, the amount SSA believes was due and the reason for the difference. Gather relevant payment records, notices and evidence of changes you reported.

A waiver asks a different question. It concerns whether SSA should collect an overpayment. The agency's rules require that the person was not at fault and that recovery meets another waiver ground, such as defeating the program's purpose or being against equity and good conscience. Financial circumstances can matter. The usual form is SSA-632. A waiver request is not simply another name for disputing the arithmetic.

Then there is the repayment rate. If you accept the debt and will repay it but cannot manage the proposed monthly deduction, SSA identifies Form SSA-634 as the request to change the recovery rate. A smaller payment can make repayment more manageable. It does not, by itself, establish that the debt should disappear.

People can have more than one issue. SSA's operating instructions allow a waiver request at the same time as an appeal. Explain what you are asking for and keep a copy of each submission. Do not assume a general conversation has been recorded as the particular request you intended.

The clocks need explaining too. SSA's current reconsideration page gives a general 60-day period after receiving the decision. Its overpayment page says a waiver or appeal requested within 30 days prevents collection from starting until the request is decided. Those are different rules. A person should not read the longer appeal period as a reason to ignore the earlier collection-protection window.

Nor should somebody assume that missing 30 days eliminates the possibility of a waiver. SSA's current operating instructions allow waiver requests at any time, including after recovery has begun. The Title II regulation also provides for stopping recovery while an initial waiver request is decided, including a request made after 30 days. Ask SSA or a qualified benefits adviser how the rule applies to your notice and benefit type. Acting promptly is still the sensible course.

## The government has to make its side understandable

The inspector general found that 58 of 126 sampled overpayments did not have notices processed according to agency policy, about 46 percent. The audit examined fiscal-year 2023 records. Its findings are not a claim that 46 percent of today's debts are invalid. The defects included inadequate explanations, missing rights information and inaccurate amounts. SSA agreed to the report's recommendations for better controls and notices.

That response matters. An institution can acknowledge a problem and improve. Agreement also needs to lead to usable corrections: a clearer letter, a properly recorded request and a decision the recipient can understand. The public should be able to see whether those changes work, rather than have to infer completion from an agreement to make them.

The power sits with the agency calculating the debt, issuing notices and controlling recovery, and with the officials responsible for its systems and oversight. A beneficiary can supply evidence and ask for review. They cannot repair the agency's records or redesign its notices on their own. Instructions need to meet that imbalance.

There is a fair counterargument. Social Security has to recover money paid beyond what the law allows. Correct recovery protects public funds and the people who depend on the program. People receiving benefits also have reporting duties. An agency error does not automatically cancel every overpayment, and hardship does not make every waiver request successful.

But collecting a valid debt and proving a debt are connected responsibilities. Accuracy protects the program too. A wrong deduction burdens the person losing income and creates more work for staff who must correct it. Clear notices help both sides identify the actual issue before it becomes another unresolved file.

## What to do with the letter

Keep the notice and envelope. Note the date received and any proposed recovery date. Ask for an understandable calculation and identify whether you are disputing the decision, seeking a waiver, requesting a lower rate or raising more than one issue. Check the current official instructions for the relevant form and submission route. Keep confirmation numbers, copies and dates.

If you are unsure, contact SSA promptly through its official website or local office and seek qualified benefits or legal-aid advice. Ask which deadline applies and whether recovery is suspended while your specific request is considered. Do not rely on a request for an explanation alone to preserve every appeal right. Someone helping a relative should protect their private information and use the appropriate authority to act for them.

For the wider accountability question, ask SSA and congressional oversight offices for aggregate measures of notice errors, corrected deductions, request-processing times and money returned after mistakes. Those measures would show whether the fix reaches the people the paperwork is supposed to serve.

A repayment notice deserves attention. It does not deserve unquestioning acceptance just because it came from the government. The person receiving it should be able to check the decision, use the right review route and understand what happens to their next payment.
