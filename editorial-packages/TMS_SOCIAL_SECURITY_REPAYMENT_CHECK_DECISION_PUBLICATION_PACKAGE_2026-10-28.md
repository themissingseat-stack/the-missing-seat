## The Short Version

- What happened: On September 1, 2026, the Social Security Administration's inspector general reported that 58 of 126 sampled retirement, survivor and disability insurance overpayment notices from fiscal year 2023 (46 percent) did not follow SSA policy. In one case SSA recovered a debt from the wrong beneficiary's benefits. SSA agreed to six recommendations.
- Who it affects: People who receive Social Security retirement, survivor or disability insurance (SSDI) benefits and get a letter saying they owe money. People on Supplemental Security Income (SSI) have different rules, and the audit did not cover them. The audit is a sample of 2023 notices, not a count of today's debts.
- What you can do: Decide which request fits your situation. Dispute the debt or amount (reconsideration), ask SSA to excuse it (waiver), or accept it and ask for a lower deduction (recovery rate). Keep proof of what you send, and ask SSA what happens to recovery while your request is pending.
- Based on: the SSA Office of Inspector General audit report 152407 and its September 4 news release, and SSA's Program Operations Manual sections on overpayment notices, reconsideration, waivers and recovery rates.

## Full editorial

In one of the cases Social Security's own inspector general reviewed, the agency sent an overpayment notice to the wrong person. The individual who actually owed the money never received it. SSA then recovered the debt from a different beneficiary's benefits, which the auditors said "created a burden for the beneficiary from whom SSA improperly withheld benefits." That was one case among 126, reported in a document dated September 1, 2026, and it is not typical of the sample. It does show what can happen when a recovery starts before anyone has confirmed who is being asked to pay.

The audit itself was broader. Auditors drew a random sample of 126 overpayments in Social Security retirement, survivors and disability insurance that SSA established in fiscal year 2023 and checked each notice against the agency's own policy. Fifty-eight, or 46 percent, fell short. Thirty lacked required information, such as why the overpayment happened, how SSA calculated it, or the person's reconsideration and waiver rights. Twenty-six had inaccurate or conflicting information. For eight, the auditors found no evidence that a notice was ever issued to the liable person. Some notices had more than one problem, so those counts do not add up to 58. The inspector general projects about 469,000 such notices out of roughly one million overpayments that year (a range of about 392,000 to 548,000 by our calculation from the report's Appendix B limits, at 90 percent confidence).

A second sample covered 125 cases coded as requests to explain a notice. SSA mishandled 79 of them, mostly through internal errors: in 56 the explanation code was used for other reasons, in 22 required notes were missing, and in 8 the explanation notice was deficient. Cases can have more than one error. As of June 2026, 11 of the 125 had been open more than 900 days. The inspector general notes that recovery is suspended while a request is pending, that no standard timeframe exists, and that the wait was not contrary to SSA policy, though delay can hinder later recovery.

Those numbers have limits that matter to anyone holding a letter. The audit covered one year of retirement, survivors and disability insurance notices, not Supplemental Security Income (SSI), and tested whether notices followed SSA policy. It did not test whether the debts were right. A defective notice is not proof that nothing was owed, and 46 percent is not the share of today's debts that are invalid. If you receive SSI, ask SSA which rules apply to you.

SSA's operating manual, in the version effective September 30, 2026, says an overpayment notice must show the total amount and how it arose: the monthly amount you were paid, the monthly amount you should have received, why those differ and the months involved. It must state your right to ask for reconsideration and your right to ask for a waiver, and describe repayment options. Form SSA-3105, which explains appeal, waiver and repayment options, is supposed to accompany the initial notice. If your letter leaves out any of that, say so when you contact SSA.

### Three problems, three requests

If you believe you were not overpaid, or the amount is wrong, the request is reconsideration. SSA's manual says the request must be in writing and made within 60 days of the date of the initial notice. The regulation counts 60 days from when you receive it, presumed five days after the date on the notice, so act within 60 days of the notice date. A later request needs a good-cause decision. The manual directs staff to stop recovery when a reconsideration request comes in, and says the recovery date must be at least 30 days after the notice date. A response within 30 days means staff must stop collection, and an SSA policy message effective August 28, 2025 says staff should set the recovery date so there are at least 60 days to appeal before withholding begins.

A waiver asks something different: that SSA not collect a debt that may be accurate. Under the manual (updated June 11, 2026), SSA waives recovery only if you were not at fault, and either repaying would leave you unable to meet basic living costs or would be unfair, or the debt is small (generally $2,000 or less). You can ask for a waiver at any time, including after recovery has begun, in writing on Form SSA-632-BK. A verbal request is accepted only where the small-amount provision allows approval; for larger debts, staff complete the form with you by phone. SSA must stop recovery when it receives a waiver request. A waiver and a reconsideration can be requested together, but SSA decides the reconsideration first.

A lower rate is for people who accept the debt and cannot pay it at the proposed pace. For notices dated on or after April 25, 2025, SSA's policy message sets a default withholding of 50 percent of the monthly benefit; earlier notices keep 10 percent unless a new overpayment arises. SSI recovery stays at 10 percent. Check the date and the rate on your letter, and if the rate is too high, ask for less, unless SSA finds fraud or similar fault. As of the manual's October 2024 text, staff first try to negotiate repayment within 12 months, with payments as low as $10 a month and no income questions, and otherwise within 60 months, which requires information about your income and expenses.

None of this replaces advice on a specific case. Different benefit types and later appeal stages have different rules, and a qualified benefits adviser or legal aid office can tell you which one applies to you.

### Who holds the power

SSA calculates the debt, writes the notice, controls the record and starts recovery. A beneficiary can supply evidence and ask for review but cannot repair the agency's files or rewrite its templates. That is why the audit's recommendations matter: use templates that include the rights language and Form SSA-3105, add checks before manually prepared notices go out, review system-generated notices for consistent content, and tighten how staff handle explanation requests. SSA agreed to implement all six. The inspector general asked for a corrective action plan within 60 days, and TMS could not confirm on November 15 whether one has been published or whether any change has reached the notices people receive.

There is a fair case on the other side. Social Security has to recover money paid beyond what the law allows, and that protects the program and the people who rely on it.  An agency mistake does not erase every overpayment, and hardship does not guarantee a waiver. But collecting a valid debt and being able to explain it are connected responsibilities.

## Pull Up A Chair

Keep the envelope and the notice. Write down the date on the letter, the date it reached you and any recovery date it proposes. Read it against the list above: amount paid, amount due, reason, months, and your reconsideration and waiver rights.

Then match the request to your situation. Facts wrong: reconsideration, in writing, saying what is wrong. Facts right, not at fault, real hardship: waiver. Debt accepted but the deduction too large: a lower rate, with your monthly expenses ready. Unsure: ask SSA to explain the calculation, and still act within 60 days of the notice date, since the inspector general describes an explanation request as a step before reconsideration, waiver or a lower rate, not a replacement for them.

Contact SSA through the phone number or office on the notice, or through your online account, and ask three things: which request it has recorded, whether recovery is paused while that request is decided, and when you can expect an answer. Write down the date, the name or ID of the person you spoke to, and any confirmation number. If you are helping a parent or spouse, use the proper authority to act for them and protect their personal information.

Free or low-cost help exists. Legal aid offices and benefits advisers often help with overpayment appeals. If SSA's explanation does not add up, ask a congressional office to inquire on your behalf.

## Final Thought

Before any money is withheld, or while it is, read the letter against the list above, match your situation to one of the three requests and keep a dated record of what you send and who you speak to. A claim the agency makes should come with the number worked out.
