## Website editorial

Theodor Wonja Michael was already an adult with a family when an advertisement offered him a route back into education. According to his account in a 2015 Hans-Böckler-Stiftung profile, the Hamburg academy did not require an Abitur, the school-leaving qualification usually associated with university entry. He could take an entrance examination, and study support was available through the Stiftung Mitbestimmung, a predecessor of the foundation. He passed the examination and began studying in 1958.

That is a useful place to enter his story. It allows us to see a man making a decision about his future, and an institution making a different decision about how to recognize his ability. His talent did not appear when someone finally agreed to admit him. The route changed what he could do with it.

Michael was born in Berlin in 1925 to a Cameroonian father and a German mother. The Historisches Museum Frankfurt describes how Nazi exclusion obstructed his schooling and apprenticeship opportunities. He later worked as an actor and editor and entered the federal intelligence service, the BND. The same museum now includes his life in its biographical displays. He died in Cologne in 2019. This is German history, with a named person at its center.

It would be dishonest to turn that sequence into a comforting story in which perseverance made racism irrelevant. His later work did not undo the earlier exclusion. But reducing him to what was done to him would leave out his choices, knowledge and contribution. Both shortcuts make the life smaller than the record.

## Who made room, and what changed?

The foundation's profile places his studies at the Hamburg academy between 1958 and 1960, followed by further study in Paris. It describes his subsequent journalism and editorship of the Afrika Bulletin before his BND appointment in 1971. This is a documented individual path. It is not an evaluation proving that every recipient of study support had the same result, or that one scholarship alone explains an entire career.

What we can identify is the mechanism. An admissions process allowed an applicant without the usual school certificate to demonstrate readiness. Financial support accompanied the opportunity. Michael still had to qualify and do the work. The institution could assess ability without treating an earlier educational barrier as a permanent verdict.

That matters to an ordinary reader who has met a capable colleague with an interrupted education, or who has been that person. A missing credential can reflect many circumstances. It does not establish the reason in any individual case, and it does not prove that every entry requirement is unfair. It should make us ask whether the requirement measures what the course or job actually needs.

There is a serious counterargument. Universities and professional programs need standards. Opening an alternative route without preparation or support can leave people carrying tuition costs and disappointment. Employers also need to know that a qualification means something. Michael's example supports assessing readiness through another route. It does not support pretending that readiness does not matter.

The people who hold power today are the admissions bodies, funders and policymakers deciding which routes exist and how applicants can use them. They should explain the purpose of their requirements and the evidence accepted in place of a conventional certificate. The burden falls on applicants when the alternative is technically available but its cost, preparation or instructions are impossible to navigate. That is a risk to examine locally, not a national failure rate established by this biography.

## Preserve the work, not just the lesson

There is a second practical decision worth recognizing. In a release dated January 20, 2021, DOMiD announced that part of Michael's estate was entering its collection. Documents, autobiographical writing, photographs and everyday objects were included. With the family's agreement, roughly 150 books went to the developing library bearing his name. The acquisition is an established event, not news that happened this week.

An archive preserves the evidence needed to tell a fuller life. A book can show what someone read. A draft can show how an argument developed. A working object can connect the public name to ordinary effort. Those possibilities are reasons to preserve material; they do not mean every object is available to every visitor or that we have inspected the collection ourselves.

DOMiD and the community library initiative made room in different ways. One preserved a research record; the other received books for a community resource. Frankfurt's museum gives visitors another route into the story. These are concrete acts of recognition. We have not measured how many readers changed their views or how many students benefited, and a responsible positive story should not invent those outcomes.

The beneficiary is not only the person whose name survives. Readers gain a record they can question and use. Families gain a better chance of seeing their history treated as history. Researchers gain material that can complicate a familiar national account. The institutions also gain collections and public value. That comes with a responsibility to credit the family, community work and sources behind the display.

## Make the next action small enough to do

A teacher or reading group can begin with Michael's account and one institutional source, then follow the educational sequence: the earlier obstruction, the examination, the support, the study and the subsequent work. Explain which parts come from his recollection and which are documented by the institutions preserving his life. Do not turn one man's path into the experience of every Black German.

A local library can check whether his memoir, *Deutsch sein und schwarz dazu*, is available to readers and consider an acquisition or interlibrary loan. Check the catalog and applicable lending terms rather than promising immediate access. A school or college can publish one clear guide to its own alternative entry routes, stating eligibility, assessment, preparation, costs and where applicants can get advice. Michael's historical route does not establish anyone's eligibility under today's rules.

If your family holds papers or recordings, contact a suitable archive before donating them. Ask about consent, privacy, ownership, cataloguing and future access. Preserving a life should not require relatives to surrender control without understanding the terms.

The missing seat is a person allowed to be more than evidence of somebody else's cruelty. Michael deserves to be encountered as a student, worker and author as well as a witness to persecution. Remembering the opportunity he used also gives institutions a practical question to answer now: how can a capable person demonstrate readiness when the usual route has been closed?
