# WEBSITE EDITORIAL

At the end of a restaurant shift, the customers leave. The server is still there, wiping tables, restocking the station, making coffee for the next shift or cleaning up before closing. Some of that work produces no tip. But the employer may still be allowed to count the worker's tips toward the legal minimum wage for those hours.

That is the part of American wage law many people do not understand. The question is not simply whether a server is carrying plates or holding a mop. It is whether the worker is doing duties that belong to a tipped occupation or working in a separate, non-tipped occupation. That distinction can change what the employer must pay directly.

On October 19, 2026, the U.S. Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division withdrew three older opinion letters about this issue. The same bulletin confirmed that the old federal “80/20” percentage guidance is no longer the agency's policy. The bulletin matters because the old letters were still available and could be mistaken for current guidance. But this was **not** the day the 80/20 rule first disappeared. A federal appeals court set aside the department's 2021 version in 2024, and the department removed the related handbook guidance in January 2025. [1]

The question for a worker is more immediate than that history: What does this mean for the hours I actually work and the paycheck I take home?

## The rule that many workers thought they had

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, covered workers generally must receive at least the federal minimum wage, currently $7.25 an hour. Employers that meet the legal conditions for a “tip credit” may pay a tipped worker as little as $2.13 an hour directly and count up to $5.12 an hour in actual tips toward that federal minimum. If the tips do not bridge the gap in a workweek, the employer must make up the difference. Overtime and other conditions still apply. [1][2]

For years, federal guidance also used a percentage test for work that supported a tipped occupation but did not itself produce tips. Under the 2021 rule, an employer could not take a tip credit for that supporting work once it exceeded 20 percent of the workweek or continued for more than 30 minutes at a time. The court rejected that task-and-time approach, concluding that it did not match the statute's focus on the employee's occupation. [1]

The distinction is easy to miss. A waiter who cleans and sets tables may still be doing work related to waiting tables. A hotel employee who works one part of the shift as a waiter and another part as a maintenance worker may be performing two separate occupations. Under the current federal dual-jobs regulation, the employer cannot use a tip credit for hours spent in that separate non-tipped maintenance occupation. [1]

So the withdrawal does not mean an employer may call every task “server work” and pay whatever it likes. It means the former automatic percentage cutoff is no longer the federal Wage and Hour Division's enforcement test. The actual occupation and the relationship of the duties matter.

## Why the dates matter

The department's October bulletin is a policy clarification with a real practical consequence: workers, employers and investigators should not rely on the three withdrawn letters or the former 80/20 handbook language as current federal enforcement guidance. It did not enact a new statute, erase the federal minimum wage, or eliminate the separate-occupation rule. [1]

There is a reasonable legal argument behind the court's decision. A restaurant employee's occupation includes preparation and cleanup, not just the seconds in which a customer leaves a tip. A rule that treats every non-tip-producing task as outside the occupation can draw artificial lines. The court found the 2021 rule inconsistent with the law, and the department restored the older occupation-based regulation. [1]

There is also a serious worker-side concern. Without a simple time threshold, someone working long stretches of preparation and cleanup may have a harder time knowing whether the employer is stretching the tipped occupation too far. That is an accountability concern, not proof that a particular restaurant has underpaid anyone.

And federal law is not the only law. Some states require tipped workers to receive the full state minimum wage before tips. Others permit a tip credit but set a higher direct cash wage than the federal $2.13. A worker who checks only the federal bulletin may miss the stronger protection that actually applies in their state or city. [3]

## Who benefits, and who carries the burden?

Employers gain a clearer federal enforcement position that does not require them to calculate the former 20 percent and 30-minute limits for related duties within a tipped occupation. Restaurants also have a legitimate interest in knowing which ordinary opening, service and closing tasks are part of a server's job. That is the strongest case for the clarification.

But the financial advantage of a lawful tip credit belongs to the employer: it can count part of the customer's tip toward the minimum-wage obligation instead of paying the entire minimum in direct wages. The worker still performs the time. The customer supplies the tip. Whether the arrangement is legal depends on the statutory conditions and the applicable state or local rule.

The person carrying the risk is the worker whose time and tasks are classified by someone else. If an employer treats a genuinely separate non-tipped job as tipped work, fails to make up a minimum-wage shortfall, mishandles tips or calculates overtime incorrectly, the worker can lose wages. Those are possible violations with their own tests; this bulletin does not establish that they occurred in any particular workplace. [1][2]

The department has not supplied a count of workers who will lose money because of the October 19 withdrawal. We should not invent one. Nor should we assume every restaurant benefits or every server is harmed. The measurable question is whether enforcement and pay records will show what workers actually did and what they were paid.

## The receipt

The documentary sequence is clear. In 2024, the Fifth Circuit vacated the department's 2021 dual-jobs rule. In January 2025, Wage and Hour removed the 80/20 guidance from its handbook. On October 19, 2026, the division withdrew the 1979, 1980 and 1985 opinion letters and explicitly confirmed that specific time limits on duties within a tipped occupation no longer reflect its policy. [1]

The department's own tipped-worker guidance still says an employer must meet the conditions for a tip credit, pay the required direct cash wage, make up any minimum-wage shortfall, follow tip rules and calculate overtime properly. Its state table shows why a worker must check the place where they actually work. [2][3]

That is the actual receipt: a federal percentage guide is gone, but a wage claim does not disappear merely because the old percentage test no longer applies.

## The recipe: check the work, not just the job title

A server who suspects a problem should start by keeping a dated record of when each shift began and ended, what tasks were performed, what direct wages were paid and what tips were received or distributed. Save schedules, pay stubs, tip reports and relevant written instructions. A description such as “I worked as a server” is less useful than a record showing which hours were spent serving, preparing the station, cleaning tables or doing a separate maintenance or kitchen job.

Next, check the current tipped-wage rules for the state and locality. Ask whether the employer takes a tip credit, what cash wage it pays, whether actual tips bring pay to the required minimum, and whether overtime was calculated correctly. If the employer assigns a separate non-tipped occupation, ask how those hours are classified and paid. Do not assume that crossing 20 percent, by itself, proves a federal violation under the current guidance. [1][2][3]

A worker can contact the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division at **1-866-487-9243** or through its official complaint page. The department says complaints are generally confidential, there is no charge to file, and federal law protects covered workers against retaliation for exercising protected wage rights. The usual federal FLSA limitations period is two years, or three for willful violations, so waiting can matter. State claims may have different rules and deadlines. [4][5]

Coworkers can compare experiences and, where protected by law, raise common pay concerns together. A union or qualified employment adviser can help identify whether the dispute concerns the tip credit, a separate job, overtime, tip pooling or a stronger state rule. Those are not all the same claim.

A restaurant owner can act too: review job assignments and payroll instructions against the current rule and applicable state law, and explain in writing how the business treats separate non-tipped duties. Accurate records protect a lawful employer as well as the person receiving the paycheck.

The useful question is not whether the worker once heard about an 80/20 rule. It is whether the employer can explain the actual legal basis for the pay, show the hours and duties it counted, and correct any shortfall. A wage protection that workers cannot identify or test is a protection that may never reach the paycheck.

**Die Kellnerin putzt noch. Die alte 80/20-Regel gilt auf Bundesebene nicht mehr.**

Nach der letzten Bestellung ist die Arbeit im Restaurant oft nicht vorbei. Tische reinigen, auffüllen, vorbereiten, abschließen – vieles davon bringt kein Trinkgeld.

In den USA dürfen Arbeitgeber unter bestimmten Voraussetzungen trotzdem Trinkgeld auf einen Teil des gesetzlichen Mindestlohns anrechnen. Auf Bundesebene beträgt der Mindestlohn 7,25 Dollar pro Stunde; bei rechtmäßig genutzter Trinkgeldanrechnung kann der direkte Stundenlohn nur 2,13 Dollar betragen. Reicht das Trinkgeld nicht, muss der Arbeitgeber die Differenz ausgleichen.

Am **19. Oktober 2026** hat das US-Arbeitsministerium drei ältere Auskünfte zu solchen Tätigkeiten zurückgezogen. Es bestätigt außerdem: Die frühere **80/20-Leitlinie**, nach der bestimmte Nebentätigkeiten zeitlich begrenzt waren, ist keine aktuelle Bundesleitlinie mehr.

Wichtig: Das begann **nicht erst letzte Woche**. Ein Bundesberufungsgericht hob die Regelung von 2021 bereits 2024 auf. Die Behörde entfernte die zugehörige Handbuchregel Anfang 2025.

Was gilt weiterhin? Entscheidend ist unter anderem, ob die Tätigkeit zum Trinkgeldberuf gehört oder ob jemand tatsächlich einen zweiten, eigenständigen Beruf ohne Trinkgeld ausübt. Für Letzteren darf der Arbeitgeber die Trinkgeldanrechnung nicht einfach verwenden. Außerdem haben manche Bundesstaaten strengere Lohnregeln.

**Was kannst du tun?** Schreibe deine Arbeitszeiten und Aufgaben auf, bewahre Lohnabrechnungen und Trinkgeldaufzeichnungen auf und prüfe die Regeln deines Bundesstaates. Bei Verdacht auf Unterbezahlung kannst du die Wage and Hour Division kostenlos kontaktieren: **1-866-487-9243**. Beschwerden sind nach Angaben der Behörde grundsätzlich vertraulich; Fristen können entscheidend sein.

Hier geht es nicht darum, jeden Restaurantbetreiber zu beschuldigen. Es geht darum, dass Beschäftigte wissen müssen, **wie ihre Stunden tatsächlich bezahlt werden dürfen** – und wie sie eine falsche Abrechnung überprüfen lassen.
