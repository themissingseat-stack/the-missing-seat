## Website editorial

When most people hear the word homelessness, they picture a shelter bed, a tent or somebody sleeping in a car. Those are real forms of homelessness. They are not the only ways a person can lack a safe and stable place to live.

In some Tribal communities, the emergency may be inside a house that already looks occupied. A family stays with relatives because there is nowhere else to go. Another household joins them. The arrangement prevents people from sleeping outside, but it can also put fifteen or more people in one home.

That is called doubling up. It can be an act of care, a practical response to a housing shortage, or both. It can also hide the size of the housing problem from a program whose rules do not necessarily count the household as homeless.

A Government Accountability Office testimony released September 16, 2026 examined this mismatch as part of ongoing work. All nineteen Tribes and Tribal organizations in its homelessness interviews described examples of doubling up. Some described fifteen or more people living in one home. GAO also found examples of people living without shelter, in vehicles, in broken-down trailers and in emergency shelters. [1][2]

Those interviews were deliberately selected and are not a national survey. They do not tell us how many Native people are homeless across the country. GAO called its observations preliminary. Those limits matter. So does what the testimony found.

## The need can be real even when the category does not fit

HUD's Continuum of Care program is its largest homelessness-assistance program. It can fund housing and services, and Tribes and tribally designated housing entities became eligible to participate through a 2021 law. As of 2026, GAO reported that nine Continuums of Care included Tribal areas within their boundaries. [2]

CoC-funded projects must serve people who meet the program's definition of homeless. GAO said that definition does not necessarily include people who are doubled up or living in overcrowded conditions. Some Tribal representatives told GAO that common forms of homelessness in their communities may therefore leave them less competitive for funding. [2]

The word “necessarily” is important. Federal programs use different definitions, and a doubled-up household may qualify under some circumstances or other programs. This article does not claim that every person sharing housing is legally homeless, or that multigenerational living is itself a problem. GAO noted that multigenerational households can reflect Tribal culture. The question is whether the arrangement is chosen and stable, or forced by a housing shortage with no safe alternative.

That distinction should be made with the community, not assumed from a crowded-room count.

GAO identified other barriers too. Some Tribal areas overlap with two or three Continuums of Care, creating multiple policies, meetings and travel demands. Some Tribes were unfamiliar with the program. Others worried about competing against longtime providers or harming local partnerships. The federal government opened the door to Tribal participation, but the route through it can still require time, staff and expertise that smaller housing programs do not have. [2]

## The same dollar already has another job

Many of the Tribal organizations GAO contacted used Indian Housing Block Grant money for emergency assistance, shelters or transitional housing. They also needed that money to maintain existing homes. When the roof, heating system and aging housing stock already need attention, every dollar assigned to an emergency bed is a dollar unavailable for keeping another home habitable.

Temporary pandemic funding helped some communities operate shelters, but one Alaska Tribe told GAO it did not know how it would continue a seasonal overnight shelter after that funding expired. This is not proof that every Tribal shelter faces closure. It shows the practical problem with building a continuing service on temporary money. [2]

The missing seat belongs to the person whose housing crisis does not look like the picture on the form. It belongs to the family offering a couch or a floor because the alternative is outside. It also belongs to Tribal governments asked to maintain old housing, build new housing and respond to homelessness from the same limited pool.

Power sits with Congress, HUD, Continuums of Care and the officials who design definitions, applications and funding priorities. Tribal governments carry sovereign responsibilities and local knowledge, but many still have to fit their needs into systems designed elsewhere.

The benefit of a narrow definition is administrative consistency. Programs need eligibility rules, and limited funds require choices. The burden appears when consistency makes a common local form of housing instability harder to document, serve or fund. A rule can be neutral on paper and still miss the emergency in front of it.

## Government-to-government should mean a usable answer

GAO found another unresolved question. One Tribal organization and a Continuum of Care developed a proposal for twenty-four efficiency homes and a community center. The Tribe did not proceed because it believed the project could not prioritize Tribal citizens.

According to GAO, HUD offices gave conflicting information in 2026 about how fair-housing and nondiscrimination requirements apply to a Tribal preference in the CoC program. One group of officials said the agency had not yet decided. Another said the requirements prohibit that preference. HUD had not reconciled those positions as of September 11, 2026. GAO said it would continue examining the issue. [2]

That is not a finding that HUD broke the law, and it is not proof that the proposed project would have been approved with different guidance. It is a documented case in which a Tribal housing proposal met an unanswered federal question. A sovereign government should be able to receive one clear, written answer before it spends scarce staff time building an application.

An ordinary person should care because definitions decide who becomes visible to public systems. We need rules that distinguish a chosen shared household from a family with no stable alternative. We also need funding decisions that recognize distance, staffing and housing shortages without turning every difference into an excuse for inaction.

Congress should ask HUD to publish a single, written explanation of how CoC eligibility applies to doubled-up and overcrowded households, including the relevant exceptions, and how Tribal preferences interact with program requirements. HUD should develop that guidance through formal consultation with Tribal governments and then train the Continuums of Care that cover Tribal areas.

Local readers can take one practical step: find the Continuum of Care for their area and ask whether Tribal governments within its boundaries have a voting seat, a named contact and a written route for documenting housing instability that may not appear in the annual street count. The answer should be public without exposing the identities of families seeking help.

GAO's final work may add evidence or recommendations. Until then, the responsible conclusion is limited but clear. The federal government has recognized Tribal eligibility for homelessness funding. It still needs to make sure the definitions, instructions and partnerships can see the people that eligibility was meant to serve.

A full house can still contain people without a home of their own. Public policy should be able to understand the difference.
