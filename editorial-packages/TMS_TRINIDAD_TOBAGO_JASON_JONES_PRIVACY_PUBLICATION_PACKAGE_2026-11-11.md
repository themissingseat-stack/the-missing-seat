## Full editorial

Jason Jones went to court over what the government could make a crime in his private life. The court’s account describes him as a gay man from Trinidad and Tobago who had experienced harassment, discrimination and violence because of his sexual orientation. On November 10, the country’s final court of appeal ruled unanimously in his favour. The judges said two sections of the Sexual Offences Act could not be read to criminalise private, consensual sexual relations between adults of the same sex. They dismissed the Attorney General’s cross-appeal. [1, 2]

Even with no prosecutions identified in the judgment for the private adult conduct at issue, a criminal law can change how a person lives. The possibility of punishment sits on one side of the decision about whom to trust or what to say. The judges recognised that keeping such a law on the books could itself intrude on private life. The effect of this ruling will be tested by what happens when people need police, prosecutors or other authorities to act on it. [1, paragraphs 60–64 and 75–79]

## How the old law got back into the case

The case is often described as a fight over a colonial law. There was a colonial predecessor: the Offences Against the Person Act of 1925. But the provisions before the court were enacted by Trinidad and Tobago’s Parliament in 1986, after independence. They changed the reach of the offences and increased penalties. The 1986 law’s exception for certain private, consensual conduct between a man and a woman did not extend the same protection to same-sex adults. The people exposed to criminal liability bore that difference. [1, paragraphs 9–15 and 26–35]

Jones won in the High Court in 2018. The Court of Appeal majority later held that a constitutional rule meant to preserve certain old laws required substitution of older provisions. The state’s case was that the 1986 provisions largely continued the same offences. The Privy Council disagreed. In the judges’ reading, Parliament had done more than carry forward the earlier law: it had created a new scheme that was open to constitutional review. [1, paragraphs 19–35]

The government had another argument. Trinidad and Tobago’s Constitution lets Parliament pass certain laws despite their inconsistency with rights provisions, provided the law says so and clears a supermajority in both houses. There is a limit: the law still fails if shown not to be reasonably justifiable in a society that properly respects individual rights. The court accepted that the 1986 Act had followed the special voting procedure. It held that the restrictions at issue still failed that test. The vote cleared the threshold. The criminal restriction still had to be justified. [1, paragraphs 52–81]

## The decision and the order

The conclusion is clear about what the challenged sections cannot criminalise. The court did not delete both sections in full. Jones’s lawyer asked it to do so, arguing that other criminal provisions already address conduct the state can properly punish. The judges provisionally favoured the High Court’s narrower remedy and gave counsel the opportunity to make further written submissions. No separate final order was visible on the case page when this article was checked. [1, paragraphs 85–89; 2]

This is a concrete gain for people whose private lives were subject to those provisions. It does not establish whether police or prosecutors have changed their guidance, whether any record will be cleared, or what will happen under other laws. The final order will clarify the court’s remedy. Evidence of what they actually do will have to come later.
