LAND, OWNERSHIP & GENERATIONAL WEALTH • JULY 27, 2026

The Farmers Bringing Land Back to Black Communities

Across the United States, Black farmers and land organizations are not simply reclaiming acreage. They are rebuilding the power to remain.

Seat Affected: Secure access to land and the power to keep it
Seat Status: Being rebuilt, while historic vulnerabilities remain
Location: United States
Theme: Agriculture, property, community and repair

For generations, land has meant more than acreage to Black families.

It has meant food, housing, work, independence, inheritance and a place where another person could not casually order you to leave.

That is why Black land loss has never been only an agricultural statistic.

And it is why today’s efforts to reclaim, protect and collectively steward land deserve attention.

More Than One Movement

There is no single “Black land reclamation” organization.

The work takes several forms.

Some communities are buying rural property collectively and building intentional communities centered on farming, ecological stewardship, culture and mutual support.

In California, Black to the Land is developing an off-grid community where residents share work and steward 189 acres. Other Black- and Indigenous-led intentional communities use collective governance and land-based skills to create places of cultural survival and greater self-reliance.

Some organizations focus on keeping families from losing land they already own.

Heirs’ property—land inherited by multiple relatives without a clear, court-recognized title—can leave families vulnerable to forced sales, tax problems and exclusion from loans or assistance. The Center for Heirs’ Property works with families in South Carolina and East Texas through legal services, education and sustainable forestry.

And some efforts grow from a much older model.

New Communities was founded in Georgia in 1969 by civil-rights organizers including Charles and Shirley Sherrod. Created for Black farmers and families facing retaliation and displacement, it held thousands of acres collectively and became a pioneering community land trust in the United States.

These projects are different. One creates new community. One defends inherited property. One offers a historic model of collective ownership.

Together they answer a common question:

How can people keep a relationship to land when ordinary market forces, unclear title, discrimination and concentrated wealth repeatedly make that relationship fragile?

Why the Land Was Lost

Black farmers and landowners did not lose property for one reason.

Mechanisms included discriminatory lending and federal program administration, violence and intimidation, partition sales, tax foreclosure, lack of access to legal services, migration, economic pressure and heirs’-property complications.

Personal decisions also mattered, as they do in every community. But describing every loss as an individual failure ignores the institutions that determined who received credit, protection, information and time.

The same financial problem has different consequences depending on the resources behind the owner.

A wealthy family can hire lawyers, clear title, pay taxes during a crisis, wait out a bad market or refuse a developer’s offer.

A land-rich but cash-poor family may own something valuable and still lack the money necessary to protect it.

Wealth does not make people immune to law or loss. It buys insulation from consequences that become emergencies for everyone else.

Land Is Not Automatically Freedom

Owning land does not guarantee prosperity.

Farmers still face debt, climate risk, equipment costs, unstable markets and the physical demands of agricultural work. Collective communities must still govern fairly. Romantic language about “returning to the land” can hide how hard the work is.

But land can create options.

It can support housing, food production, conservation, education, business, cultural practice and an inheritance that does not disappear when rent rises.

Reclamation is therefore not a retreat from modern life.

It is an attempt to build enough stability to participate in modern life without being permanently at its mercy.

Who Is Missing?

Families whose names are on the history of a place but no longer on its deeds.

Farmers who have knowledge and commitment but cannot obtain affordable land or capital.

Heirs who jointly own property but lack clear title, legal guidance or the cash to protect it.

And young people who were taught that leaving rural land was progress, but never shown how land could become power if supported and protected.

One Action

If your family owns land, begin the uncomfortable conversation before a crisis: locate the deed, confirm property taxes, create a will, identify every legal heir and seek qualified local legal advice.

If your family does not own land, support organizations that provide heirs’-property legal services, farmer training, community-land ownership or emergency tax assistance.

Do not wait until an auction notice makes the decision for everyone.

Return to the Seat

The Missing Seat will return to the communities and organizations doing this work.

We will ask whether families retained title, whether collective projects remained sustainable, whether public policy reduced forced loss, and whether new farmers gained real—not symbolic—access to land and capital.

Reclamation does not undo every acre taken or lost.

But every family that clears a title, every farmer who gains secure access and every community that keeps land from becoming another extraction opportunity changes what can be inherited.

The seat is not simply at the table.

Sometimes the seat is the ground beneath it.

Verification Notice

This editorial distinguishes intentional communities, heirs’-property preservation and community land trusts; they are related approaches, not one unified program. The account of current intentional communities relies partly on reported interviews. Readers should investigate governance, finances and legal structure before contributing to or joining any land project.
