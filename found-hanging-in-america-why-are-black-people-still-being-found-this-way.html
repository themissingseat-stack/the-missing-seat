Found Hanging in America: Why Are Black People Still Being Found This Way?

More Black people have been reported found hanging. Congress is asking for federal review. And in Mississippi, investigators now say a woman found hanging from a tree had already been killed before her body was placed there. The question is not whether every case is a lynching. It is whether America has a system capable of finding out what actually happened.

Seat Affected: The right to a credible, evidence-based investigation of unexplained death
Seat Status: Fragmented and under renewed scrutiny
Theme: Black deaths, investigations, national data, racial memory and public trust

In August, The Missing Seat wrote about Black families being asked to trust early conclusions when people they loved were found dead under circumstances that immediately raised questions. We were careful then, and we need to be careful now: being found hanging does not mean a person was lynched. A family’s suspicion is not proof of murder. A police statement is not automatically a medical finding. And several disturbing deaths do not become a conspiracy simply because they resemble one another.

But the record has changed since that story was published. More Black people have been reported found hanging. Members of Congress are now asking the Justice Department and FBI to investigate these deaths systematically. And in Mississippi, one death that initially presented to the public as a Black woman hanging from a tree has now been ruled a homicide. Investigators say Tasia Fortune was already dead when her body was hung there.

That matters. Not because it proves every suspicious hanging is a lynching. It proves something more basic: the first appearance of a death can be wrong, and getting the investigation right matters.

What Changed

On September 10, Representative Ayanna Pressley and 59 other members of Congress asked the Justice Department and FBI for what they called a thorough, transparent federal investigation into hanging deaths involving Black people. Their letter said at least ten such deaths had been reported in 2026.

That number needs a warning label. It is a claim in a congressional request for investigation. It does not mean ten murders occurred. It does not mean ten lynchings occurred. Some deaths may ultimately be determined to be suicide. Others may remain undetermined. Some families dispute official conclusions.

But Congress asked for something remarkably concrete: independent autopsies with family permission, audits of local and state investigations, review of physical and testimonial evidence, reports explaining federal findings, and systematic national data collection.

In other words: stop asking the public to assemble the national picture from scattered local news stories.

Then Came Tasia Fortune

Tasia Fortune was 29 years old and a mother of four. Her body was found hanging from a tree behind a vacant house in Jackson, Mississippi, on August 3.

The image immediately carried historical weight. Mississippi does not have the luxury of pretending a Black body hanging from a tree is culturally neutral. But history is context. It is not evidence of who killed someone.

Now investigators say Fortune was killed before she was hung from that tree.

A Jackson police detective testified that the medical examiner concluded the hanging was staged. Police have described the apparent motive as connected to a drug dispute. Three men have been arrested and charged with murder. Fortune’s mother has said she does not believe race motivated her daughter’s killing.

Those facts matter too. We should not turn Fortune’s death into a racial hate crime when the evidence currently available does not establish one. But we should not miss what her case demonstrates simply because the apparent motive may not have been racial.

A woman found hanging was not, according to investigators, killed by hanging at all.

Someone allegedly killed her and created a scene that initially told the world something different from what investigators now believe happened. That is exactly why investigation must come before assumption.

And Other Cases Are Still Not Settled

On August 21, 38-year-old Demetrius Fleming was found dead in Roanoke Rapids, North Carolina. Police said the original call came in as an attempted suicide. But Police Chief Lawrence Wiggins publicly called it an open death investigation and said investigators were not working from a strict assumption about suicide, homicide or accident.

That is what responsible uncertainty sounds like. It does not promise a murder where none has been proved. It also does not close the door before the evidence has finished speaking.

There will be cases in which the evidence supports suicide. There may be cases in which families remain unconvinced. There may be deaths in which investigators never obtain enough evidence to answer every question.

The answer cannot be to declare all of them lynchings. The answer also cannot be to pretend that America’s history makes the public reaction irrational.

The History Is Not a Footnote

Thousands of Black Americans were lynched during the era of racial terror. Hanging became one of the country’s most recognizable symbols of racial domination because these killings were not merely private crimes. They were warnings.

That history does not tell a medical examiner what happened to a person in 2026. It does explain why the government carries an unusually heavy responsibility to investigate these deaths carefully and communicate its findings clearly.

If a death is suicide, show the evidence that supports that determination as far as the law and the family’s privacy permit. If it is homicide, investigate who did it and why. If the evidence is inconclusive, say that. And if the original conclusion turns out to be wrong, correct it publicly.

That is not special treatment. That is competent government.

America Has a Counting Problem

The congressional request exposes another problem: we do not have an easily accessible national system that lets the public answer basic questions about these cases.

How many Black Americans have been found hanging this year? How many were officially ruled suicide? How many were homicide? How many remain undetermined? How many conclusions were challenged by families? How many received independent autopsies? How many were reviewed by state or federal authorities?

Those categories matter because without them, a frightening number can circulate without telling us what the number actually means.

Ten reported hangings are not ten proven lynchings. But ten reports are enough to justify asking whether anybody in government is systematically counting, comparing and reviewing what is happening.

Who Benefited? Who Paid?

There is no responsible basis for claiming one group benefited from all of these deaths. The cases are too different.

But institutions benefit from weak accountability when fragmented records prevent the public from comparing outcomes. Local agencies face less outside scrutiny when every case remains isolated. Rumor merchants benefit when government leaves information vacuums large enough for speculation to become its own evidence.

Families pay for that failure. So do investigators who actually do careful work, because public confidence in good investigations gets damaged by the history of bad ones. And Black communities pay a psychological price every time an image associated with racial terror appears again and the country tells them not to remember what that image has meant.

Who Is Missing?

The families. Independent reviewers when legitimate questions remain. Medical evidence that can be understood rather than merely announced. National data capable of separating suicide, homicide, undetermined deaths and disputed findings.

And a government willing to recognize that trust is not something an institution can demand from the people it serves. Trust is something the evidence earns.

What We Can Do

Congress has already identified a practical first step: require systematic national data and transparent federal review standards for suspicious hanging deaths.

That does not mean federalizing every suicide investigation. It means creating a way to recognize patterns if they exist, distinguish cases that are not connected, and identify investigations that deserve another look.

Journalists and the public have responsibilities too. Stop turning “found hanging” into “lynched” before evidence supports it. Stop turning “police say suicide” into an unquestionable final fact when the investigation or medical findings are still incomplete. Label what is known, what authorities allege, what families dispute and what remains unresolved.

And keep the receipts. Because Tasia Fortune’s case has already shown why.

The body told one story when it was found. The investigation now tells another.

America owes every family enough investigation to know the difference.

Verification Notice

This editorial does not claim that the reported 2026 hanging deaths are connected or that they are all homicides or lynchings.
