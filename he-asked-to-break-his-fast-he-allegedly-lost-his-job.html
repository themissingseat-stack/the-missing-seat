JUSTICE & LAW • WORK • RELIGIOUS FREEDOM • RETALIATION

A workplace right means little if asking to use it may cost the worker a paycheck.

He Asked to Break His Fast. He Allegedly Lost His Job.

Seat Affected: Muslim workers and employees whose religious practices conflict with a work schedule
Location: New York City, United States
Theme: Scheduling power, religious accommodation and protection against retaliation

A worker should be able to ask a question about his schedule without wondering whether he has just asked himself out of a job. Most people understand that a business needs employees to show up. They also understand that a temporary adjustment is different from refusing to work. A fair workplace needs a way to tell those situations apart.

According to the EEOC, a Muslim barista at Hungry Ghost Coffee requested a temporary daytime schedule or voluntary shift swap in March 2023 so he could break his Ramadan fast at sunset. The agency alleged that his manager refused and fired him after his next shift. On October 6, 2026, the EEOC announced a $22,500 settlement and a two-year consent decree requiring policy review and training across the company's locations. Those are the agency's reported allegations and settlement terms, not trial findings establishing the employee's account. The release does not report an admission of liability.

That distinction matters. We can take a complaint seriously without pretending a settlement proves everything alleged. We can also examine the protection a workplace is supposed to provide without inventing a manager's motives, a company's finances or a conversation we did not hear.

A right you can actually use

Religious freedom at work becomes practical when someone needs the schedule, uniform or workplace rule to change. The question is no longer whether the company respects diversity in principle. It is whether the person controlling the shift knows how to consider a request fairly.

Under Title VII, covered employers must reasonably accommodate religious practice unless the accommodation would cause undue hardship. EEOC guidance identifies flexible schedules and voluntary swaps as possible solutions. That does not mean every requested arrangement must be granted. It means a conflict requires an assessment of what can work.

The Supreme Court clarified the standard in Groff v. DeJoy on June 29, 2023. A burden slightly greater than trivial is insufficient. The employer must show substantial increased costs in the context of its particular business. Size, operating costs and the proposed arrangement matter. The Court also explained that hostility toward religion cannot supply the hardship and that considering other options matters; simply rejecting one proposed arrangement may not finish the job.

Groff involved a Christian employee's Sunday observance. Its legal rule is not reserved for Christians. That is an ordinary but necessary point: equal protection for religious practice must remain useful when the person asking observes a faith different from our own.

The alleged Hungry Ghost events occurred before Groff was decided. This article does not claim that the manager had already received that ruling or that the settlement adjudicated compliance with it. The decision is relevant to the standard employers must understand today.

A café can have real staffing difficulties. A shift change might leave nobody trained to close, move work onto an unwilling colleague or create a serious operational cost. Those possibilities deserve evidence and consideration. This article does not establish that any occurred in this case. Saying a request is inconvenient is also different from showing the substantial business burden the law requires.

Who is missing?

The missing seat belongs to the worker whose religious practice is treated as a scheduling problem before the worker is treated as a participant in solving it. Other employees belong in that conversation too, especially when a solution affects their hours. A fair process should hear both needs without turning colleagues' resentment into a substitute for analysis.

The employer and schedule manager hold the immediate power: they can change hours, approve alternatives and make employment decisions. The worker carries the risk of losing wages or becoming known as someone who asks for exceptions. In general, managers benefit from predictable staffing, customers benefit from reliable service and workers benefit from dependable hours. None of that establishes a discriminatory financial benefit in this particular dispute.

The larger burden is easy to overlook. A person may know a right exists but still decide that rent, groceries and the next paycheck make asking too dangerous. That is an editorial inference about the stakes of workplace power, not a finding about this employee's finances or the prevalence of religious discrimination.

This should matter even to someone who is not religious. We all depend on institutions applying their rules consistently. A supervisor's discretion can make a written policy usable or leave it as something employees are afraid to test. Supporting someone else's ability to ask for a lawful adjustment does not require sharing their belief.

EEOC guidance treats a request for religious accommodation as protected activity. Retaliation means punishing someone because they asserted a protected right. That protection does not excuse unrelated misconduct or prevent legitimate discipline. It does require keeping the request itself out of the reasons for punishment.

The distinction protects the employer as well as the employee. A documented review can show a genuine constraint, the alternatives considered and the reason for a decision. An unexplained rejection followed by adverse action leaves everyone arguing later about something that should have been handled clearly at the time.

Receipts and a workable recipe

My position is straightforward: a workplace right should come with a process ordinary employees can use without needing to become employment lawyers. Employers should identify who receives accommodation requests, who reviews a denial and how employees can report retaliation. A second review before adverse action involving a recent requester is a sensible safeguard, not a requirement this article claims the decree imposed.

Workers can help make a request clear by explaining the work conflict, the adjustment needed and the relevant dates. Keeping a copy is useful. It should not become another way to blame the person asking. The employer still controls the workplace and is responsible for evaluating its obligations.

One realistic action is to ask your employer or HR department: “Where is our religious-accommodation process, and who can review a denial if the request went to my direct manager?” If you manage people, answer that question before the next request arrives. If you believe you experienced discrimination or retaliation, contact the EEOC or qualified local assistance promptly to check the applicable filing deadline rather than assuming an internal complaint stops the clock.

We do not need to promise that every schedule conflict has an easy solution. We do need to insist that asking for one is not treated as disloyalty. A right that can only be used by people able to risk their income leaves too many people outside the protection it promises.

Ally = Action. 🪑
