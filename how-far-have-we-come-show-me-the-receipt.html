Race, History & Accountability

How Far Have We Come? Show Me the Receipt.

America loves to celebrate racial progress. But removing a barrier, repairing the damage it caused and paying the outstanding account are three different things.

Seat Summary

Celebrate every person who breaks a barrier. But don't confuse that person's victory with the virtue of the institution that kept the barrier standing. If America wants credit for racial progress, it should be able to show what was repaired, who received the remedy and what remains unpaid.

I've been thinking about something that's probably going to make some people uncomfortable.

The Missing Seat talks about injustice. We talk about what's broken, what was stolen, what was denied, who was excluded and who is still paying for decisions made long before any of us were born. And of course I want to talk about the good too. I want to tell stories about progress. I want to recognize people who break barriers and institutions that finally change.

But I keep running into one problem.

What exactly am I supposed to applaud?

Seriously. Help your boy out, because I'm confused.

If you stop discriminating against me, am I supposed to say thank youuuuuu?

If you stop keeping Black people out of a profession, a university, a neighborhood, a courtroom, a voting booth or an elected office, exactly when does your obligation to stop doing something wrong become an accomplishment deserving applause?

I'm not hatin'. I'm askin'.

And before somebody deliberately misunderstands me, when someone becomes the first Black president, first Black governor, first Black CEO, first Black astronaut, first Black justice or first Black anything, that person's accomplishment is real. They may have worked twice as hard, walked through doors that were never designed to open for them, survived treatment their white counterparts never had to think about and carried expectations that had nothing to do with the job itself.

Give that person every damn flower they earned.

But what exactly are we celebrating about the word first?

Their excellence? Absolutely.

The fact that it took the United States 100, 150 or 200 years to finally let somebody who looked like them into the room? That part isn't an achievement.

That's evidence.

The first Black person to reach a position isn't proof that Black excellence suddenly appeared. Black excellence was standing outside the door long before somebody finally unlocked it.

And being first doesn't automatically mean that person was better than every Black person who came before them. How would we even know? How many equally talented people never got the education, introduction, loan, interview, vote, promotion, protection or simple opportunity necessary to get anywhere near that door?

That's why I struggle with the phrase we hear constantly:

Look how far we've come.

Okay.

Compared with what?

That question matters, because distance means nothing until somebody identifies the starting line.

If the starting point is slavery, then yes, America has come an extraordinary distance. Black people aren't legally property anymore. Congratulations, America.

But wait a minute.

Aren't we also constantly being told that slavery was a long time ago and we need to stop talking about it?

You can't have that both ways.

You don't get to tell me slavery is too old to discuss when I ask about the debt, then drag slavery back out when you need a before picture to prove how wonderful America is today.

If 1860 is irrelevant when we discuss responsibility, it can't suddenly become the benchmark when we discuss progress.

So tell me where the proper starting line is. Is it 1865, when the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery except as punishment for crime? Is it 1870, when the Fifteenth Amendment prohibited race-based denial of the vote? Is it 1954, 1964 or 1965? Is it 2008? Is it 2026?

And who decided?

Depending on where somebody places that line, "how far we've come" can mean almost anything.

That's part of what bothers me about the way America sometimes celebrates racial progress. We're very good at celebrating the removal of a barrier without spending nearly as much time asking who built it, how long it stood, how many people it kept out and what happened to the people whose lives were spent waiting on the other side.

Sometimes the receipt is uglier than the celebration.

Here's one.

When slavery was abolished in Washington, D.C., in 1862, the federal government actually provided compensation. Good. There's a payment. There's a government program. There are records. There's a receipt.

Only one problem.

The money went to former slaveholders.

The law allowed loyal Union slaveholders to claim up to $300 for each enslaved person freed. The people who had been owned, worked, bought, sold and denied control over their own lives weren't the ones Congress chose to compensate for emancipation.

Think about that for a minute.

When the government finally ended the arrangement, one of the immediate financial questions it answered was how to compensate some of the people who had claimed ownership of human beings. The loss it quickly translated into dollars was the enslaver's loss of property, not the enslaved person's loss of wages, liberty, family, safety and years of life.

Now that is what I mean when I say show me the receipts.

America also knows how to make redress concrete when it chooses to.

After the federal government forcibly removed and incarcerated Japanese Americans during World War II, Congress passed the Civil Liberties Act of 1988. The United States acknowledged the injustice, apologized and created a redress program. The Justice Department ultimately paid $20,000 each to 82,219 eligible claimants.

Was $20,000 enough to restore lost homes, businesses, freedom, time, dignity and years of life? Of course not.

And the histories of Japanese American incarceration and Black enslavement aren't interchangeable. They shouldn't be flattened into the same story.

But look at the structure.

The government identified a wrong. It acknowledged responsibility. It apologized. It identified people who had been harmed. It attached an actual remedy. Then money changed hands.

Now we're getting somewhere.

That's a receipt.

It doesn't prove the account was paid in full. It proves the government understood that an apology without a remedy would leave something essential undone.

So when somebody tells me how far Black America has come, I want something more substantial than a photograph of another first. I want to know what happened to the bill.

Not just whether Black people eventually got through the door. What happened to the people who were deliberately kept outside it?

Not just whether discriminatory laws disappeared. What happened to the wealth, land, education, wages, political power, safety and opportunities lost while those laws operated?

Not just whether America stopped doing something. What did America do about the damage already done?

And please don't confuse that question with refusing to acknowledge progress.

There has been progress. Enormous progress.

But progress has a recipe too. Generations of Black Americans fought, organized, sued, marched, voted, wrote, taught, bled and sometimes died to force much of that progress into existence. Allies stood with them. Some public officials acted with courage. Laws changed. Institutions moved.

America often tells the last part of that story as though the institution woke up one morning, discovered its conscience and graciously opened the door.

Maybe that's another part we've got backward.

When a barrier finally falls, the congratulations shouldn't automatically go first to the institution for finally removing it. Maybe they belong to the people who spent generations kicking the damn thing.

That's who I want to celebrate.

I'll celebrate the Black child walking into a school that once would've rejected them. I'll celebrate the Black woman taking an oath for an office generations of women like her were never allowed to hold. I'll celebrate the family buying a home where racial covenants and discriminatory lending once told people with their skin color they weren't welcome. I'll celebrate every person who crosses a line somebody else once drew specifically to keep them out.

But I'm not going to confuse their victory with the system's virtue.

Those are two very different things.

And I'm definitely not ready to declare the bill paid because we finally have Black billionaires, judges, professors, generals, mayors and a Black former president.

Those are achievements. They aren't receipts for the entire account.

The strongest argument against what I'm saying is that progress still matters even when it arrives late. A first can inspire a child, weaken another barrier and prove that a door once declared permanently closed can open. Today's people didn't personally build every old barrier, and no government can calculate every opportunity stolen from every person across generations.

All true.

But recognizing progress and accounting for damage aren't competing obligations. We can celebrate the person who made history without pretending history has been repaired. We can admit that perfect calculation is impossible without treating zero as the only acceptable estimate. We can honor change without giving an institution extra credit for ending conduct it never had a right to impose.

Removing the barrier, repairing the damage and paying the account are three different acts.

America keeps showing us the first and calling it all three.

So maybe before The Missing Seat starts handing out congratulations for how far we've come, we need to establish what that phrase actually means.

What was owed? What was returned? Who received it? Who didn't? What damage was repaired? What damage was merely inherited by the next generation? Who paid the bill? Where? To whom?

And please, show me the receipt.

Because if the argument is simply that things aren't as horrible as they once were, I'm sorry, but you've gotta come with more than that.

Better than oppression is one hell of a low bar for equality.

I'm not looking for a perfect America. I'm looking for an honest accounting of the one we actually have.

And when we finally find something that deserves celebrating, I'll gladly stand up and clap.

But first, I want to know exactly what I'm clapping for.

Ally = Action. 🪑

Pull Up a Chair

The next time a government agency, university, company or public institution announces a historic first, celebrate the person. Then ask the institution for the rest of the record:

  • What barrier delayed this first?
  • Who maintained it, and for how long?
  • How many people were excluded before the door opened?
  • What measurable harm did that exclusion cause?
  • What has been done to repair the harm, not merely stop repeating it?
  • Who received the remedy, and who is still waiting?

A milestone tells us somebody finally crossed the line. A receipt tells us what the line cost.
