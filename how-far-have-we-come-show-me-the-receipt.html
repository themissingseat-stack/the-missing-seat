RACE, HISTORY & ACCOUNTABILITY

How Far Have We Come? Show Me the Receipt.

America loves to celebrate racial progress. Good. We should celebrate progress. But ending a wrong, repairing the damage it caused and settling the bill are three different things.

Seat Summary

Celebrate the person who breaks a barrier. But do not confuse that person's victory with proof that the system has repaired everything the barrier caused. If America wants credit for progress, show what changed, what was repaired and what is still missing.

What exactly are we celebrating?

I keep hearing the same phrase:

Look how far we've come.

Okay. I can celebrate progress.

I can celebrate the first Black president, the first Black governor, the first Black CEO, the first Black justice, the first Black astronaut, the first Black woman to hold an office that was closed to women like her for generations.

Give those people every flower they earned.

But the word first also tells us something else.

It tells us there was a long period when people who looked like them were not in that room.

So when someone says, "Look how far we've come," I have a simple question:

Compared with what starting point?

The starting line matters

If the starting point is slavery, then yes, America has come a very long way.

Black people are no longer legally owned as property.

But that is a very low bar for equality.

And there is another problem. We are often told that slavery was too long ago to matter when people ask about responsibility or repair.

So we cannot use slavery only when it makes today's America look good, then call it ancient history when someone asks what happened to the damage.

If we are going to measure progress, we need to be honest about the starting line and the bill that came with it.

Removing the barrier is not the same as repairing the damage

This is the part I think America skips too quickly.

Imagine a door that was locked for 100 years.

Then one day the lock is removed.

That matters. The door is finally open.

But what happened during those 100 years?

Who lost the job?

Who lost the education?

Who never got the loan?

Who could not buy the house?

Who could not vote?

Who could not build wealth?

Who passed less to their children because the door stayed locked?

Opening the door stops one part of the problem.

It does not automatically return what was lost while the door was closed.

Stopping the wrong, repairing the damage and paying the account are three different things.

Here is a real receipt

When slavery was abolished in Washington, D.C., in 1862, the federal government created a compensation program.

So there was money. There were claims. There were records.

But the money did not go to the people who had been enslaved.

It went to loyal slaveholders.

The law allowed some enslavers to receive up to $300 for each enslaved person who was freed.

Think about what that means.

When the government ended slavery in the District, it quickly figured out how to put a dollar amount on the slaveholder's loss of "property."

It did not create the same kind of payment for the enslaved person's stolen labor, stolen freedom, broken family ties, lost wages or years of life under slavery.

That is why I keep saying: show me the receipt.

America knows what a real remedy looks like

There is another example worth looking at.

During World War II, the federal government forcibly removed and incarcerated Japanese Americans.

Decades later, Congress passed the Civil Liberties Act of 1988.

The government acknowledged the wrong, apologized and paid $20,000 to each eligible person. The Justice Department says 82,219 people received payments.

Was $20,000 enough to fully repair lost homes, businesses, freedom and time? No.

And Japanese American incarceration and Black enslavement are not the same history.

But look at the structure:

  • The government named the wrong.
  • It admitted responsibility.
  • It identified people who were harmed.
  • It created an actual remedy.
  • Money changed hands.

That is more than a speech.

That is an attempt at repair.

So what does "progress" really mean?

Progress matters.

Black Americans fought, organized, voted, sued, marched, wrote, taught and sometimes died to force major changes in this country.

Allies helped. Some public officials acted with courage. Laws changed. Institutions changed.

We should celebrate that.

But we should also be careful about who gets the credit.

When a barrier finally falls, the first congratulations should not automatically go to the institution that kept the barrier standing.

Maybe they belong first to the people who spent generations pushing against it.

I will celebrate the Black child entering a school that once would have rejected them.

I will celebrate the Black family buying a home in a place where people with their skin color were once shut out.

I will celebrate the Black woman taking an oath for an office that women like her were once not allowed to hold.

But I will not confuse their victory with proof that the whole system has been repaired.

Who is missing?

The people who were qualified but never got through the door.

The families who lost land, wages, education, votes, housing, safety or opportunity before the rule changed.

The children and grandchildren who inherited less because someone before them was denied more.

The people whose losses never made it into the celebration.

Receipts & Recipes

Receipt: In 1862, the federal government compensated some slaveholders in Washington, D.C., when slavery there ended. The formerly enslaved people themselves did not receive that compensation. In 1988, Congress created a redress program for Japanese Americans incarcerated during World War II, including a formal apology and $20,000 payments to eligible survivors.

Recipe: The next time an institution announces a "historic first," celebrate the person. Then ask the rest of the questions: What barrier caused the delay? How long did it last? Who was kept out? What did that exclusion cost? What has been repaired? Who is still waiting?

What should we measure?

Not just whether Black people eventually got through the door.

Ask what happened to the people who were deliberately kept outside it.

Not just whether a discriminatory law was finally removed.

Ask what happened to the wealth, land, education, wages, safety, political power and opportunities lost while the law was in place.

Not just whether America stopped doing something wrong.

Ask what America did about the damage already done.

A milestone tells us somebody finally crossed the line. A receipt tells us what the line cost.

The bottom line

I am not asking for a perfect America.

I am asking for an honest accounting of the America we actually have.

Celebrate progress.

Celebrate the people who fought for it.

But do not call the account settled just because the country finally stopped one part of the harm.

Better than oppression is one hell of a low bar for equality.

So yes, tell me how far we have come.

Then show me what was repaired.

Show me who received it.

Show me what is still missing.

Show me the receipt.

Stories are evidence.
Evidence drives change.

Ally = Action. 🪑

Sources and Receipts
