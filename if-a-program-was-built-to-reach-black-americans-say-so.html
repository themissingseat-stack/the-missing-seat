RACE, PUBLIC POLICY & ACCESS

If a Program Was Built to Reach Black Americans, Say So.

If the government creates a program because Black people were left out, denied help, or harmed, then say that clearly. The people the program is meant to reach should not need a law degree to figure it out.

Seat Affected: The Seat of Access
Seat Status: Too often hidden behind government language
Location: United States
Theme: Race • Public Programs • Access • Government Communication

Meet people where they are

Here is the basic problem.

The government may know exactly who was hurt by a problem. It may even create a program because of that harm. But when it is time to tell the public about the help, the language suddenly becomes hard to understand.

Instead of saying, “Black families were hit especially hard by this problem,” a website may say things like “historically underserved communities” or “priority populations.”

Sometimes those words are needed for legal reasons. Sometimes a program is truly meant for many different groups. But if the wording gets so vague that the people the program was created to help cannot recognize themselves, something has gone wrong.

If Black Americans are part of the reason a program exists, Black Americans should not have to guess whether it was built with them in mind.

Picture a family at the kitchen table

A family hears there may be help with housing, a small business, health care, school, farming, or money.

They open the government website.

They find long paragraphs, agency terms, and instructions that sound like they were written for another government office instead of an ordinary person.

What they do not find is a simple answer to the questions they actually have:

  • Why does this program exist?
  • Was this problem especially bad for people like me?
  • Can I apply?
  • What do I need?
  • Where do I start?

If people cannot understand the help, then the help may never reach them.

Being honest about the problem is not the same as making a program race-only

This is where people often get confused.

Saying, “Black people were hit especially hard by this problem,” is not the same as saying, “Only Black people can use this program.”

A program can have rules that apply to people of different races and still be honest about why the program was created.

If Black farmers faced proven discrimination, say that.

If Black neighborhoods were shut out of fair home loans, say that.

If Black business owners had a harder time getting money to start or grow a business, say that.

If Black women are dying or getting sick at higher rates in pregnancy and childbirth, say that.

Then say, just as clearly, who can apply today.

You can tell the truth about who was hurt without lying about who qualifies now.

Sometimes “neutral” language becomes another wall

Government language is often written to sound careful and professional.

But careful language can become useless language if normal people cannot understand it.

Imagine building a fire station because one neighborhood has been left without enough emergency help. Then imagine never clearly telling that neighborhood the new station was built to serve them too.

The building exists. The money was spent. The service is real.

But if the people who need it do not know about it, the job is not finished.

The receipt: five things every program should say plainly

Every public program should answer these five questions near the top of the page:

  • Why was this program created?
  • Who was most hurt or left out?
  • Who is the program trying to reach now?
  • Who can actually apply?
  • What should the person do next?

That is not political spin. That is basic communication.

Why this matters

When a program is hard to understand, the work gets pushed back onto the person asking for help.

Now that person has to figure out the history, learn government terms, find the right office, understand the rules, collect the documents, and hope they are in the right place.

People with more time, money, education, lawyers, or experience dealing with government usually have an easier time getting through that maze.

Then we wonder why some programs do not reach the people they were supposed to help.

If you have to already understand the system before the system can help you, the system is not meeting people where they are.

What should happen now?

This is fixable.

Government websites could put a simple box at the top of every program page:

Why this program exists: This program was created because this problem has hit certain communities especially hard, including Black families.

Who can apply: Here are the rules.

What to do next: Check these requirements and apply here.

That would be clearer than hiding the history and making people hunt for the answer.

Who is missing?

The person who does not speak government.

The family that never heard the program existed.

The Black business owner who assumes the program must be for somebody else.

The parent who sees a phrase like “underserved communities” and has no reason to know it includes people with a history like theirs.

The person the government meant to help but never actually reached.

Receipts & Recipes

Receipt: Check the program’s own rules, the law or policy that created it, and the evidence the government used to explain the problem.

Recipe: When you see a public program, ask five simple questions: Why was it created? Who was most affected? Who can apply? Is that explained in normal language? Can a regular person get from the first page to the application without becoming an expert first?

What needs to change

Government agencies should test their websites and forms with the people they actually want to reach.

Plain language should come before legal language.

The history behind the program should not be hidden.

The rules about who can apply should be easy to find.

And if a program is supposed to close a gap, government should measure whether the people it was meant to help actually received the help.

If government is willing to point to Black suffering when it explains why a program is needed, it should also be willing to tell Black people clearly that the program was created with them in mind.

Otherwise we get an absurd result: the government knows who was hurt, researchers know who was hurt, the program exists because of that harm, and the family at the kitchen table still has no idea the help was meant to reach them.

Meet people where they are.
Name the problem.
Say who was affected.
Explain who can apply.
Make the next step obvious.

Stories are evidence.
Evidence drives change.

Verification Notice

This editorial is about clear communication. It does not claim that every program dealing with a racial problem can legally limit help by race. Different programs have different rules. The point is simple: government should tell the truth about the problem, tell people clearly who can apply, and make the process understandable.
