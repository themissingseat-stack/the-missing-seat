WAR, POWER & CIVILIAN CONSEQUENCES • JULY 30, 2026

Iran: The War Behind the War

The public is told this is a war about nuclear danger. It is also about regional power, regime survival, shipping lanes, oil prices, deterrence—and who gets to define self-defense after the missiles have already begun to fly.

Seat Affected: The civilian right to life, truth and meaningful political voice before leaders choose war
Seat Status: Under direct threat
Location: Iran, Israel, the Persian Gulf and the wider Middle East
Theme: War narratives, nuclear risk, regional power, energy and accountability

There is the war we can see: aircraft, missiles, drones, funerals, damaged infrastructure and ships waiting outside dangerous waters.

Then there is the war behind it: the struggle to decide what the conflict means.

Washington and Israel describe military action as necessary to contain Iranian nuclear and missile threats and protect regional security. Tehran describes itself as resisting aggression, coercion and foreign domination. Each account contains facts. Neither account is the whole story.

The war did not begin with one grievance. It emerged from decades of confrontation: Iran’s nuclear program; Israeli fears of an existential threat; Iranian support for armed groups across the region; U.S. forces and alliances surrounding Iran; sanctions that weakened Iran’s economy; failed or abandoned agreements; attacks, assassinations and retaliation.

By 2026, those pressures had become open U.S.-Israeli war with Iran. Strikes and counterstrikes killed people, displaced families, damaged infrastructure and disrupted two of the world’s most important shipping corridors. A pause in attacks did not settle the underlying disputes. On July 28, the U.S. military said it intercepted Iranian ballistic missiles aimed at American forces, ending another brief lull while negotiators still searched for terms.

The Nuclear Question Is Real

Iran insists its nuclear program is peaceful. It is a party to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, which permits peaceful nuclear activity under safeguards. But the International Atomic Energy Agency has documented unresolved safeguards questions and serious losses in its ability to verify Iran’s nuclear material and activities.

That uncertainty matters. Israel sees a nuclear-capable Iran as an intolerable threat. Iran points to Israel’s widely understood but officially unacknowledged nuclear arsenal and asks why one regional state may retain nuclear ambiguity while another is threatened over enrichment.

These positions are not morally or legally identical. They do reveal a legitimacy problem: nonproliferation rules are harder to defend when they appear to be enforced selectively.

Military strikes can destroy facilities, equipment and personnel. They cannot manufacture trust. Bombing may delay technical capacity while increasing Iran’s incentive to conceal, disperse or accelerate it. A durable answer still requires verification, access and enforceable commitments.

The Security Question Is Also Real

Iran has armed and supported groups that have attacked Israel, U.S. forces and commercial shipping. Its missile and drone programs give it the ability to impose costs far beyond its borders. Gulf states have legitimate reasons to fear escalation.

Iran also sees U.S. bases, sanctions, Israeli operations and repeated threats of regime change as a security system directed against it. That perception does not excuse attacks on civilians or neutral shipping. It helps explain why Tehran treats regional armed networks and control of strategic waterways as leverage rather than merely ideology.

“Self-defense” therefore appears in every government’s vocabulary. But self-defense cannot become a blank check. The necessary questions remain: What threat was imminent? What target was chosen? Was the force necessary and proportionate? What precautions protected civilians? And what lawful authority governed the decision?

The Strait Is Power

The Strait of Hormuz is not a side issue. Roughly one-fifth of global oil and liquefied-natural-gas trade normally passes through this narrow route. Whoever can disrupt it can affect fuel prices, inflation, shipping insurance and household budgets thousands of miles away.

Iran’s attempts to control or charge vessels transform geography into bargaining power. U.S. efforts to keep the route open protect international commerce, but also preserve an economic order backed by American military power. Gulf states want neither Iranian domination nor an endless regional war that destroys their infrastructure.

Oman’s current Gulf-backed proposal for voluntary navigation and safety fees is an attempt to turn coercive leverage into a negotiated arrangement. It may fail. Its significance is that regional states are searching for a formula in which Iran gains recognition without receiving unilateral control of an international passage.

Regime Survival and Political Survival

Wars are fought by states, but leaders also operate inside political systems.

Iran’s rulers can use foreign attack to demand unity, suppress dissent and describe domestic opposition as collaboration. U.S. and Israeli leaders can use Iranian threats to justify military action, reinforce alliances and redirect public attention. None of that proves the security threats are invented. It means genuine threats can also be politically useful.

Calls for regime change require special scrutiny. Many Iranians oppose repression by their government. That does not mean they consent to foreign bombardment or that outside powers can reliably create a freer successor. Iraq, Afghanistan and Libya demonstrate how quickly removing a government can become a much longer struggle over what replaces it.

Who Pays?

The people with the least control pay first.

Iranian civilians live under authoritarian restrictions, economic sanctions and military attack. Israeli civilians face missiles and drones. U.S. service members and communities across the Gulf become targets. Sailors and migrant workers cross dangerous routes. Families everywhere absorb higher energy and food costs.

Women, dissidents, ethnic and religious minorities, political prisoners and ordinary families inside Iran must not be reduced to symbols useful to either side. Opposing Iran’s government is not the same as endorsing war against Iranian people. Opposing U.S. or Israeli military action is not the same as excusing Tehran’s repression or attacks.

The Missing Questions

What specific political result can military force realistically achieve—and what happens if bombing destroys targets without resolving the conflict?

Who has authority to widen the war? What evidence is available to the public and legislatures? What would Iran have to do for attacks and sanctions to end? What must the United States and Israel guarantee in return? Who verifies compliance? Who investigates civilian harm? And who benefits if negotiations remain impossible?

A strategy without a reachable end state is not accountability. It is escalation with better language.

Who Is Missing?

Iranian civilians who are neither their government nor its military.

People across Israel and the Gulf whose security is invoked but whose consent to prolonged war is rarely measured.

Legislatures expected to scrutinize decisions before executive power makes them irreversible.

Independent inspectors, journalists and humanitarian organizations needed to test every side’s claims.

One Action

When reading any claim about Iran, ask four questions: Who made it? What independently verifiable evidence supports it? What legal authority is being invoked? What outcome—not merely what target—is the proposed action expected to achieve?

Reject the demand to choose between pretending Iran poses no danger and granting its enemies unlimited power to wage war.

Return to the Seat

We will track IAEA access, the status of the Strait of Hormuz, civilian harm, legislative authorization, sanctions, stated war aims and whether any agreement addresses the security concerns of all affected populations.

The war behind the war is a contest over who controls the story.

The people living beneath it deserve control over their future.
