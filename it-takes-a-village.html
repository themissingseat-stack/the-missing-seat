The Missing Seat Editorial

It Takes a Village. Where Did It Go?

People love saying, “It takes a village to raise a child.”

But millions of parents are raising children without anything that feels like a village.

Single mothers lead most single-parent households, but they are not alone. Single fathers, grandparents, foster parents, older siblings and guardians are also trying to do the work that two adults, an extended family or an entire community might once have shared.

The missing seat is not love. Most of these caregivers have plenty of love.

What is missing is support.

One person cannot be everywhere

A parent may need to work while a child needs care. A school may call during a shift. Rent, groceries, transportation and medical appointments do not wait for the parent to have more time or money.

Government programs often make struggling families prove their hardship through forms, appointments and repeated checks. That process takes time from people who already have the least time available.

A program in Jackson, Mississippi, tried a different approach. Eligible low-income mothers received direct monthly payments with fewer conditions. Participants reported less stress, greater financial stability, better housing security and more time to focus on their children.

Direct support does not solve every problem. But it recognizes something obvious: Parents usually understand what their families need.

Why should an ordinary person care?

When families lack support, the consequences reach schools, workplaces, hospitals and entire neighborhoods.

A parent who loses childcare may lose a job. A child whose family is forced to move repeatedly may struggle in school. A caregiver who never gets rest can experience serious physical and mental strain.

Family support is not a private luxury. It affects how well the whole community functions.

Who has power?

Government controls benefit rules, childcare funding, paid-leave laws and housing assistance. Employers control schedules and flexibility. Schools and community organizations control many of the systems parents must navigate.

But ordinary people also have useful power. A ride, an hour of childcare, a meal or help completing a form can change someone’s week.

Pull Up a Chair

Do not stop at telling a struggling parent, “I’m sorry.” Ask what would actually help.

Offer something specific instead of saying, “Let me know if you need anything.” Bring a meal. Give a ride. Watch the child for an hour. Share verified information about childcare, housing or family benefits.

And when politicians talk about “family values,” ask what their policies provide to the people doing the daily work of raising families.

A village is not a slogan. It is people, time, money and public systems showing up when a family needs them.

Who is missing?

Ally = Action. 🪑
