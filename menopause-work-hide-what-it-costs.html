She's Still Doing Her Job. Why Must She Hide What It Costs?

By Jansen Bowden / The Missing Seat · November 27, 2026

The Short Version

What happened: On January 1, Illinois becomes one of the first places in the U.S. to require workplace accommodations for menopause. No federal law specifically requires workplace accommodations for menopause. In a major U.S. study, frequent hot flashes and night sweats lasted longest for Black women, about 10 years. In one company study, only 9 percent of workers with symptoms said they got support at work.

Who it affects: Women in midlife, especially those in jobs where they cannot change their hours, their breaks or the temperature, and anyone whose career depends on not looking "past it."

What you can do: Ask your employer who handles health-related adjustment requests and how the information is kept private. Ask your state lawmakers whether your state protects menopause at work.

Website editorial

Picture a woman who has done the same job for twenty years. Now she is sleeping three hours a night, soaking through her shirt in meetings, and losing words she has used her whole career. She knows what it is. The question is whether saying so at work will get her help, or get her quietly written off.

For millions of women, that is the real cost of menopause. The symptoms are ordinary. How a workplace responds to them is a choice.

What the research shows

Menopause is a normal stage of life. Symptoms range from mild to severe. They include hot flashes, night sweats, broken sleep, mood changes and trouble with memory and concentration. [1]

Frequent hot flashes and night sweats can last for years. The Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN) followed more than 3,000 women at seven U.S. sites for up to 17 years. For more than half of the women with frequent hot flashes and night sweats, the symptoms lasted more than seven years. [2] Among the women in the study who had frequent hot flashes and night sweats, Black women had the longest median duration, about 10 years. Japanese and Chinese American women had the shortest, around five. [3] The researchers told doctors to warn women that symptoms may last longer for African American women. [2]

For many of the Black women in that study, it lasted ten years. That is not a phase. It is a large share of a working life, arriving at the age when many women are in line for their most senior jobs.

What it costs at work

A 2023 Mayo Clinic study surveyed women aged 45 to 60 who were in primary care. Among the 4,440 who were working, 13.4 percent reported at least one adverse work outcome tied to menopause symptoms, and 10.8 percent had missed work because of them in the past year. Some cut back hours. Some were laid off, quit or retired. The authors extrapolated the missed-work results to estimate about $1.8 billion a year in lost productivity nationally. [4] The study has limits: it drew on four Mayo Clinic primary-care sites, only 16 percent of those invited responded, and 93 percent of respondents were white. A sample that white cannot tell us much about Black women's experience at work, which is one of the questions this research most needs to answer.

A 2025 study at one U.S. drug company found that only 9 percent of employees with current symptoms said they received any support at work. Some said they no longer recognized themselves on the job. The study was funded by Pfizer, and it covers one employer. [5]

Why women hide it

A woman may be managing symptoms while also worrying about whether her employer still sees a future for her.

Workplaces have long been designed around a worker who does not menstruate, get pregnant or go through menopause. When a woman's health needs show up, they are often treated as a private inconvenience, and she is expected to absorb the cost. In TMS's reading, that is how patriarchy works at the office. It shows up in rules about who gets a break, who controls the thermostat, and whose sick days count against them, not in anyone's stated intentions. We are describing how these rules work, not reporting a measured national pattern.

Ageism adds a second pressure. A manager who assumes menopause makes a woman unreliable may stop sending her to training or passing her name up for promotion. She may never know why. So some women say nothing, and the problem stays invisible to the people who could fix it.

The answer is not to make women announce their symptoms. A good workplace offers help without requiring anyone to explain intimate details. Someone can ask for a fan, a cooler desk, a flexible start time or a break to change clothes, and the request goes to one named person, stays confidential and gets an answer in writing. The 2025 study's authors made the same point: education and support should be available without requiring disclosure of symptoms. [5]

That matters most where workers do not control their own day. In a kitchen, a warehouse, a store or a hospital ward, a fan on the desk is not an option. A workable route could mean a relief worker who covers a short break, a spare uniform, cold water at the station, and shift swaps that do not count against attendance. These are proposals for employers to test, not proven programs.

What the law says

No federal law specifically requires workplace accommodations for menopause. Workers rely on overlapping protections. Title VII bans sex discrimination, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act bans age discrimination, and the Americans with Disabilities Act can cover severe symptoms. Courts have been mixed on whether menopause itself is covered. [6]

States are moving. Rhode Island became the first state to require workplace accommodations for menopause in 2025. Illinois enacted the Menopause Equity and Care Act in August 2026. Its workplace provisions take effect January 1, 2027, along with a Philadelphia ordinance. [7] At least 16 menopause bills were introduced in state legislatures in the first months of 2026. [8] Not every one survives. California Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed one bill in September, and Virginia Governor Abigail Spanberger vetoed another in May. Both said existing state civil rights law already covers it. [9] In Congress, Representatives Debbie Dingell and Yvette Clarke introduced the Menopausal Workers' Fairness Act, H.R. 9671, on July 14. It would create a federal right to reasonable accommodation. As of November 25, TMS found no record that it has advanced. [10]

The warning label changed. The conversation still matters.

For two decades, hormone therapy carried the FDA's strongest "boxed" warning, based on a 2002 study. After it was published, prescriptions dropped among women of all ages. [12] In November 2025, the FDA asked manufacturers to revise those warnings. On February 12, 2026, it approved the first six updated labels, removing cardiovascular-disease, breast-cancer and probable-dementia statements from their boxed warnings. Other risk information remains in the labeling, and systemic estrogen-only products keep the boxed warning about uterine cancer. [11] Some doctors welcomed the change. Others criticized the FDA for skipping its usual public advisory process. [12]

The label change does not make hormone therapy right for everyone, and it does not by itself make care cheaper or appointments easier to get. It means a woman can expect a real conversation with her doctor about her symptoms, her health history and her options, including non-hormonal treatments. "It's natural" is not an answer when she cannot sleep or do her job.

The test

A menopause policy is only as good as what happens when someone uses it. Did she get the adjustment? Did she keep her shot at the next promotion? Did anyone count how many women left before they had to?

She is still doing her job. She should not have to hide what it costs.

Pull Up A Chair

  • At work: You can begin by explaining the adjustment you need, without discussing intimate details with colleagues. Ask the designated contact what information is necessary and how it will be protected. A formal accommodation process may require limited medical documentation. Putting the request in writing can help create a record.
  • Keep a record: Write down how symptoms affect your sleep and work, and bring it to your doctor. Ask about all of your options, including non-hormonal ones.
  • If you manage people: Name one confidential contact and track whether requests get answered.
  • Check your state: Ask your state legislators whether your state protects menopause at work. Illinois rules take effect January 1, 2027.

Seat Summary

In a major U.S. study, frequent hot flashes and night sweats lasted about a decade for Black women who had them, often just as careers peak. In one company study, only 9 percent of workers with symptoms reported support, and no federal law specifically requires menopause accommodations. A few states are starting to fill that gap. The missing seat is a woman's right to keep her career and her privacy at the same time.
