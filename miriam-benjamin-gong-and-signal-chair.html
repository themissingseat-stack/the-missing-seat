Miriam Benjamin Built a Chair That Could Call for Help.

By Jansen · November 14, 2026

The Short Version · 20-second read

What happened: On July 17, 1888, Miriam E. Benjamin received U.S. patent 386,289 for a chair with a gong and a visible signal to summon an attendant.

Who it affects: People learning Black technical history and anyone who wants to give an inventor credit without making claims the records do not support.

What you can do: Open the patent drawing and trace the movement from button to signal. Compare it with the contemporary Congressional Record listing and the Patent Office's account of her effort to sell it.

Based on: Benjamin's patent, an 1894 Congressional Record entry and a historical account from the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office.

Seat Affected: Black women inventors whose technical work and business efforts deserve accurate credit · Seat Status: Achievement documented and formally honored; the practical educational use of that recognition remains an opportunity.

The button on Miriam E. Benjamin's chair was within reach of the person sitting down. Press it and a gong sounded. The same movement turned a signal on the back of the chair so an attendant could tell which seat needed help. Once the person had been served, the attendant could reset it. The drawing in Benjamin's 1888 patent shows the rods and levers that joined those steps. [1]

A gong by itself could tell an attendant that someone wanted help, but the sound would not identify a seat in a crowded room. The signal answered that second question. The person asking could stay seated instead of calling across a room. Benjamin listed hotels and dining rooms as possible uses. She also imagined use in legislatures, trains and hospitals. The patent records those intentions. It does not show how many places tried the design. [1]

The work after the patent

Benjamin had worked as a teacher before joining Washington's community of Black professionals, according to the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office. She tried to persuade the U.S. House of Representatives to use her chair. The House had the power to decide what system to install. The Patent Office historian reports that it did not choose Benjamin's design and later installed a more complicated system. A resemblance does not tell us who designed the later equipment, whether it borrowed from her, or whether she received money for her own invention. [2]

Benjamin pitched an economic benefit, too. Her patent argued that attendants could cover more guests because they would no longer have to keep checking each table. That was a selling argument, not a measurement of what happened in a hotel. A business owner might see a way to staff a room differently; a guest might see a quieter way to ask for help. We have no staffing or sales records that show what those possibilities meant for workers or for Benjamin's income. [1]

The effort did leave a trace. A list printed in the 1894 Congressional Record identifies Benjamin by name, gives the patent number and dates the grant. The Patent Office history records that Representative George Washington Murray brought her name before Congress while seeking support for the Cotton States Exposition, where her patent model was later displayed. The congressional mention and the exhibition put her design in front of people. Neither tells us whether anyone bought one. [2, 3]

Pull Up A Chair

Try reading the drawing with someone who has never opened a patent. Start at the seat, where the person presses the button. Follow the linkage up the back until the visible signal turns. Then find the step the attendant performs to reset it. You can explain the whole design without a story about who eventually bought it.

Teachers and librarians can put the drawing beside the Congressional Record entry and ask students what each source establishes. The drawing explains the work. The list shows that Benjamin's name and patent number made it into the public record. Keep both with her name when you pass the story on.

Seat Summary

Benjamin designed the signaling device and made the case for using it. Seated guests could have summoned help without calling out; attendants could have found the right chair. Buyers controlled adoption. The patent and public record give Benjamin her technical credit without treating a proposed saving, exhibition or similar device as proof of a sale.
