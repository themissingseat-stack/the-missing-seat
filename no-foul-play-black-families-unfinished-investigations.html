JUSTICE, INVESTIGATIONS & PUBLIC TRUST • AUGUST 2, 2026

“No Foul Play”: What Black Families Are Asked to Trust

Nolan Wells was not found hanged. Kyle Bassinga and Jerard Jackson were. These deaths are not evidence of a connected plot—but together they expose why official conclusions must be earned through evidence, transparency and respect.

Seat Affected: The right to an independent, transparent and credible investigation of every unexplained death
Seat Status: Uneven and still under examination
Location: Mississippi, Georgia and Michigan
Theme: Unexplained deaths, suicide, racial memory, evidence and institutional trust

Three young Black men. Three different places. Three deaths that have generated grief, questions and online claims.

Their cases must not be collapsed into one story.

Nolan Xavier Wells, 18, was found dead on Horn Island, Mississippi, on July 6 after a July Fourth boat trip. He was not found hanged. His official cause and manner of death remained pending in the latest reliable reporting; a second autopsy commissioned by his family could not determine either.

Kyle Bassinga, 21, was found hanging in a wooded section of Fair Oaks Park in Cobb County, Georgia, on February 18. Police concluded that he was alone and found no indication of foul play. His family publicly said investigators had been professional, transparent and diligent, asked for privacy and urged people to avoid speculation.

Jerard “Jay” Jackson, 28, was found dead in woods near Michigan’s Electric Forest festival after he was reported missing in late June. Michigan State Police said preliminary evidence indicated suicide and no foul play, while reporting said the investigation awaited the medical examiner’s final report. Members of Jackson’s family have publicly demanded further answers.

Those facts do not establish that any of these men was murdered. They do not prove a coordinated pattern. And “found hanged” is not synonymous with “lynched.”

But refusing unsupported conclusions does not require refusing the harder question: what must an investigation show before a community with good historical reasons for distrust is asked to accept “no foul play”?

Nolan Wells: The Cause Is Still Undetermined

Wells traveled to Horn Island with friends. He did not return with them. His body was found two days later.

Public reporting has described disputed accounts of his final hours, questions about why his phone returned without him and family concerns about missing digital content. Those concerns are being investigated; they are not proof that anyone deleted evidence or caused his death.

The family’s independent autopsy reported decomposition and the earlier autopsy as barriers to a definitive finding. It found no fracture or deep tissue injury associated with a red discoloration on the back of his skull, but said the discoloration warranted further investigation. Cause and manner remained undetermined.

That means neither “murder” nor “accidental drowning” should be presented as established fact. The honest answer is that the public record remains incomplete.

Kyle Bassinga: Respecting What His Family Said

Bassinga’s death immediately evoked America’s history of racial terror. Cobb County officials acknowledged that reaction publicly.

Police said witness information and surveillance showed Bassinga walking into the wooded area alone. In March, the department said its investigation was nearing completion, that the evidence showed he was alone at the time of death and that there was no indication of foul play.

Most importantly, Bassinga’s family did not publicly reject that process. In a statement released by police, they praised investigators, asked for privacy and urged the public to rely on official updates rather than speculation.

TMS will not turn a family’s loss into a conspiracy claim the family itself has asked the public not to spread. Accountability includes holding institutions to evidence. It also includes listening when a grieving family asks not to have its loved one used as proof of an unproven theory.

Jerard Jackson: A Preliminary Conclusion, Not the Last Word

Jackson attended the Electric Forest festival in Michigan. Police said he contacted officers about what he believed was a crime involving a friend; investigators concluded no crime had occurred. His rental vehicle was later found unoccupied, with his wallet and keys inside. His body was found in nearby woods.

Michigan State Police said the investigation indicated suicide and no foul play, but also said the matter remained open pending the medical examiner’s final report. Jackson’s family has publicly questioned the conclusion and called for a fuller investigation.

Those positions are not equal forms of evidence. Police possess investigative material the public may not have. A family’s disbelief does not prove homicide. But a preliminary assessment is also not a final medical finding. Transparency requires authorities to identify what can be released, what remains pending and what evidence supports the final determination.

Why Hanging Cannot Be Treated as an Ordinary Image

The Equal Justice Initiative has documented more than 4,400 racial-terror lynchings in the United States between 1877 and 1950, including more than 4,000 Black victims in twelve Southern states. These were not merely individual killings. They were public acts of terrorism used to enforce racial subordination, frequently tolerated or enabled by government officials.

That history does not determine the cause of a modern death.

It does determine why the sight or report of a Black person hanging from a tree produces collective alarm. Calling that response irrational asks Black communities to forget what the country is still struggling to acknowledge.

At the same time, suicide is real. CDC data show that hanging, strangulation or suffocation accounts for a substantial share of suicides. Treating every such death as homicide can erase people who died by suicide, injure families and spread accusations unsupported by evidence.

Historical awareness and evidentiary discipline must coexist.

What a Credible Investigation Owes

A trustworthy investigation should not begin with the assumption that a Black death is homicide. It should not begin with the assumption that it is suicide either.

It should preserve the scene, document the position and condition of the body, obtain witness and digital evidence, complete autopsy and toxicology work, examine possible third-party involvement, reconcile conflicting timelines and explain which findings support the final cause and manner of death.

Authorities may need to withhold details while an investigation is active. But “we cannot release everything yet” is different from offering a confident public conclusion before the underlying process is complete.

Families also deserve timely communication. Silence creates an information vacuum. Social media fills it—often with rumor, invented details and racial fear.

The Question Is Not “Trust Us”

Public trust is not produced by repeating “no foul play.” It is produced by demonstrating how investigators reached that conclusion and by correcting early statements when later evidence changes the picture.

When American history includes Black deaths ignored, misclassified or never prosecuted, what process must government provide before asking a family and community to accept that no one else was involved?

The answer cannot be automatic suspicion of every official conclusion.

It also cannot be automatic deference to authority.

Evidence must decide. Transparency must show the work. And when the evidence is not finished, institutions should say so plainly.

Who Is Missing?

Families waiting for definitive answers rather than premature certainty.

Investigators with the time, resources and independence to pursue every credible lead.

Black communities whose historical memory is often treated as paranoia instead of relevant context.

And people lost to suicide, whose deaths deserve truth and dignity rather than stigma or political appropriation.

One Action

Before sharing a claim about any unexplained death, identify its source and label it correctly: confirmed fact, official preliminary assessment, medical finding, family concern or unsupported allegation.

Do not call a death a lynching without evidence of an extrajudicial killing. Do not call an undetermined death a suicide or accident before the responsible medical authority does.

Verification Notice

This editorial does not claim that Wells, Bassinga or Jackson was lynched, murdered or connected to the others. Wells was not found hanged. The official investigation into Wells’s death remained incomplete in the latest reliable reporting, and Jackson’s publicly reported police conclusion was preliminary pending the medical examiner’s final report. “No foul play” is an investigative assessment; it is not itself a medical cause of death.
