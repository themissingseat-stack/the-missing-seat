MISSING HISTORY • GERMANY

The Berlin Wall Opened When People Went to the Border.

By Jansen · November 12, 2026

The Short Version · 20-second read

What happened: On November 9, 1989, East German official Günter Schabowski said new travel rules took effect immediately. At Bornholmer Straße, guards initially let selected people through, then stopped checking people that night.

Who it affects: East Berliners who lived under restrictions on travel and readers learning how the border actually opened.

What you can do: Put the press conference transcript, a witness interview and the crossing timeline side by side. If you are in Berlin, visit the exhibition at the former crossing.

Based on: The press conference transcript, Rainer Eppelmann’s recollections, the German federal chronology and Chronik der Mauer.

Seat Affected: East Berlin residents who faced restrictions on leaving the German Democratic Republic, and the people whose role in opening the border is easy to miss · Seat Status: A documented opportunity opened; the wider lesson needs careful teaching

Rainer Eppelmann remembers getting close enough to the barrier at Bornholmer Straße to hear a man ask the guards to open it. The man had a reason to expect an answer: an East German official had just told a room full of reporters that new travel rules were taking effect immediately. Yet the guards were still there and the barrier was still down. In Eppelmann’s account, people kept asking to cross. Once the barrier opened, he remembers strangers embracing. He and a companion walked a short way into the crossing area and turned around to watch. They did not go all the way into West Berlin that night. [1, 2]

The video of Günter Schabowski at his press conference is famous for good reason. Asked when the new rules took effect, he looked through his papers and answered that, as far as he knew, they applied immediately. Read the rest of the transcript, though, and you find a policy about applying for trips and issuing permissions. You do not find an instruction to stop checking everyone at Bornholmer Straße. Residents who heard the news went to see what it meant. At the crossing, staff now faced a crowd expecting to be let through. [1, 3, 4]

What changed at the crossing

Five days earlier, East Berlin’s Alexanderplatz had filled with demonstrators calling for change. The pressure on East Germany’s leadership was already visible before the press conference. The government still had the ability to decide who could cross; for people facing those decisions, the burden was years of having to ask permission or weigh the costs of leaving. The evening of November 9 shows that authority weakening in stages. [2, 3, 5]

The federal chronology records an initial opening at about 9:20 p.m. Guards let some people through while still checking them and invalidating their identity documents. Chronik der Mauer records the end of controls around 11:30 p.m. at that crossing, after the crowd had grown. Those accounts describe different points in the evening. The early step gave some people a way out on terms the authorities still imposed. Later, the staff stopped checking the people who crossed. Harald Jäger, a senior passport-control officer at the crossing that evening, later recalled the decision to end the checks. [3, 4]

Eppelmann also remembered people at the front helping open a barrier. That is his account of what he and others did and saw from the crowd. Jäger’s account helps explain what the people responsible for checks decided. Neither memory establishes what every guard did or who moved every gate. There is room in the record for both the person insisting on passage and the person who could stop checking papers. [2, 3]

The immediate benefit went to people who could cross that night, including people who could see family and friends on the other side. The checkpoints did not all open at once, and East and West Germany did not become one country that evening. Reunification followed on October 3, 1990. November 9 also marks the Nazi pogrom of 1938, a separate history that deserves to be taught on its own terms. Remembering the joy at the crossing does not require asking that date to carry only one memory. [4, 8, 9]

Pull Up A Chair

Read the press conference answer and the timeline from the same evening. Then read Eppelmann’s account and compare what he could see with what the passport-control recollections describe. Ahead of the 37th anniversary, Berlin’s Mitte district announced a November 9 commemoration with Pankow at the former crossing. The Berlin Wall Foundation’s permanent outdoor exhibition gives visitors a way to study the site beyond the anniversary. If you are teaching the story, ask students to mark the announcement, the selective crossing and the end of checks on three separate lines. The time between those lines is the part the television clip leaves out. [1, 2, 3, 6, 7]

Seat Summary

East Berliners faced the restrictions and bore the costs of permission-based travel. State authorities held the power to set those restrictions and local staff controlled passage. People who went to the crossing made their demand visible; the end of local checks gave them passage that night. The case shows what changed at one crossing and who helped make it happen. It cannot measure each actor’s contribution or explain the fall of East Germany on its own.

Verification notice

Checked November 9, 2026. The press conference is a contemporary record; the Eppelmann and Jäger accounts are retrospective. The federal and archival timelines describe an initial controlled release and a later end to checks. They are not treated as contradictory or as independent eyewitnesses to every barrier movement. No invented dialogue, interview or claim that the 2026 commemoration already occurred. No incident counts or claim that this opening alone produced reunification.
