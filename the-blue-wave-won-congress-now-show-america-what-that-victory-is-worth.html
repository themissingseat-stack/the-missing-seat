DEMOCRACY & GOVERNMENT • SPECIAL ELECTION EDITORIAL

Voters Changed Congress. The Old Majority Still Has Eight Weeks.

By Jansen · November 4, 2026

The Short Version

What happened: Voters handed Democrats the House and the Senate on November 3. The new Congress is not sworn in until January 3, 2027. Until then, the outgoing Republican majority still holds the gavel, and President Trump still holds the pen for two more years.

Who it affects: Anyone who depends on health coverage, federal funding, fair courts, or the right to vote without new hurdles. Decisions made in the next eight weeks can last for decades.

What you can do: Call your current senators and representative before December 11, when government funding runs out. Ask them where they stand on war money, voting rules, and lifetime judges. Then hold the new majority to its promises from day one.

Seat Affected: The voter's seat in Congress, during the eight weeks between the vote and the swearing-in · Seat Status: Seat At Risk

Everywhere people gather this week, at the kitchen table, at the barbershop, in the group chat, the same word keeps coming up: finally. That feeling is earned. It is also the most dangerous feeling a voter can have right now, because the people who lost on Tuesday still have eight weeks of power, and some of them have said out loud how they plan to use it.

What voters did

The result was not close. Democrats won both chambers of Congress, with networks calling the Senate late Tuesday night and the House shortly before. In the Senate, Democrats flipped at least seven Republican seats, including Kansas, Iowa, Texas, Ohio, Maine, North Carolina and South Carolina. By Wednesday they held 54 Senate seats, with races in Alaska and Mississippi still being counted.

In the House, NBC News projects about 258 Democratic seats, the most since 1993, with dozens of races still uncalled. That happened even after Republicans redrew maps in several states to help themselves. Hakeem Jeffries, who won reelection in New York, is in line to become the first Black Speaker of the House.

Heavy spending did not secure Republican control. Elon Musk alone had spent about $366 million this election cycle by mid-October, most of it to help Republicans keep Congress, Reuters reported. Voters told pollsters the economy and health care mattered most.

It also matters what voters were saying. They did not all vote for the same reasons. Some wanted relief from prices, some wanted an end to the war, some wanted stronger checks on the President. Many were independents or former Republican voters who decided the administration had failed them. Their votes came with expectations, not lifelong membership cards.

Here is the part that matters now. The new Congress takes office on January 3, 2027. The President stays until January 2029. Every promise made on Tuesday runs through those two facts.

The next eight weeks: what is happening right now

Congress comes back on November 9 for what Washington calls the lame duck. Members who just lost, or are retiring, still vote. The House is scheduled for about 20 working days before the year ends, the Senate about 22. One Republican senator, Bernie Moreno of Ohio, described the plan as packing "everything that we could possibly get" into one last party-line bill.

None of this is a loophole. Lawmakers keep their authority until their terms end, including those who lost. That is how the constitutional calendar works. But legal authority is not the same as a mandate, and the public is entitled to watch how it is used.

Two more things deserve a plain look.

The pardons. Two days before the election, President Trump said he would be willing to preemptively pardon Elon Musk and White House aides if a Democratic Congress went after them. "I would if I needed to, sure," he said, pointing to President Biden's preemptive pardons in 2025 as his precedent. A presidential pardon covers federal crimes only. It cannot stop Congress from asking questions or erase exposure to state charges, but it can block a federal prosecution before any charge is filed.

The grant rule. In May, the White House budget office proposed a rule that would require senior political appointees to review every discretionary federal grant before it is awarded, and would make it easier to cancel grants that do not match the administration's priorities (Inside Higher Ed). Rep. Rosa DeLauro, the top Democrat on the House Appropriations Committee, says it would put political appointees in charge of virtually all federal funding. The September funding law pauses the rule only until December 11 (ASAHP). If the next funding bill does not extend that pause, the rule can move forward.

So here is the question worth sitting with: if voters fired a majority on Tuesday, should that same majority be allowed to make lifetime decisions on their way out the door?

What can still be saved before January 3

The new majority has no gavel yet, but it is not empty-handed. The biggest lever is the December 11 funding deadline. Most spending bills still need 60 votes in the Senate to end debate, and Republicans hold 53 seats there until January. That means current Democratic senators can refuse to pass a funding bill unless it meets basic conditions.

What that leverage can protect right now:

  • Block the grant rule. Senators can insist the December funding bill keeps the pause on the White House grant rule, or blocks it for good.
  • Keep war money out of the regular budget. The September stopgap left out Iran war funding. Senators can insist the December bill does the same, so any war money has to be voted on in the open.
  • Keep health programs alive. The Community Health Center Fund runs out on December 31 under current law. The National Flood Insurance Program runs out with government funding on December 11. Both can be extended in the same bill.
  • Leave the big budget to the people voters just elected. A short funding extension into early 2027 means the full-year budget is written by the new Congress, not the outgoing one.

What this leverage cannot stop is the party-line bill. Reconciliation needs only a simple majority and cannot be filibustered, so only Republicans can stop it. That is not impossible. Senate Republicans resisted the House plan this summer. Majority Leader Thune has called getting it done in the lame duck "difficult." Senate rules also limit what a budget bill can carry, and some conservatives admit the voter ID money may not survive those rules. If every Democrat and independent votes no, four Republican defections sink it. With only three, the Vice President breaks the tie. Every senator who is coming back in January still has voters to answer to.

Judges are harder. Democrats cannot block them with a filibuster, but they can force every confirmation to take its full floor time. With fewer than 25 working days left, time itself is a defense.

The pardons cannot be stopped by Congress. What can be done now is making sure records are kept. Incoming committee chairs can put agencies and companies on formal notice to preserve documents before January, so that nothing quietly disappears in the handover.

What changes on Day One, without a single new law

Some power arrives as soon as the new members are sworn in and the chambers organize. None of it needs the President's signature.

Questions with teeth. Once the new chambers organize their committees, Democratic chairs can issue subpoenas under committee rules. Rep. Robert Garcia, expected to lead the House Oversight Committee, says his focus is Trump family business dealings, including cryptocurrency ventures that NBC News reports have earned the President more than $1 billion. Rep. Jamie Raskin has said the Judiciary Committee's top priority will be bribery, kickbacks and getting money paid back to the public. Subpoenas can be fought in court, so this takes persistence, not just headlines. A hearing that produces a viral clip but no finding, no correction and no next step is not oversight. A congressional finding is also not a criminal conviction, and the same standard has to apply whether the person under investigation is a Republican, a Democrat or a private contractor.

A closed door on nominees. With at least 54 seats, the Senate decides who gets confirmed. For the next two years, that covers judges, cabinet officers and any Supreme Court vacancy.

Control of the money. Both chambers must agree to any new funding. The new majorities can refuse new war money and write limits into the defense bill. They cannot force the President to sign, but no new funding bill passes without them.

A public agenda. Jeffries has named the first targets: reverse the nearly $1 trillion in Medicaid cuts in the President's 2025 tax and spending law, bring back the Affordable Care Act tax credits that expired at the start of 2026, end the Iran war, and stop the tariffs. Senate leader Chuck Schumer says the first job is lowering costs. Jeffries said no member has asked him to pursue impeachment, and he has not ruled anything in or out.

What will take time and new laws

This is where honesty matters most. Winning Congress is not the same as running the country. Most ordinary bills face a 60-vote hurdle in the Senate if opponents filibuster, then need the President's signature. Overriding a veto takes two thirds of both chambers: 67 senators and 290 House members. Democrats will have about 54 and about 258. The math does not reach.

That does not make the next two years useless. It changes what success looks like. Must-pass bills, like government funding, the defense bill and the debt limit, are where trades happen. Bills that pass the House and Senate and get vetoed still put every vote on the record. And states do not have to wait: voters also flipped governorships in Texas, Georgia, Nevada, Ohio and Iowa, and state laws on voting access, health coverage and prices can move faster than Washington.

The honest timeline is this: some damage can be stopped in December, some can be exposed starting in January, and the deepest repairs depend on whether people stay engaged through 2028.

Why this matters at The Missing Seat

This country has a long record of writing rights down and then failing to enforce them. Black Americans spent generations fighting to use voting rights the Fifteenth Amendment had already promised. Discriminatory laws, intimidation and violence made that promise hollow for many, until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 forced the issue. The lesson is not that elections do not matter. It is that rights need enforcement, and people willing to insist on it.

That is why a last-minute bill paying states to tighten voter ID rules deserves a close look. It is also why the possibility of the first Black Speaker matters but cannot be the finish line. A seat at the table has to come with the power, and the willingness, to act for the people who were left out.

Pull Up A Chair

The election was the start of the job, not the end of it. You do not have to be a Democrat to demand answers: people who voted Republican have the same right to representation, and the standard has to stay the same no matter which party holds power. Here is what one person can do this month.

  1. Call before December 11. The Capitol switchboard is (202) 224-3121. Ask for your current senators and your current representative, not the ones just elected. Ask three questions: Will you vote for a party-line bill that funds the Iran war and offers states money to tighten voter ID? Will you vote to confirm lifetime judges after voters replaced the majority? Will the funding bill keep blocking the White House rule that puts political appointees over federal grants? Then ask where you can read the actual bill, and its bill number.
  2. Write to the winners now. Ask your newly elected members for their first 100 days in writing. Sign up for their newsletters so you can check the promises against the votes.
  3. Check your own health coverage. If you buy insurance through the marketplace, open enrollment for 2027 is happening now. Check the deadline at healthcare.gov and compare prices, because the expired tax credits changed what many people pay.
  4. Watch the record, not the headlines. Every floor vote is posted at congress.gov and on the House and Senate clerk websites. One vote you looked up yourself is worth ten opinions about it.
  5. Look closer to home. Your state legislature and your new governor can act on voting access and costs before Washington does. Find your state lawmakers and show up when they meet.
  6. Americans living abroad: absentee ballot requests through the Federal Post Card Application should be renewed every year. Do it in January, not in October 2028.

Seat Summary

Seat Affected: The voter's seat in Congress, between the election and the swearing-in.

Who is missing: For eight weeks, the majority voters just chose. It is elected but not yet seated, while lawmakers on their way out can still make decisions that last a lifetime.

Who helped: The voters who turned out in places that have not sent a Democrat to the Senate in decades, and the organizers who got them there.

Final Thought

A wave changes who holds the gavel. It does not change what happens to a lifetime judge confirmed in December, a war funded before Christmas, or a voting rule paid for on the way out the door. Those are decided in the next eight weeks, by people who already know they lost. Call before December 11. After January 3, check every promise against the vote.
