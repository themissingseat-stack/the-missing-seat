DEMOCRACY & GOVERNMENT • SPECIAL ELECTION EDITORIAL

The Blue Wave Won Congress. Now Show America What That Victory Is Worth.

By Jansen · November 4, 2026

The Short Version

Congratulations to the winners. Now do the work. Democrats are projected to control both chambers after remarkable upsets in Texas, Kansas, South Carolina and Iowa. But a blue majority is not a repaired government. Congress must use oversight, legislation and the power of the purse to produce documented results, while voters stay involved after election night. Do not vote, celebrate and go silent.

Take action today: Find your representatives, identify one issue that matters to you, ask for a concrete plan and follow up until you receive an answer.

Seat Affected: Voters and communities demanding accountable government · Seat Status: Power changed; results pending

Read the full editorial

Congratulations to the Democrats who won on November 3, and to the voters and volunteers who made those victories possible. This was a remarkable election. Democrats are projected to control both chambers of Congress. Texas elected James Talarico to the Senate for the first Democratic win there since 1988. Kansas elected Adam Hamilton, ending a Democratic Senate drought dating to 1932. Annie Andrews won in South Carolina, Josh Turek won in Iowa, and Democratic gains reached well beyond the usual battlegrounds. These were real upsets, not routine changes in party control.

The expected part was that the president's party faced a difficult midterm. The unexpected part was the scale and geography of the result. Democrats also held critical seats, won competitive House races and benefited from dissatisfaction over living costs, tariffs, the war with Iran and the direction of the country. Hakeem Jeffries is now positioned to become the first Black Speaker of the House, if the incoming chamber elects him. That would be a milestone worth celebrating. But milestones do not pay the rent, restore a violated right or hold an abusive official accountable.

Here is the part that should make every winner uncomfortable: the public did not spend months organizing, arguing, donating and voting merely to replace red nameplates with blue ones. A change in the party holding power is an opportunity to change government conduct. It is not evidence that the conduct has changed. And if the public goes silent now, it will surrender much of the leverage it worked so hard to gain.

The victory was a beginning, not a receipt

The people who voted for Democrats did not all vote for the same reasons. Some wanted an end to policies they believed were harmful. Some wanted relief from high prices. Some wanted stronger constitutional checks. Others were independents or previous Republican voters who decided the administration had failed them. Democrats should resist the temptation to call all of those people ideological converts. Their votes came with expectations, not lifelong membership cards.

Republicans must examine why voters rejected candidates in places that had long been considered safe. Democrats must examine what those voters actually demanded. Neither party should be permitted to substitute campaign language for answers. And citizens should resist the equally dangerous idea that casting a ballot completes their civic responsibility. Democracy is not a two-year subscription that renews automatically when another campaign asks for money.

What Congress can do, and must do

The new majority will not take office until January 2027, and President Donald Trump remains in the White House. Democrats cannot simply order every policy reversed. The president can veto legislation; the Senate has procedural constraints; courts decide cases independently. These are real limits. But they do not erase Congress's own constitutional powers, and no member should use them as a blanket excuse for inaction.

First, Congress must reclaim oversight. That means serious hearings, lawful demands for records, examination of executive spending and federal contracts, investigation of conflicts of interest, and scrutiny of how emergency powers have been used. It means following the evidence even when the person embarrassed is a Democrat. If officials broke laws, identify the conduct and pursue appropriate legal referrals or reforms. If an agency failed, determine who knew, who could have acted and why no effective action followed. A hearing that produces a viral clip but no finding, correction or documented next step is not enough.

Second, Congress must confront the economic pressure that helped shape this election. Families do not need another speech explaining that prices are painful. They need proposals with numbers: housing and healthcare costs, wages, taxes, tariffs, consumer protection and the price of basic necessities. Every major plan should show its estimated cost, who pays, who benefits, when results might be felt and how the public will know whether it worked. No party gets to advertise the benefit and hide the bill.

Third, Congress must defend constitutional rights in practice, not merely in speeches. Voting access, due process, equal protection, freedom of the press, the rights of immigrants and citizens alike, and effective civil rights enforcement all deserve scrutiny. That includes reviewing whether legal protections actually reach people and whether remedies restore anything meaningful after a violation. The country has a long record of writing rights into law while permitting those rights to be denied in practice. We should know better than to confuse a promise with its enforcement.

Fourth, Congress must address ethics and public money. Lawmakers should examine conflicts of interest, disclosure, whistleblower protections, congressional trading and federal programs that consume large budgets without transparent results. But cutting a program is not automatically a success. Neither is preserving it. Ask whether the intended people were reached, whether outcomes improved and whether a change simply transferred the cost to families, states or hospitals. Reward what works, correct what fails and make the evidence public.

Fifth, Congress must investigate the administration's decisions about the war with Iran, including the legal basis for military action, the human and financial costs, congressional authorization and oversight of the conduct of war. Those questions are too consequential to become either a partisan spectacle or a matter the public is told to stop asking about.

What needs to be dismantled

The new Congress should work to dismantle the habits that allow power to escape accountability: secrecy without a lawful reason, retaliation against people who report wrongdoing, discrimination tolerated after notice, contracting arrangements that reward insiders, and the practice of announcing a reform without checking whether the injured people actually received relief. None of these failures belongs exclusively to one party. Their persistence is exactly why changing party control cannot be the final objective.

Some remedies require new legislation. Others can begin with oversight, budget scrutiny, publication of records, improved rules and genuine constituent engagement. When a measure is blocked, lawmakers should tell the public exactly what they proposed, who blocked it and which lawful options remain. A serious Congress can explain its limits without hiding behind them.

The missing seat after election night

For The Missing Seat, this moment has a historical weight. Black Americans spent generations fighting to exercise voting rights supposedly protected by the Fifteenth Amendment. Discriminatory laws, intimidation and violence made the constitutional promise hollow for many, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 was a major response. The lesson is not that elections do not matter. The lesson is that rights require sustained enforcement and people willing to insist upon it.

The possibility of the first Black Speaker matters. So does every new representative whose presence broadens the experiences heard in Congress. But representation cannot stop at a photograph of who took the oath. The seat must have the power and the willingness to act for people whose needs have been ignored. The same test applies to every elected official, whatever their race or party.

Now comes the public's job

If you helped deliver this blue wave, do not disappear. Find out who represents you in the House and Senate. Sign up for their official updates, not just their fundraising emails. Learn which committees they sit on. Ask what oversight they will pursue and what legislation they will prioritize. Request a public timetable and follow up when a promised action is late. Show up at town halls, submit testimony where available, join credible local organizations, and support independent journalism that checks the claims of both parties.

Choose one issue that directly affects your community: a school, a hospital, housing, wages, voting access, veterans' services or the treatment of people by government agencies. Gather reliable evidence. Ask your representative's office for a written response explaining what authority it has and what action it will take. Share that response publicly and respectfully. Then return to the question after thirty, sixty or ninety days. Organized, persistent citizens are harder to dismiss than a burst of applause followed by silence.

You do not have to agree with Democrats to demand accountable government. You do not have to be a Democrat to participate. People who voted Republican have the same right to answers, representation and lawful treatment. The standard must be fair, because otherwise accountability becomes nothing more than another weapon used against whichever side is out of power.

A mandate must be earned again every day

So congratulations. Celebrate the organizers who knocked on doors, the candidates who competed in difficult states, the people who voted for the first time and those who refused to give up on democratic participation. Their work changed the balance of power in Washington. That is no small accomplishment.

But there is no reason to wait until 2028 to discover whether it meant anything. Congress can be judged by the records it obtains, the misconduct it exposes, the legislation it advances, the money it accounts for, the rights it protects and the improvements that reach real people. Citizens can be judged by whether they remain involved after the victory speeches end.

The blue wave won Congress. It did not finish the work. If this election is to become more than a change of color, elected officials must deliver and the public must keep showing up. Silence after victory gives power the same freedom from scrutiny that voters just rejected. Do not hand it back.
