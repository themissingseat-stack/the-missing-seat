The Campaign Was the Ad. We’re Stuck With the Bill.

American candidates can promise tax cuts, new programs and sweeping savings without giving voters one independent receipt for the whole plan. Australia already shows that another way is possible.

Seat Summary

  • Seat Affected: Voters and taxpayers, especially people who cannot absorb higher costs or the loss of public services
  • Seat Status: Asked to choose among promises without one independent receipt for the whole plan
  • Location: United States
  • Theme: Democracy, Elections and Government Accountability

Election campaigns sell us the future.

Lower taxes. Better healthcare. More police. Cheaper housing. Higher wages. Stronger borders. New roads. Smaller government. Bigger benefits.

The promises sound good one at a time. That is how campaigns present them. One speech. One ad. One applause line after another.

But after the election, the promises do not arrive one at a time. They land in the same budget.

That is when the public learns what was missing from the campaign: the bill.

America checks the price too late

The United States already has a respected, nonpartisan Congressional Budget Office. The CBO estimates the budget effects of legislation and helps Congress understand what proposed laws may cost.

That work matters. But CBO’s main job begins when proposals enter the congressional process. It is required to estimate nearly every bill approved by a full House or Senate committee. It does not publish one complete, independent receipt comparing every major presidential candidate’s campaign platform before Americans vote.

That leaves a strange gap.

A candidate can spend months promising large tax changes, new spending, cuts, tariffs or savings. Journalists, universities and private organizations may try to calculate the cost. Campaigns may publish their own numbers. Opponents publish different numbers.

Then voters are expected to decide which math to trust while the people asking for power control the details of their own sales pitch.

The campaign becomes the advertisement. The public gets the invoice later.

Australia already does something America could learn from

Australia has a Parliamentary Budget Office, or PBO. It is independent and nonpartisan.

After a federal election, the PBO publishes an Election Commitments Report showing the budget effects of the major parties’ campaign promises. The report includes individual commitments and their combined effect on the government’s finances. Its 2025 report looked beyond the next few budget years and also showed effects over a ten-year period.

The PBO also allows parliamentarians to request policy costings. During an election caretaker period, publicly announced policies can be submitted for costing and released publicly.

This system is not magic. A costing is an estimate, not a guarantee. The answer depends on the details supplied, the assumptions used and what later happens in the economy. Australia’s PBO says its estimates are not exact numbers, and it identifies promises that cannot be reliably quantified.

That honesty is part of the value.

The public does not need fake certainty. We need an independent referee who can say: Here is the best estimate. Here are the assumptions. Here is what cannot be calculated. Here is what the whole package may do to the budget.

A promise can help one person and send the bill to another

Campaign accounting is not only about the national debt.

If a candidate promises a tax cut without explaining the lost revenue, the bill may return as reduced services, higher borrowing or a different tax. If a candidate promises a new benefit without identifying stable funding, the program may be cut later or never reach the people who counted on it. If promised savings depend on numbers that do not add up, workers and families may carry the correction.

That is why the missing seat belongs to ordinary voters and taxpayers.

Candidates and parties hold the information and write the promises. Wealthy donors, consultants and organized interests often have experts who can study the details for themselves. Most people do not. They are working, raising families and trying to pay their bills.

They should not need an economics degree to learn whether a campaign platform is even financially possible.

Who benefits from the gap?

Every candidate benefits when promises can be judged separately from their total cost.

One promise can be aimed at parents. Another at retirees. Another at businesses. Another at workers. Each group hears what it may receive, but nobody is required to show voters the complete tradeoff before Election Day.

That does not prove every promise is dishonest. It means the system makes exaggeration easier and accountability harder.

An independent election receipt would not tell Americans whom to support. It would give them better information for making that choice.

What America should build

Congress should authorize a nonpartisan election-costing process with clear public rules.

Major presidential candidates should submit enough detail for their proposals to be examined. Each costing should show the expected effect over the next budget years and over a longer period, the assumptions used, important uncertainties and any promise too vague to calculate.

Before Election Day, voters should receive one readable report comparing the total effect of each platform. After the election, a final report should include the full set of commitments made during the campaign.

Participation rules would need care. A government agency must not be allowed to shape an election by helping one party and obstructing another. The same deadlines, methods and disclosure requirements must apply to everyone.

The goal is not to give economists the power to choose the president. Cost is not the same as value. A policy can cost money and still be worth doing. A policy can save money and still harm people.

The goal is simpler: If candidates want control of the public’s money, the public should see an independent receipt before handing them the keys.

Why should an ordinary person care?

Because the bill never stays in Washington.

It reaches the school that loses staff, the family whose health costs rise, the worker whose taxes change, the neighborhood waiting for a promised project and the next generation asked to pay for decisions it never saw clearly.

We would not hire a contractor who refused to provide an estimate, listed only the attractive features and waited until the work began to mention the full price.

We should not accept less from people asking to manage trillions of public dollars.

Pull Up a Chair

The next time a candidate announces a major promise, ask four questions:

  1. What will the entire plan cost, not just this one promise?
  2. Who produced the estimate?
  3. Which assumptions make the number work?
  4. Who pays if the estimate is wrong?

Then ask candidates something bigger: Will you support an independent election receipt that costs every major platform under the same public rules?

Campaigns already know how to make the promise look good.

Voters deserve to see the bill before they vote.

Who is missing?
