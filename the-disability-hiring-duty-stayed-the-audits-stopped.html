DISABILITY RIGHTS • WORK • FEDERAL CONTRACTING • ACCOUNTABILITY

The Disability Hiring Duty Stayed. The Audits Stopped.

Federal contractors still have a legal duty to recruit, employ, advance and accommodate qualified people with disabilities. The government stopped the audits that tested whether those promises were being kept.

Who is missing?

Seat Affected: Disabled applicants and employees seeking work, advancement and reasonable accommodation at federal contractors.
Seat Status: Protected by law; federal compliance audits suspended.
Location: United States.
Theme: Disability rights, work and public accountability.

The federal government spent about $793 billion on contracts in fiscal year 2025. The companies doing that work employ more than one million people. When public money reaches that far into the labor market, the public has a right to expect more than a nondiscrimination slogan in a job advertisement.

Federal law agrees. Section 503 of the Rehabilitation Act requires covered federal contractors to do two things. They may not discriminate against qualified people with disabilities, and certain contractors must take affirmative steps to employ and advance them.

The duty is still there. The audits are not.

In a report publicly released on October 6, 2026, the Government Accountability Office said the Labor Department's Office of Federal Contract Compliance Programs had suspended Section 503 compliance audits in January 2025. Those audits were one of the government's main ways to check whether contractors were following the law.

From fiscal years 2017 through 2025, 532 audits found Section 503 violations. That number is a count of audits with findings. It is not a count of disabled workers harmed, and it does not tell us how many unique companies were involved. It does tell us that the oversight process found real compliance problems when it looked.

A promise without a check

Section 503 is not simply a rule against an employer saying, “We do not hire disabled people.” The current regulations cover recruitment, applications, hiring, promotion, training, pay, job assignments, leave, termination and other conditions of employment. They require reasonable accommodation for a qualified applicant or employee unless the contractor can show undue hardship.

For contractors with at least 50 employees and a federal contract of $50,000 or more, the rules also require a written affirmative action program. That program must be reviewed every year. It must examine personnel processes, job qualifications, recruitment, accommodations, training and the contractor's own internal auditing.

In August 2026, the Labor Department changed parts of these regulations. It removed the former 7 percent disability utilization goal, associated self-identification requirements and some data-analysis provisions. The final rule became effective September 21, 2026, apart from a separate December change to administrative-procedure rules. The department said the old analysis had become unworkable after the executive order supporting some job-group structures was revoked, and it raised concerns about disability inquiries under the Americans with Disabilities Act.

Those changes matter, but they did not erase Section 503. The current rules still prohibit disability discrimination. They still require covered contractors to take affirmative action. They still require accessible notice, outreach and recruitment, reasonable accommodation, review of qualification standards, staff training, and internal audit and reporting systems.

That is why the suspended federal audits matter. A contractor can have a policy on paper and still have an inaccessible application system. It can promise accommodation and leave a manager unsure what to do when somebody asks. It can conduct outreach without checking whether qualified candidates were reached. An internal promise is not the same thing as independent verification.

Who is missing?

The missing people are disabled applicants who never learn why an application process stopped working for them. They are employees who need an accommodation, workers passed over for training or promotion, and people whose qualifications are filtered through assumptions about disability.

They were also missing from the practical result of the oversight decision. When audits stop, the people most affected do not receive a substitute inspection, a public accounting, or a new way to know whether billions in federal contracts are attached to equal opportunity in practice.

The power sits with contractors that control hiring systems and workplace decisions, and with the Labor Department that decides how to monitor them. Contractors benefit from federal business and reduced outside scrutiny. The government may save enforcement time and staff. Disabled workers carry the risk that a legal duty becomes harder to test until somebody files a complaint after the damage is done.

That is not proof that every contractor is violating the law. It is the reason audits exist.

What the receipt shows

GAO did not claim that all federal contractors discriminate or that every audit would uncover a violation. It found that the Labor Department had reduced Section 503 oversight since January 2025 and that the suspension left the department with less assurance that contractors were supporting disability employment.

GAO also found that the Labor Department reduced the OFCCP workforce without strategic workforce planning consistent with key principles for deciding what skills and staffing the office still needed. Contractors and other stakeholders described practical problems with recruiting, retaining, advancing and accommodating disabled workers. They said clearer guidance and examples of successful practice would help.

GAO made eight recommendations. They include resuming Section 503 audits and collection of affirmative-action-program certifications, improving complaint handling, planning the OFCCP workforce, and publishing better resources on recruitment, accommodation, outreach and compliance. The Labor Department agreed with six recommendations and neither agreed nor disagreed with two. GAO still showed all eight recommendations as open when this article was checked on October 21, 2026.

There is a fair counterargument. Agencies have limited staff. Audits take time. Contractors also face changing rules, and compliance work can become paperwork that does little for an actual worker.

That is an argument for smarter oversight, not an oversight vacuum. A useful audit should test whether an applicant can access the hiring process, whether accommodation requests reach somebody who can act, whether job standards are truly necessary, and whether qualified workers have a fair path to advancement. The answer to empty paperwork is accountability tied to outcomes people can recognize.

Why an ordinary person should care

You do not need to work for the federal government to be affected. The contractor may build roads, manage technology, supply hospitals, provide security, manufacture equipment or operate services in your community. Your taxes help pay the bill.

Disability can also enter any family through birth, illness, injury, military service, aging or an event nobody planned. A hiring system that works only for people who never need flexibility or access is not a strong labor market. It is a gate pretending to be neutral.

Public contracting is one of the government's largest economic tools. If Washington can set deadlines, prices, security standards and performance terms for a contract, it can also verify whether the people doing that work have a fair chance to enter and advance.

The action

Ask the Labor Department for a dated public plan to resume Section 503 compliance audits and affirmative-action-program certification. The plan should state what will be reviewed, how workers and applicants can report barriers, how confidential disability information will be protected, and when GAO's eight recommendations will be completed.

If you work for a federal contractor, ask for the company's Section 503 affirmative action program. Current regulations say the full program must be available to employees and applicants for inspection on request. Ask who handles accommodations, how accessible hiring technology is tested, and what the company's internal audit found. A policy becomes real when the people it protects can use it.

The law kept a seat for disabled workers. Oversight is how the public checks whether anybody can actually sit in it.
