The Missing Seat Editorial

The Equality Paradox

Can a policy designed to fight discrimination become discriminatory itself?

That question now shapes arguments about affirmative action, scholarships, internships, hiring, government contracts and workplace diversity programs.

Some White and Asian Americans say race-conscious programs can deny them opportunities because of their race. Many Black Americans and members of other historically excluded groups respond that removing those programs does not remove the unequal conditions that made them necessary.

Both sides use the word equality, but they are often talking about different things.

Equal treatment

One view says government, schools and employers should never give a person an advantage or disadvantage because of race.

From this perspective, even a well-intended program becomes unfair if it treats applicants differently based on race. Each person should be judged as an individual.

That is a real civil-rights concern. Anti-discrimination law protects people of every race.

But it does not answer the entire problem.

Equal opportunity

The other view asks whether people are truly competing from equal starting positions.

For much of American history, Black people were excluded by law and policy from schools, neighborhoods, voting, loans, jobs, property and government contracts. Those exclusions affected who could gain experience, build a business, inherit wealth and qualify for later opportunities.

A rule can look equal today while measuring results created by yesterday’s unequal system.

Imagine two runners. One begins at the normal starting line. The other has been held back for half the race. Releasing the second runner and announcing that the rules are now equal does not remove the distance already created.

That is why some programs consider race. Their supporters believe identical treatment cannot repair an unequal starting position.

Where the argument becomes dishonest

The debate goes wrong when either side refuses to examine evidence.

Supporters of a race-conscious program should be able to identify the inequality, explain how the program addresses it and measure whether the gap actually becomes smaller.

Opponents should not assume that ending the program produces fairness simply because the new rule no longer mentions race. They should examine whether the older exclusion remains built into the qualifications, networks and wealth being measured.

The important question is not only, “Did this program use race?”

It is also, “What inequality existed, what produced it, what did the program change and what remained unequal after it ended?”

A Black beneficiary is not automatically proof of repair

A company can hire several Black workers and still leave a larger employment gap unchanged. A university can admit Black students without repairing unequal schools or family wealth. A government can announce funding without ensuring that Black businesses receive durable contracts and build lasting capacity.

The Missing Seat will not call every benefit a completed remedy. We will ask whether the underlying difference actually narrowed and whether the improvement lasted.

That same standard should apply to claims of reverse discrimination. Identify the person harmed, the opportunity lost, the rule responsible and the evidence connecting them.

Nobody should be reduced to a political symbol.

Why should an ordinary person care?

These policies influence who attends schools, receives jobs, wins contracts and builds wealth. Vague slogans about “merit” or “diversity” can hide the real decisions being made.

Merit is not created in a vacuum. Access to good schools, unpaid internships, professional networks, transportation and inherited money can all affect who appears most qualified at the final decision.

That does not mean standards should disappear. It means we should examine how people gained the chance to meet them.

Pull Up a Chair

When an institution announces or ends an equality program, ask for four things:

  1. The specific inequality the program was meant to address.
  2. The evidence showing what caused that inequality.
  3. The measurable results while the program operated.
  4. What will prevent the inequality from returning after the program ends.

Equality cannot mean forgetting how the starting line was built.

Who is missing?
