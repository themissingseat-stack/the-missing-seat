Government Power &amp; Accountability

The Government Isn’t the Public’s Boss

Seat Summary

  • Seat affected: People whose protected rights were violated but who received no effective judicial remedy
  • Seat status: Recognized as members of the public, excluded from the final design of accountability
  • Institutional question: What must happen after courts or existing law leave a proven or credibly documented rights violation without repair?
  • Proposed public demand: Create an independent Public Rights and Remedy Commission with investigative authority, limited corrective power, a compensation process and the ability to trigger expedited congressional consideration

We pay taxes. That money funds the agencies that regulate us, investigate us, arrest us, determine our benefits, educate our children and defend the government when we sue it.

That does not make every public worker our personal employee. It also does not give each taxpayer the right to cancel a law they dislike.

But an election does not transfer ownership of the country from the public to government officials. Public power still belongs to the public.

The difficult question begins when government violates a protected right and the injured person receives no meaningful repair.

How can a right exist without a remedy?

The Constitution and federal laws protect many rights. But having a right does not guarantee that a court can compensate every violation.

Sometimes an official is protected by immunity. Immunity is a legal rule that can prevent certain lawsuits against government officials, even when the person suing says an official violated a right.

Sometimes the government itself is protected by sovereign immunity, meaning it cannot be sued unless it has agreed to allow that kind of case.

Sometimes no law gives the person a right to demand money damages. In cases involving federal officials, the Supreme Court has also sharply limited when courts may create a damages claim that Congress never specifically authorized.

These rules can cause a case to end without a court deciding that the government acted properly.

That difference is crucial. “The court could not provide this remedy” is not the same as “no right was violated.”

What happens after the case closes?

Usually, very little.

The injured person goes home without repair. Congress may never hear the person’s name. The agency may describe the dismissal as a legal victory. News reports may say the government won, even though the court decided only that the person had no available path to compensation.

The system treats the end of the lawsuit as the end of the government’s responsibility.

That is where accountability disappears.

A Public Rights and Remedy Commission

The Missing Seat proposes an independent Public Rights and Remedy Commission.

The commission would examine cases in which substantial evidence suggests that a government official or institution violated a protected right, but an immunity rule or another legal barrier prevented an effective remedy.

It would not retry every lawsuit, and it could not simply overrule a final court judgment. Its job would be to investigate what the judgment did and did not decide.

The commission could obtain government records, hear witnesses and publish a plain-language report answering basic questions:

  • What happened?
  • What right was involved?
  • Did the court reject the facts, or did a legal barrier block the remedy?
  • What harm remains?
  • Which official or institution can still correct it?
  • What change could keep the same gap from harming someone else?

Congress could give the commission limited power to order agencies to reconsider certain administrative decisions, correct records or return property where existing law allows it. Congress could also create a fund for qualified claims and require lawmakers to consider larger or unusual cases.

Congress should not be allowed to bury the finding

When the commission identifies a serious accountability gap, its recommendation should receive a public hearing and a prompt congressional vote.

Congress could accept, reject or change the proposal. What it should not be able to do is make the injured person disappear by leaving the report untouched in a committee forever.

No law can force every future Congress to act courageously. Each chamber controls many of its own procedures. But a public deadline and recorded vote would at least show who chose repair and who chose silence.

Who would watch the commission?

A body created to investigate unaccountable power could become another source of unaccountable power.

Its members should therefore come from more than one political party and more than one branch of government. They should serve staggered terms, disclose conflicts, publish their votes and face outside audits. Current elected officials, agency employees, party officers and lobbyists should not control it.

Its decisions should be written in plain language and reviewable to make sure the commission followed the law.

Why should an ordinary person care?

An election can change future policy. It cannot restore one person’s lost liberty, property, health or family life.

A protected right should not become more enforceable because the injured person is wealthy, famous, knows a powerful lawyer or manages to create a national scandal.

Pull Up a Chair

When a government lawsuit is dismissed, ask why. Did the court decide that no violation occurred, or did it decide that the law offered no remedy?

Share that distinction. Ask civil-rights organizations and lawmakers whether they support a permanent public process for unresolved government-caused injuries.

The demand is not merely, “Give citizens a voice.” Government can listen and keep walking.

The demand is accountability that cannot disappear through immunity, procedure or silence.

Who is missing?

Ally = Action. 🪑

Verification Notice

This editorial proposes a new institution. It does not claim that a Public Rights and Remedy Commission currently exists. Existing federal bodies and compensation programs demonstrate components of the model, but none has the complete jurisdiction or authority proposed here. A commission could not simply reverse a final Article III court judgment. Congress would need to define its jurisdiction, corrective powers, evidentiary standards, compensation authority and funding. Expedited legislative procedures can create deadlines and privileged consideration, but either chamber retains constitutional authority over its own rules and may later alter or waive those procedures.
