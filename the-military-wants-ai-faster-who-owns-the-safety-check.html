The Military Wants AI Faster. Who Owns the Safety Check?

By Jansen · October 30, 2026

The Short Version

  • What happened: GAO's October 14 report identified responsible-AI staffing and collaboration gaps. Its three-of-ten staffing number is dated February 2026, not a verified current headcount.
  • Who it affects: Service members, civilians, oversight staff and taxpayers who depend on a functioning chain of responsibility.
  • What to do: Ask congressional representatives for an unclassified current staffing account, council charter and meeting schedule, and a process for assigning unresolved implementation problems.

Seat Affected: Service members, civilians and public accountability · Seat Status: Current oversight accounting needed

Read the full story

The military wants to use artificial intelligence faster, and service members have a reasonable interest in tools that help them do their jobs well. Better information, maintenance and logistics can matter. So does knowing who has checked a system before someone is expected to rely on it under pressure.

The Pentagon has already made that promise. Its ethical principles call for responsible, equitable, traceable, reliable and governable AI. The question is how those words become a working chain of responsibility, with people who have the time and authority to carry it out.

An October 14 Government Accountability Office report gives a useful, limited answer. Most of the ten selected Defense components surveyed had taken initial oversight steps. GAO also identified gaps in the central office helping them implement responsible AI. The report supports neither a claim that safeguards are absent nor a verdict that military AI is generally unsafe. It shows why adopting principles and providing the capacity to follow them are separate jobs.

The staffing number has a date

Officials told GAO that three of the Responsible AI Division's ten authorized positions were filled as of February 2026. They said staffing shortages hindered additional implementation work. That is a documented condition at a particular time, not a verified headcount for today.

That distinction matters. An old vacancy figure should not become a permanent accusation simply because nobody has supplied an update. The proper next step is a current accounting of the work, the people available to do it and any gap between them. Filling posts is one possible response; officials should also explain whether the responsibilities, skills or organizational arrangements have changed.

GAO also records an important change in direction: the January 2026 strategy emphasized lawful military applications without model usage-policy constraints that could limit them. Officials described restructuring and aligning earlier guidance with that strategy as part of the staffing context. A current accounting should explain the duties under that changed approach, rather than assume the office is simply carrying on unchanged.

The underlying management question remains practical. People promoting a technology tend to have a mission and a delivery timetable. People checking its use need an equally clear assignment. A promise to consider safety does not establish who can require another test, resolve conflicting guidance or make sure a known problem reaches another military component.

Service members should not have to reconstruct that chain for themselves while trying to perform the task in front of them. The public should be able to ask whether the chain exists without demanding classified operational details.

Collaboration needs an owner

GAO reported that the Responsible AI Working Council did not meet from late 2024 until August 2025. It resumed, but GAO found that it lacked a charter defining responsibilities and meeting frequency. Four surveyed components described challenges that would have benefited from collaboration during the gap; six saw value in sharing resources and practices.

A meeting is not a safety result. Neither is a charter. Their value depends on whether a problem raised in one place reaches the people who can act on it, produces a decision and leads to a change where necessary. A forum without defined responsibility can leave that process dependent on whoever happens to keep it going.

The department's own work also deserves recognition. GAO describes guidance, a toolkit and technical exchanges intended to help components apply the principles. Those are assets to build on. The useful test is whether they reach the people making decisions and whether the office supporting that work can sustain it.

As of October 30, GAO's public tracker still marked its two new recommendations open: issue a council charter and address the division's workforce needs. Defense concurred with both. An open recommendation means GAO has not confirmed sufficient action to close it. It does not, by itself, prove that no work has occurred since the report.

Who carries the consequence?

The missing seats belong to service members expected to rely on the systems, civilians who may be affected by military decisions and the staff responsible for making safeguards usable. These groups do not necessarily control procurement or deployment. Their interests need to appear in the decisions made before a system becomes routine.

Congress controls legislation, oversight and appropriations. Department leaders control assignments and resources. Military components apply the guidance in particular programmes. Contractors supply technology, but supplying it does not transfer the government's responsibility for public authority to the vendor.

Useful AI can benefit military personnel and the public. Contractors can earn revenue from supplying it. The burden of a poor decision can fall elsewhere, including on people who never agreed to participate. That is a reason to demand a credible process, not evidence that any particular contract or system has caused harm.

For taxpayers, there is also a familiar spending question. Buying a capability creates continuing work: testing it, monitoring its use, maintaining knowledge and dealing with problems. Treating that work as optional can make the purchase look simpler than the responsibility that follows it.

The strongest objection is that the military cannot expose its capabilities or allow every useful tool to disappear into endless review. Some secrecy protects people and operations. Bureaucratic activity can consume time without improving a decision. Both points are fair, and neither prevents officials from explaining the management structure in an unclassified form.

Ask for the current accounting

Readers can ask their House member or senators, particularly those serving on armed-services committees, to seek four things: the division's current staffing and responsibilities; the adopted council charter, if completed; its meeting schedule; and how unresolved implementation problems are assigned and followed to a decision. The request should allow legitimate operational details to remain protected while requiring an answer about management.

Then compare that answer with GAO's dated findings and recommendation status. A later follow-up should report what changed, whether the arrangement is functioning and what evidence supports that conclusion. It should not keep recycling February's vacancies as though time stopped there.

Responsible AI will not be established by a reassuring label, and speed alone cannot tell the public whether a system is ready. The department has made commitments and begun the work. It now owes service members and the public a clear account of who is responsible for carrying those commitments through.
