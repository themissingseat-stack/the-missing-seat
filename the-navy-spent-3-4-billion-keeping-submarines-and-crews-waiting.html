Government Accountability &amp; Military Spending

The Navy Spent $3.4 Billion Keeping Submarines and Crews Waiting

Seat Summary

  • Seat Affected: Sailors, military families and taxpayers
  • Seat Status: Asked to absorb the cost of preventable delay
  • Location: United States
  • Theme: Government Accountability, Military Readiness and Public Spending
  • Proposed filename: `the-navy-spent-3-4-billion-keeping-submarines-and-crews-waiting.html`

The United States Navy has 44 attack submarines. They gather intelligence, track threats and can strike targets while remaining difficult to detect.

They cannot perform those missions while sitting at a pier waiting for maintenance.

According to the Government Accountability Office, often called the GAO, maintenance delays and idle time cost the Navy more than 15,000 operational days between fiscal years 2016 and 2025. The estimated cost was $3.4 billion.

The GAO is Congress’s independent watchdog. Its job is to examine how the federal government spends public money and whether programs work as promised.

Its finding means the Navy spent billions supporting submarines and crews during periods when those submarines provided no usable military capability.

This was not one broken submarine

The GAO examined ten years of records and spoke with Navy officials. It found several connected problems.

Some active submarines waited because shipyards could not begin maintenance on time. Other, older submarines waited to be taken out of service because the Navy lacked enough dry-dock space. A dry dock is the special facility needed to lift a vessel out of the water for major work.

The result was a traffic jam. Submarines needing repairs waited. Submarines ready for retirement also occupied limited space. Delays grew, and the Navy kept paying for crews and equipment attached to submarines it could not use.

Who carried the burden?

Taxpayers paid $3.4 billion without receiving the readiness that money was supposed to buy.

Sailors and their families also carried the consequences. Maintenance schedules affect training, deployments, assignments and family plans. When one submarine is unavailable, the remaining fleet may have to do more work.

That is why this is not just a story about military hardware. It is about people being asked to organize their lives around a system that was not organized well enough to use their work.

Supporting the troops requires accountability

Questioning military spending is not the same as opposing the military. If anything, refusing to examine waste leaves service members with the consequences while contractors and institutions continue receiving money.

National defense is expensive. Safe repairs require skilled workers, specialized facilities and careful planning. Unexpected problems will happen.

But a ten-year pattern is not an unexpected emergency. It is a management problem.

The public deserves to know when officials first understood that maintenance capacity was insufficient, what plans they approved, who missed the deadlines and what changed afterward.

Why should an ordinary person care?

Government budgets involve choices. Every billion dollars trapped in preventable delay is money that cannot be used somewhere else, including for service members’ housing, veterans’ care, infrastructure or other public needs.

The same accountability demanded from schools, benefit programs and local governments should apply to the military. A large budget should require more public scrutiny, not less.

Pull Up a Chair

When elected officials describe a military budget as “supporting the troops,” ask how much of the money reaches readiness, personnel and families, and how much is consumed by delay.

Follow GAO reports. Share the actual findings instead of arguing only about the total defense budget. Ask members of Congress who oversee the Navy what deadlines and public measurements will show that the maintenance backlog is shrinking.

The sailor waiting, the family absorbing the disruption and the taxpayer funding it all deserve more than another promise that lessons were learned.

They deserve the receipt.

Who is missing?

Ally = Action. 🪑

Verification Notice

GAO analyzed Navy data for fiscal years 2016 through 2025 and estimated $3.4 billion in costs associated with more than 15,000 lost operational days. Its forward projection exceeds 14,000 inactive idle days and $3.1 billion from fiscal years 2026 through 2030 if the Navy does not mitigate the problem. The Navy verbally agreed with GAO's two recommendations, which remained open when the report was released.
