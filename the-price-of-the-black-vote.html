POLITICS & ACCOUNTABILITY · JULY 21, 2026

The Price of the Black Vote

Black voters are repeatedly told what is at stake. They are rarely allowed to ask what their support purchased.

Seat Summary

🪑 Seat Affected: The Seat of Political Power

📍 Location: United States

⚖️ Seat Status: Courted during elections. Too often undervalued after them.

🏛️ Theme: Black Voters • Political Accountability • Representation • Voting Rights • Public Policy

Every election season, Black Americans hear some version of the same warning:

This election is too important to sit out.

Democracy is at stake. Civil rights are at stake. The courts are at stake. Our communities are at stake.

Often, those warnings are true.

But another question deserves an answer:

If Black votes are essential to winning elections, what do Black communities receive for supplying them?

That is not political disloyalty.

It is political accountability.

A Constituency—or an Emergency Service?

Black voters have been among the Democratic Party’s most dependable constituencies for decades.

According to Pew Research Center’s analysis of validated voters, 83% of Black voters supported Kamala Harris in the 2024 presidential election, while 15% supported Donald Trump.

Trump’s share nearly doubled from the 8% he received among Black voters in 2020. Even so, Black voters remained overwhelmingly Democratic in 2024.

That support was not incidental.

Pew projected that approximately 34.4 million Black Americans would be eligible to vote in 2024. In Georgia, one of the country’s most closely watched political battlegrounds, Black Americans represented approximately one-third of eligible voters.

Campaigns understand these numbers.

That is why candidates return to Black churches, barbershops, historically Black colleges and universities, civil-rights commemorations, community organizations, and Black media outlets when elections become close.

Black voters are regularly described as the backbone of the Democratic Party.

But a backbone should not be noticed only when the body is in danger of collapsing.

Protection From the Alternative Is Not a Complete Agenda

Democratic leaders can point to real accomplishments: expanded access to healthcare, judicial appointments, infrastructure investment, support for historically Black colleges and universities, clemency and sentencing reforms, federal contracting initiatives, and historically low Black unemployment during parts of the Biden administration.

Those achievements matter.

Republicans can also identify policies they believe benefited Black Americans, including pre-pandemic employment gains, permanent federal funding for historically Black colleges and universities, criminal-justice reform through the First Step Act, and Opportunity Zones.

Those measures should also be examined honestly.

But neither party should be permitted to substitute a list of announcements for evidence of lasting improvement.

A program benefiting some Black Americans is not automatically a transformation of Black economic life.

A historically low unemployment rate does not erase the racial wealth gap.

A Black cabinet secretary does not guarantee that Black families can afford housing.

A criminal-justice reform bill does not finish the work of criminal-justice reform.

Representation matters. Results matter more.

The Bill Is Still Unpaid

In June 2026, the national unemployment rate was 4.2%, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

For Black Americans, it was 6.6%.

For White Americans, it was 3.6%.

Those figures do not prove that one president or one political party created the disparity.

They demonstrate something larger: regardless of which party controls Washington, Black unemployment remains substantially higher than White unemployment.

The same test should be applied across public policy:

  • Has the Black homeownership gap meaningfully narrowed?
  • Has the racial wealth gap decreased?
  • Are predominantly Black schools adequately funded?
  • Has maternal mortality improved for Black women?
  • Are Black farmers receiving equal access to federal assistance?
  • Are Black-owned businesses receiving a fair share of public contracts and affordable capital?
  • Are voting protections stronger than they were before the Supreme Court weakened federal preclearance in Shelby County v. Holder?
  • Can Black communities elect representatives of their choice, or are their votes being divided, concentrated, or diluted through redistricting?

These are not symbolic questions.

They determine whether political participation produces measurable power.

What Democrats Cannot Keep Doing

Democrats cannot continue treating fear of Republican government as the full value of their offer to Black voters.

“You cannot afford the other side” may be a persuasive warning. It is not a governing vision.

A party cannot praise Black women for saving democracy, criticize Black men for considering alternatives, depend on Black churches for turnout, and then become vague when asked what policies were specifically delivered, funded, enforced, and protected.

Black voters do not owe any political party permanent gratitude for legislation passed generations ago.

The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 were monumental achievements.

They were also responses to constitutional and civil rights Black Americans had already been denied.

Protecting those laws today is a basic democratic obligation—not a favor that purchases unlimited political loyalty.

What Republicans Cannot Keep Doing

Republicans cannot demand serious consideration from Black voters while supporting policies or legal arguments that weaken the mechanisms protecting Black political representation.

They cannot promote themselves as the party of opportunity while dismissing every measurable racial disparity as grievance, victimhood, or personal failure.

They cannot ask Black Americans to ignore offensive rhetoric, attacks on diversity programs, resistance to voting protections, and attempts to minimize the continuing effects of government-enforced discrimination.

A few campaign appearances, pardons, or favorable economic statistics cannot establish lasting trust by themselves.

If Republicans want more Black votes, the question is not whether Democrats deserve them.

The question is: What will Republicans protect, build, and deliver for Black communities—and how will the results be measured?

Black Voters Are Not One Political Mind

There is no single “Black vote.”

Black Americans are conservative, progressive, moderate, independent, religious, secular, wealthy, working-class, Southern, Northern, rural, suburban, and urban.

They disagree about immigration, policing, abortion, education, gender, taxation, foreign policy, and the proper role of government.

No candidate is entitled to their support because of race, party history, or the identity of the person at the top of the ticket.

That does not mean history should be ignored.

It means history should inform a decision without making that decision compulsory.

Political freedom must include the right to compare parties, withhold support, demand concessions, vote strategically, or decide that neither candidate has earned enthusiastic endorsement.

The Black-Voter Scorecard

Before the 2026 midterm elections, every candidate seeking substantial Black support should be asked to provide clear answers in writing:

  1. What specific Black disparities does your agenda address?
  2. What legislation, funding, or enforcement action will you support?
  3. What measurable outcome should voters expect?
  4. What is the deadline for achieving it?
  5. Who will be responsible if the promise is not kept?
  6. Will you publicly report the results after the election?

Not a slogan.

Not a commemorative speech.

Not a photograph inside a Black church.

A commitment that can be measured.

The scorecard should track outcomes in voting access, employment, wages, wealth, housing, healthcare, education, business investment, environmental safety, and criminal justice.

It should also distinguish between three very different things:

  • A campaign proposal
  • A policy that was actually enacted
  • A policy that produced measurable results

Too often, politicians receive credit for all three when they have accomplished only the first.

The Real Price

The price of the Black vote is not a payment, a special privilege, or a promise that government will serve only Black Americans.

It is accountable representation.

Black taxpayers fund the government. Black workers sustain the economy. Black service members defend the country. Black families live with the consequences of public policy.

Asking what their votes produce is no more divisive than farmers, business owners, veterans, unions, or retirees asking what government will do for them.

The question is not:

Which party owns the Black vote?

Neither does.

The question is: Which candidates have earned it—and what evidence will prove that the investment was worthwhile?

Black voters have spent generations helping to preserve and expand American democracy.

They are entitled to receive more than another receipt marked:

Thank you. See you in four years.

Who is missing?

The Black voter who is welcomed as a supporter but excluded as a negotiator.

Who benefited?

Political parties, candidates, campaign organizations, and elected officials who receive Black votes without facing a consistent public system for measuring what they delivered afterward.

What can I do?

Stop measuring candidates only by what they say about the opposing party.

Ask what they will enact, fund, defend, and measure.

Save campaign promises. Contact elected representatives after the election. Track legislation, budgets, court appointments, executive actions, and local outcomes.

Support organizations that protect voting access and help communities understand how public policy affects their daily lives.

Return to the Seat

After the 2026 midterm elections, The Missing Seat will return to this seat and compare campaign promises with enacted policy.

We will ask:

  • What was promised?
  • What passed?
  • What was funded?
  • What was blocked—and by whom?
  • What measurably changed in Black lives?
  • Which candidates returned after Election Day?

The value of a vote cannot be measured by the applause it receives when it is cast. It must be measured by what happens after it has been counted.

Who is missing?
