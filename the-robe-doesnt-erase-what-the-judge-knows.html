COURTS & ACCOUNTABILITY

The Robe Doesn’t Erase What the Judge Knows

A judge’s recorded prejudice raises a question retirement alone cannot answer: What happened in the cases already decided?

Seat Affected: The Seat of an Impartial Tribunal
Seat Status: Constitutionally protected, institutionally dependent
Location: United States
Theme: Judicial bias, recusal, due process, institutional accountability and remedies

Judges are human beings.

They have histories.

They have beliefs, experiences, relationships, assumptions, fears and blind spots.

The robe does not erase any of that.

What the robe is supposed to do is impose a duty.

A judge must separate personal belief from legal judgment.

A person entering a courtroom should not have to wonder whether the result was determined before the evidence was heard.

That principle sounds obvious.

The harder question begins when evidence appears suggesting that a judge may not have done it.

If a judge’s own words reveal prejudice serious enough to question future impartiality, what happens to the people whose freedom, property, families or rights were already placed in that judge’s hands?

Removing the judge answers only one question.

Suppose a judge retires.

Or resigns.

Or is disciplined.

Or is reassigned.

That may prevent the judge from deciding another case.

It does not automatically answer whether earlier cases were affected.

Those are two different accountability questions.

Question One: Should this judge continue exercising judicial power?

Question Two: Do prior decisions require review?

Institutions often become much more comfortable with the first question than the second.

The first can be resolved prospectively.

The second requires reopening the record.

It may require identifying defendants, litigants, sentencing decisions, credibility determinations, bail rulings, family-court orders, suppression hearings or discretionary judgments.

It may require admitting that a problem did not begin on the day the institution finally acknowledged it.

Due process requires more than a courtroom.

The Constitution does not guarantee that every judge will be free of every opinion.

It does require an impartial tribunal when judicial bias creates an unconstitutional risk of unfairness.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that certain circumstances create a constitutionally unacceptable probability of bias.

In Tumey v. Ohio, the Court addressed a judge with a financial interest in conviction.

In Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co., the Court held that extraordinary campaign support created a serious risk of actual bias requiring recusal.

In Williams v. Pennsylvania, a state supreme court justice participated in a death-penalty case after previously being personally involved in approving the decision to seek death when he was district attorney.

The principle running through those cases is larger than any one fact pattern.

Justice cannot depend entirely on whether a judge believes himself or herself to be impartial.

Sometimes the structure itself creates too great a risk.

Federal law already recognizes the appearance problem.

Federal recusal law requires a judge to disqualify himself or herself in proceedings where impartiality might reasonably be questioned.

The standard matters.

It does not ask only:

Did the judge secretly intend to discriminate?

It asks whether the circumstances create a reasonable question about impartiality.

That is significant because public confidence in courts depends on more than the private conscience of the person wearing the robe.

Courts demand trust from people over whom they exercise extraordinary power.

They issue warrants.

They determine bail.

They decide whether evidence reaches a jury.

They evaluate credibility.

They impose sentences.

They decide custody.

They dismiss claims.

They decide whether government conduct crossed a constitutional line.

A system demanding that level of trust cannot treat reasonable questions about impartiality as merely a public-relations problem.

Bias does not have to announce itself in the judgment.

This is where retrospective review becomes difficult.

A biased decision does not necessarily contain a sentence saying:

I ruled this way because of prejudice.

Judicial discretion often operates inside ordinary-looking decisions.

Was the defendant believable?

Was the explanation credible?

Was the conduct threatening?

Was the sentence appropriate?

Was the police officer more credible than the person arrested?

Was remorse genuine?

Was a parent responsible?

Was the lawyer’s objection persuasive?

Those judgments can be enormously consequential while leaving very little documentary evidence of what influenced them.

That is precisely why evidence of prejudice outside a particular opinion can matter.

The strongest counterargument is finality.

Courts cannot reopen every case whenever troubling information about a judge later emerges.

Final judgments serve important purposes.

Evidence disappears.

Witnesses die.

Victims need closure.

Governments and private parties need to know when litigation has ended.

A legal system incapable of finality would become unstable.

That argument is serious.

It is also incomplete.

Finality is a legal value.

It is not a magic word that converts an unreliable process into a reliable one.

The question is not whether every old case must automatically be overturned. The question is whether credible evidence of judicial prejudice triggers a duty to determine which cases require review.

Review does not have to mean automatic reversal.

There are intermediate steps.

An institution could identify cases involving substantial judicial discretion.

It could examine whether particular racial, ethnic, religious or other groups received systematically different outcomes.

It could prioritize incarceration, sentencing, bail, juvenile cases, custody determinations and other decisions involving significant liberty interests.

It could provide notice to affected litigants or their attorneys.

It could appoint independent reviewers.

It could preserve records.

It could permit motions for reconsideration where a litigant can show that the newly discovered evidence is relevant to a discretionary ruling.

It could publish aggregate findings without pretending that statistics alone prove discrimination in each individual case.

None of those measures requires declaring every prior judgment invalid.

They require taking the evidence seriously.

Who should conduct the review?

This is where institutional design matters.

If the same court whose legitimacy is being questioned controls the entire investigation, decides what evidence matters, determines which cases qualify, and issues the final judgment about whether anything went wrong, public confidence may remain damaged even if the internal review is sincere.

The appearance problem does not disappear simply because the institution investigating itself is a court.

Independent review can take many forms.

Another court.

A specially appointed judicial panel.

A disciplinary body.

An inspector, commission or outside counsel with access to records.

Legislative oversight where appropriate.

The exact mechanism will depend on jurisdiction.

The principle is simpler:

An institution should not assume that its own confidence in itself is sufficient evidence for everyone else.

What the judge knew can matter.

Judges are not blank slates.

Nor should they be.

Judicial knowledge can improve decision-making.

Understanding history can illuminate how a law operates.

Understanding policing can illuminate a suppression hearing.

Understanding discrimination can illuminate an employment case.

Understanding institutional practices can illuminate whether a supposedly neutral rule produces foreseeable consequences.

Judicial neutrality cannot mean pretending not to know documented facts.

But knowledge and prejudice are not the same thing.

The obligation is to distinguish evidence from assumption.

A judge should not treat a Black defendant as dangerous because of race.

A judge also should not erase documented racial disparities because acknowledging them feels politically uncomfortable.

Neutrality is not ignorance.

Neutrality is disciplined judgment.

The institution’s response becomes part of the evidence.

When credible evidence of prejudice emerges, the response tells the public something.

Does the institution preserve the record?

Does it identify potentially affected cases?

Does it notify people?

Does it invite independent examination?

Does it publish findings?

Or does it treat departure from office as the end of the matter?

Retirement can stop future harm.

It cannot establish that earlier decisions were unaffected.

Silence cannot establish that either.

Who is missing?

The defendant whose sentence depended partly on a judge’s view of character.

The parent whose credibility determined custody.

The person denied bail.

The litigant whose discrimination claim was dismissed.

The lawyer who suspected something was wrong but had no evidence at the time.

The family living with a judgment long after the judge who issued it has left the bench.

And the public, which is routinely asked to trust judicial decisions without having access to everything the judge thought, knew or believed.

One Action

When a judicial-bias controversy appears in your jurisdiction, do not stop at:

Did the judge resign?

Ask:

  • How many cases did the judge decide?
  • Which involved incarceration, sentencing, bail, custody or major discretionary judgments?
  • Has anyone reviewed those cases?
  • Who conducted the review?
  • Were affected litigants notified?
  • Were the findings published?
  • Is there a process for requesting reconsideration?

Those questions do not presume guilt.

They ask the institution to show its work.

The Receipt

American law already recognizes that judicial impartiality is fundamental.

Federal law requires recusal where impartiality might reasonably be questioned.

Supreme Court precedent recognizes circumstances in which the probability of bias becomes constitutionally unacceptable.

Judicial ethics rules repeatedly emphasize independence, integrity and impartiality.

The unresolved problem is often not whether impartiality matters.

It is what institutions do when evidence arrives late.

Return to the Seat

The judicial robe represents authority.

It also represents restraint.

The person wearing it is given extraordinary power precisely because the public is promised that personal prejudice will not decide the case.

When credible evidence puts that promise in doubt, removing the robe cannot be the entire answer.

The robe can come off in a day. The judgments remain.

Accountability therefore cannot end with:

The judge is gone.

It has to continue with:

Who sat before that judge, what happened to them, and who is going to look?

Verification Note: This editorial addresses the legal and institutional problem created when credible evidence raises questions about judicial prejudice or impartiality. It does not presume that every decision made by a questioned judge was biased, nor that evidence of prejudice automatically invalidates every prior judgment. The constitutional, statutory and ethical authorities cited above establish the importance of an impartial tribunal and recusal where appropriate. Whether a particular prior case requires reopening depends on the governing law, the evidence and the procedural posture of that case.
