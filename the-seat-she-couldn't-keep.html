The Seat She Couldn't Keep

Sometimes a seat becomes empty before a single vote is cast.

Seat Affected: The Seat of Representation
Seat Status: Removed Before Election Day
Location: Massachusetts, USA
Theme: Representation • Democracy • Ballot Access

Democracy depends on voters choosing between qualified candidates. Before that choice can happen, however, candidates must first be allowed onto the ballot.

Jordan Korgood understands how fragile that opportunity can be.

In 2023, while studying at Northeastern University in Boston, she became unhoused. For nearly five months she slept in her car, in public libraries, and wherever she could safely find shelter while continuing her studies and her community work.

Two years later, at just 24 years old, she launched a campaign for the Massachusetts Governor's Council, the elected body responsible for confirming judicial nominees throughout the Commonwealth.

It quickly became a serious campaign. She earned endorsements from local Democratic leaders, built a volunteer network, and attracted thousands of supporters online.

Then the campaign stopped—not because voters rejected her, but because of a legal challenge.

One of her opponents argued that Korgood had not satisfied Massachusetts' five-year residency requirement. During the months she was unhoused, she had remained registered using her mother's previous New York address because she no longer had a permanent residence of her own.

Korgood maintained that she had continuously lived in Massachusetts since 2019 and presented supporting documentation including apartment leases and participation in city programs.

On June 18, 2026, the Massachusetts State Ballot Law Commission ruled that she was ineligible to appear on the ballot.

She appealed.

The legal battle reportedly consumed roughly 40 percent of her campaign funds and thousands of dollars in legal expenses. On July 10, she suspended her campaign.

Why It Matters

Election laws exist for important reasons. Residency requirements and filing rules help protect the integrity of elections and ensure candidates meet legal qualifications.

The debate raised by this case is not whether those rules should exist.

It is whether they are being applied consistently.

Reporting by the Washington Blade highlights similar procedural challenges involving transgender candidates in Michigan and Ohio. In some cases, those candidates ultimately prevailed. In others, the legal costs alone became a significant barrier to continuing their campaigns.

Legal scholars interviewed in the reporting argue that courts may eventually need to examine whether ballot-access rules are being enforced equally across candidates or whether certain groups face disproportionate scrutiny.

Representation is not only about winning elections. It is also about having a fair opportunity to stand before voters.

Who is missing?

Candidates who have earned community support but never receive the opportunity to let voters decide because the race ends before Election Day.

Who helped?

Journalists continued reporting on the case. Legal advocates examined broader patterns affecting ballot access. Volunteers, donors, and community members continued supporting candidates facing costly procedural challenges.

Despite suspending her campaign, Jordan Korgood thanked those supporters, saying she was grateful to have earned the attention and support of thousands of people throughout the race.

What can I do?

Learn how ballot-access laws work where you live.

Support organizations such as the LGBTQ+ Victory Fund that help LGBTQ+ candidates navigate legal and procedural challenges.

Ask whether election rules are being enforced consistently for every candidate, regardless of identity or background.
