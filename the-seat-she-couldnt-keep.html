The Missing Seat Editorial

The Seat She Couldn’t Keep

Jordan Korgood’s campaign ended before voters had the chance to accept or reject her.

That does not automatically mean election officials acted unlawfully. It does raise an important question: Can residency rules fairly measure belonging when a candidate has experienced homelessness?

What happened?

In 2023, while studying at Northeastern University in Boston, Korgood became unhoused. For nearly five months, she slept in her car, spent time in public libraries and used other safe places while continuing her studies and community work.

Two years later, at age 24, she ran for the Massachusetts Governor’s Council. This elected body reviews and confirms judicial nominees in the state.

Her campaign attracted endorsements, volunteers and thousands of online supporters.

An opponent then challenged whether she met Massachusetts’ five-year residency requirement. During the period when she had no permanent home, Korgood remained registered using her mother’s former New York address.

Korgood said she had continuously lived in Massachusetts since 2019. She offered records including apartment leases and participation in city programs.

On June 18, 2026, the Massachusetts State Ballot Law Commission ruled that she was not eligible to appear on the ballot.

Residency sounds simple until someone has no address

Election laws use residency requirements to make sure candidates have a genuine connection to the communities they want to represent.

That purpose is reasonable.

But a street address and a person’s actual home are not always the same thing. Students, military families, people escaping abuse and people experiencing homelessness may live in one place while official records point somewhere else.

When the government uses paperwork as proof of belonging, people with stable housing have an advantage. They can produce leases, bills and registration records that follow a normal pattern. Someone without a permanent address may have lived in the community every day while leaving a less tidy paper trail.

Who carried the burden?

Korgood had to prove not only where she lived but that homelessness had not broken her connection to Massachusetts.

The commission had authority to interpret and apply the residency rule. Voters never reached the stage of deciding whether her experience made her more or less qualified.

That is the missing democratic question. Ballot rules protect elections, but they also determine which choices voters are allowed to make.

Why should an ordinary person care?

Housing instability should not make somebody politically invisible.

If election rules recognize only people whose lives produce perfect paperwork, public office becomes easier to reach for people with stable housing and harder for the people who understand instability firsthand.

Pull Up a Chair

States should explain how candidates and voters without permanent housing can prove residency. The rules should identify acceptable evidence beyond a traditional lease or utility bill.

Voters can ask election officials whether homelessness, temporary housing and student status are addressed clearly before a candidacy is challenged.

This is not an argument that every residency challenge should fail. It is an argument that losing a home should not automatically mean losing political belonging.

Democracy should protect the ballot without turning stable housing into an unofficial qualification for public service.

Who is missing?

Ally = Action. 🪑
