The Workers Were Teenagers. The Complaint System Was Built for Adults.

By Jansen · October 27, 2026

The Short Version · 20-second read

  • What happened? Il Fornaio agreed to pay $3 million to settle an EEOC harassment and retaliation lawsuit. Many workers described by the agency were teenagers. The company denied the allegations.
  • Who carries the burden? Young workers may depend on the same manager for shifts, income and a first reference. The case raises a broader question about usable reporting routes.
  • What can you do? Employers should offer a route outside local management. Young workers can preserve messages and schedules, speak to a trusted adult and contact the EEOC promptly.

Based on EEOC records and the company response. Read the receipts

Read the full story

Seat affected: Teenage and young women working in restaurants. Location: California and Nevada, United States. Seat status: Present at work; usable protection is the accountability question.

A teenager working in a restaurant may depend on the same manager for her schedule, her paycheck, a reference and the decision about whether she works next weekend. Now imagine telling her that if someone at work touches her, pressures her for sex or punishes her for objecting, she should simply follow the company complaint process. That advice becomes much harder to follow when the process leads back to the same workplace hierarchy she is afraid of.

On October 13, 2026, the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission announced that Il Fornaio (America) LLC agreed to pay $3 million and provide other relief to settle a federal sexual-harassment and retaliation lawsuit. The agency described the chain as having 15 locations in California and Nevada on that date.

The EEOC alleged that supervisors and employees repeatedly subjected female workers, many of them teenagers, to sexual harassment across restaurant locations. The alleged conduct included forceful groping, grabbing intimate body parts, requests for sexual favors and explicit sexual comments. The agency said women complained, the company failed to stop the conduct, and many complainants faced retaliation. According to the EEOC, several women resigned.

Those are allegations made in a federal lawsuit. The settlement is real, but it is not a court judgment proving every allegation. Il Fornaio has denied wrongdoing and said it settled to avoid the burden, expense and uncertainty of continued litigation. That distinction matters. So does the fact that the company agreed to pay $3 million to end the case.

Who was missing?

The missing seat belongs first to the young workers themselves. Teenagers are often told to speak up, but speaking up at a first job can carry a price. A manager may control their shifts. A parent may need their income. The teenager may not know what conduct crosses a legal line, who outside the restaurant can help, or whether complaining will leave her without work.

The EEOC says at least 100 women were negatively affected by Il Fornaio's work environment during the case. That statement comes from the agency responsible for bringing the lawsuit. It does not establish that a court found 100 individual violations. It does show why this cannot be reduced to one bad interaction between two employees.

The missing seat also belongs to parents and trusted adults. A company that employs minors should not assume that an employee handbook alone creates a safe reporting system. Young workers need to know, in language they understand, who they can contact inside the company, what they can do if that person is involved, and how to reach help outside the company.

Who held the power?

The company controlled the workplace rules, managers, schedules and complaint channels. Supervisors controlled day-to-day working conditions. The workers carried the immediate risk of losing hours, income or a first reference.

That imbalance is why retaliation law matters. The EEOC explains that federal law protects workers who complain about unlawful harassment or participate in a discrimination process. It also warns that filing deadlines can be short. The general EEOC charge deadline is 180 days, extended to 300 where a state or local agency enforces a law covering the same discrimination. An internal grievance generally does not pause that clock. Ask the EEOC promptly about the rules for your circumstances rather than waiting for an internal process to finish.

A written policy is not the same as a usable door

The EEOC's guidance for young workers says harassment may come from a manager, coworker, customer or someone else connected to the workplace. It advises young workers to check the employer's policy, report the conduct and speak to another manager if the designated person feels unsafe. It also says they can speak with a parent, another adult or the EEOC.

That guidance is practical, but it exposes the weakness in many workplace systems. A policy can name one reporting person and still fail if that person is unavailable, connected to the accused or responsible for the worker's schedule. Training can exist on paper while a teenager still has no private phone number, no independent contact and no confidence that retaliation will be noticed.

For an adult with years of work experience, navigating several reporting levels may already be difficult. For a sixteen- or seventeen-year-old at a first job, it can be unrealistic.

The question is not only whether a company prohibited harassment. The question is whether the youngest worker could safely use the protection without first becoming a legal expert or risking her livelihood.

Who benefited, and who carried the burden?

An organization can benefit in the short term when unresolved complaints do not disrupt staffing or management. That is an institutional incentive to watch, not a finding about Il Fornaio's motive. In this case, the burden described by the EEOC fell on women who allegedly endured harassment, faced retaliation or resigned. A reporting system should make those costs visible to the people with power to act.

The settlement may compensate affected workers and require changes. It cannot return the time, safety or first-job confidence that workers say they lost.

That is why the receipt cannot stop at the dollar amount. The real test is what happens after the press release: whether workers know the new rules, whether complaints bypass local conflicts, whether retaliation is tracked, and whether a review independent of local management can see patterns across locations. This is the accountability test this editorial recommends, not a claim that those results have already been delivered.

The counterargument

It is fair to say that employers cannot prevent every act by every employee and should not be treated as automatically guilty whenever someone makes an allegation. Investigations must test evidence, and accused people and companies must be allowed to respond.

It is also fair to recognize that Il Fornaio denied the allegations and that settlement avoids the cost and uncertainty of trial.

But due process does not make prevention optional. A company that chooses to employ teenagers assumes a responsibility to build a reporting system they can realistically use. That means more than a policy hidden in onboarding paperwork.

Why should an ordinary person care?

Restaurants are often where young people learn what work is supposed to feel like. If their first lesson is that powerful people can cross boundaries while the worker must choose between silence and a paycheck, the harm follows them long after the shift ends.

Parents should care because a familiar restaurant is still a workplace. Customers should care because low prices and pleasant service should not depend on workers absorbing conduct the public never sees. Employers should care because an unusable complaint system allows one person's conduct to become an organizational failure.

What action looks like

If you employ teenagers, give every young worker at least two reporting routes, including one outside the local management chain. Put the contact information where workers can reach it without asking a supervisor. Explain retaliation with concrete examples such as lost shifts, undesirable assignments or sudden discipline. Tell parents or guardians, where lawful and appropriate, what safeguarding resources exist without compromising the worker's privacy.

If you are a young worker, document what happened, keep relevant messages and schedules, and speak with a trusted adult. You can also contact the EEOC through its public portal or call 1-800-669-4000. Deadlines apply, so do not assume you can wait indefinitely.

If you are a customer, ask restaurant chains a simple question: Can a teenage worker report harassment to someone outside the restaurant where she works? A serious employer should be able to answer.

The settlement created a consequence. The next step is building a door young workers can use before another case is necessary.
