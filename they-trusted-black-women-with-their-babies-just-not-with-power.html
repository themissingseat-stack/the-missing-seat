They Trusted Black Women With Their Babies. Just Not With Power.

America trusted Black women with some of its most intimate responsibilities, including caring for white children. But trust in their labor did not become equal protection, fair pay or equal power.

Seat Summary

  • Seat Affected: Black women whose care, labor and political work were relied upon while their protection, pay and authority were restricted
  • Seat Status: Essential labor accepted; equal power and protection delayed
  • Location: United States
  • Theme: Black Women, Labor, Care, Health and Power

America trusted Black women with its babies.

Not every family. Not every Black woman. But often enough to build an entire image around it.

Black women cooked the meals, washed the clothes and cared for white children. Some did that work while enslaved. Others did it later for wages because racism and sexism kept many better-paid jobs closed to them.

They were trusted with the most personal work in a family’s life.

But that trust had a limit.

It did not mean Black women were trusted to control their own labor, protect their own families or help govern the country their work helped hold together.

That contradiction deserves a closer look.

The care was real. The “mammy” story was not.

The Smithsonian’s National Museum of African American History and Culture explains that enslaved Black women did skilled domestic work in white households and cared for white children. After slavery, Black women continued to work as domestic servants and nursemaids.

Then America cleaned up that history.

It created the “mammy” stereotype: a Black woman who was supposedly happy, loyal and completely devoted to the white family she served. The image pushed her own family, needs and resistance out of sight.

That stereotype did something useful for the people who benefited from her labor. It made an unequal relationship look loving and natural. It turned service performed under slavery or limited economic choices into proof of contentment.

The care could be genuine. The conditions were not equal.

That distinction matters.

A Black woman could care deeply for a child and still be exploited by the system that placed her in that home. Affection does not erase ownership, low wages, exclusion or the fact that she might have had children of her own waiting for her.

Even Black girls carried the burden

The National Museum of American History preserves a photograph of a young Black nanny with a white boy. Its description explains that some Black girls became nursemaids and domestic workers when they were still children themselves. Girls as young as three carried babies, scrubbed diapers and stayed up late mending clothes.

Read that again.

Some Black children were made responsible for caring for other people’s children.

The white family received labor and care. The Black girl carried the burden.

This is not a sentimental story about how close everybody was. It is a story about who had choices and who did not.

The law accepted the work and withheld the protection

The pattern continued after slavery.

Black women worked, organized and supported families. The Smithsonian reports that, in the late nineteenth century, most African American women worked as washerwomen or domestic servants. In 1881, Atlanta washerwomen organized a major strike and demanded better pay and control over their work.

But when the federal government built major worker protections in the 1930s, domestic and agricultural workers were left out of key laws.

The U.S. Department of Labor says that almost 90 percent of Black women worked in domestic service or agriculture when the National Labor Relations Act was passed. Those were the very sectors excluded from the law’s protection. The Department also notes that the Social Security Act of 1935 initially excluded domestic workers, most of whom were Black women.

That is not a side note.

The country relied on Black women’s labor, then designed major protections in a way that left much of that labor outside.

Employers and households benefited from the work. Black women carried the insecurity.

Care was welcome. Authority was treated as dangerous.

The same country that relied on Black women inside private homes fought their power in public life.

Black women organized for voting rights long before the Nineteenth Amendment. Even after it was ratified in 1920, voter suppression continued to block many Black women in the South.

The House Historian records how opponents of women’s suffrage openly worried that Black women would become a political force. One senator called Black women the “real leaders” of Black political activity and warned that voting rights would make Black people a political factor again.

That language tells us something.

Black women were not denied power because nobody believed they were capable. Some opponents feared them precisely because they knew Black women could organize and lead.

America was comfortable receiving their service. It was far less comfortable receiving their demands.

Representation came late and remains incomplete

Shirley Chisholm became the first Black woman elected to Congress in 1968 and began serving in 1969.

Think about that timeline.

Black women had helped raise children, sustain households, organize workers and fight for voting rights for generations before one Black woman finally received a voting seat in Congress.

The House Historian now lists 65 Black women who have ever served in Congress. Rutgers’ Center for American Women and Politics reports that Black women currently hold 5.4 percent of voting seats in Congress.

That is progress. It is not proof that the old account has been settled.

Representation is not only about seeing someone who looks like you. People in power decide whose work is protected, whose family receives support, whose history is taught and whose warning is taken seriously.

When the people who carried the burden are missing from the room, the country can keep treating their labor as essential while treating their needs as optional.

Who benefited, and who carried the burden?

White households received childcare, cleaning and domestic labor.

Employers received workers whose legal protection and bargaining power were restricted.

Political institutions benefited from Black women’s organizing, votes and community leadership.

Black women carried the low pay, the exclusions, the stereotypes and the long wait for authority.

That does not mean every white child raised by a Black caregiver was personally responsible for the system. It does mean the system made the relationship unequal, then often remembered the affection while forgetting the power.

Why should an ordinary person care?

Because this recipe did not stay in the past.

We still praise care workers as essential while paying many of them poorly. We still expect Black women to organize, rescue institutions and deliver votes, then question whether they are electable, qualified or too angry when they demand power themselves.

The job title may change. The contradiction survives.

If someone is trusted with your child, your family, your community or your vote, why does that trust disappear when she asks for authority, protection or fair pay?

Pull Up a Chair

Do not share the “mammy” image as harmless nostalgia. Learn what it was built to hide.

Check whether domestic and care workers in your state receive minimum-wage, overtime, leave and organizing protections. Ask candidates what they will do for the people who make other families’ work possible.

And look at the decision-making tables around you. Who is doing the care work? Who is doing the organizing? Who is asked to save the institution? Then ask who actually has a vote when the decisions are made.

Black women were trusted with the children.

They should never have had to wait this long for the seat.

Who is missing?

Ally = Action. 🪑
