CURRENT NEWS UPDATE • INVESTIGATIVE TRUST • JULY 20, 2026

Three Deaths. Three Families. And the Questions That Refuse to Disappear.

One young man was recovered from the waters surrounding a Mississippi barrier island. One young woman was found hanging near a public park in Miami. One teenage girl was found hanging from a tree on church property in North Carolina.

These are three separate cases. They occurred in different places and under different circumstances. No verified evidence currently establishes that any of the three was murdered.

But they now share something else: Black families and communities are once again being asked to trust conclusions they did not watch authorities reach.

When trust is missing, even an official answer may not feel like an answer.

Nolan Wells: An Investigation Still Unfinished

Eighteen-year-old Nolan Xavier Wells disappeared during a July Fourth boating trip to Horn Island, Mississippi. His body was recovered from the water on July 6.

As of the evening of July 19, no final cause or manner of death had been released publicly. The official autopsy, toxicology findings, and results of the family’s independent autopsy remained unavailable.

Nolan’s family does not accept the idea that he simply chose to remain behind on the island without his cellphone, keys, or a way home. They have questioned conflicting accounts surrounding his final known movements and expressed concern that material may have been deleted from his phone.

Those concerns are not proof of a crime. But they are legitimate reasons to preserve evidence, reconcile witness accounts, and explain the timeline completely.

The family and the Jackson County district attorney have agreed to a joint forensic inspection of Nolan’s cellphone. District Attorney Angel Myers McIlrath has also agreed to present the completed investigation to a grand jury.

The FBI is assisting local authorities and investigating interstate threats connected with the case. That involvement does not mean the FBI has determined that Nolan was murdered or targeted because of his race.

GPS information reportedly documents the boat’s movements. It does not, by itself, establish Nolan’s exact location at every moment, explain how he entered the water, or determine whether his death was accidental.

We still do not publicly know exactly how Nolan Wells died.

That is not an accusation. It is the status of the evidence available to the public.

To’Nea Nicole Miller: One Conclusion, Different Responses

To’Nea Nicole Miller, a 27-year-old Black woman from Michigan, was found hanging near Gwen Cherry Park in Miami on June 18.

The Miami-Dade Sheriff’s Office said investigators and the medical examiner found no evidence of foul play and concluded that the evidence was consistent with suicide.

One of Miller’s aunts later told NBC 6 that, after speaking with the investigator and medical examiner, members of the family also believed that To’Nea took her own life. Her sister publicly rejected that conclusion.

Those two positions can exist within one grieving family. Grief does not always produce agreement, especially when relatives receive different information or struggle to reconcile an official finding with the person they knew.

Authorities have said the case is closed but that credible new evidence would be reviewed.

What remains troubling is how long To’Nea’s death circulated principally through social media before substantial local reporting and an official public explanation followed.

Even when an investigation reaches the correct conclusion, silence creates a vacuum. Into that vacuum come history, fear, speculation, and distrust.

Transparency is not only about proving wrongdoing. Sometimes it is what prevents unsupported claims from becoming the only explanation people hear.

Juliana Nzita: Ten Days Missing, Then Found on Church Property

Juliana Nzita was a 16-year-old Black girl from the Democratic Republic of the Congo living in Charlotte, North Carolina.

She was reported missing on April 28. Ten days later, on May 8, she was found hanging from a tree on property associated with the United House of Prayer for All People.

Police classified her death as suicide. Public reporting has not established that her death was a homicide or that authorities subsequently reopened it as one.

But the circumstances produced questions that have not disappeared:

  • Where was Juliana during the ten days she was missing?
  • What efforts were made to locate her?
  • Was every part of the scene processed with the possibility of third-party involvement in mind?
  • Were cameras, witnesses, and her movements adequately examined?
  • What information was given to her family?
  • Why has so little official explanation been made publicly available?

Questions are not evidence of murder. But an official classification should not be treated as a substitute for explaining how investigators reached it—particularly when the person who died was a missing child.

Were These Lynchings?

Based on the verified information currently available, The Missing Seat cannot responsibly call these deaths lynchings.

No public evidence presently establishes that another person killed To’Nea Miller or Juliana Nzita. Nolan Wells was not found hanging, and his death remains under active investigation.

Words matter. “Lynching” describes a killing, historically used as racial terror and social control. It should not be assigned to a death solely because a Black person was found hanging or died under circumstances a community considers suspicious.

But history also matters.

For Black Americans, a body hanging from a tree is not a historically neutral image. Generations of racial terror, incomplete investigations, and official indifference do not disappear because authorities announce that no crime occurred.

That history does not prove homicide in these cases. It explains why transparency is essential.

Trust cannot be demanded from communities whose distrust was built from documented experience. It must be earned through evidence preservation, independent review, respectful communication, and conclusions that can withstand public scrutiny.

What Responsible Accountability Looks Like

Accountability does not require declaring every unexplained Black death a murder. It requires refusing to treat a quick classification as the end of every legitimate question.

Authorities should:

  • Preserve phones, messages, location data, video, and physical evidence before information can disappear.
  • Clearly distinguish preliminary impressions from final medical and investigative findings.
  • Communicate directly and consistently with families.
  • Explain what evidence supports the stated cause and manner of death.
  • Permit independent review when confidence has broken down.
  • Correct false online claims without withholding verified facts.
  • Treat public concern as a reason for clarity—not as an inconvenience.

The public has responsibilities too.

We should demand answers without inventing them. We should not identify suspects without evidence, threaten witnesses, or convert rumors into facts. Doing so can damage innocent people, contaminate investigations, and make the truth harder to establish.

🪑 Return to the Seat

The missing seat in these cases is not automatically the Seat of Justice—because the evidence has not established that justice was denied through criminal acts.

It is the Seat of Investigative Trust.

Who gets immediate public concern?

Whose family receives timely information?

Whose unanswered questions are treated as reasonable?

And how much uncertainty is a Black family expected to accept before asking for another set of eyes?

The Missing Seat will return to these cases when autopsy findings, toxicology results, forensic evidence, or official investigative developments become available.

Responsible journalism must be willing to say two things simultaneously:

We do not yet have proof of murder.

These families still deserve complete, credible, and transparent answers.

Who is missing?

The family waiting for a complete explanation.

The community whose historical distrust is dismissed instead of understood.

The person whose death becomes a conclusion before the public can understand the investigation behind it.

What is the missing seat?

The seat where investigative authority and public accountability meet—where families receive facts, conclusions are explained, and legitimate questions are not mistaken for accusations.

What can I do?

Share verified reporting instead of rumors. Correct unsupported claims, even when they support the conclusion you expected. Ask authorities for evidence-based transparency. Support families seeking lawful independent review. Return to these stories when new facts emerge instead of allowing their names to disappear with the news cycle.
