Housing, Race &amp; Equal Treatment

Two Tenants Were Incarcerated. Only One Allegedly Lost the Keys.

Seat Summary

  • Seat Affected: Black and Hispanic renters, formerly incarcerated people and families in subsidized housing
  • Seat Status: Subject to an allegedly unequal rule and retaliation after complaining
  • Location: Wrightstown, New Jersey
  • Theme: Race, Housing, Incarceration and Civil Rights
  • Proposed filename: `two-tenants-were-incarcerated-only-one-allegedly-lost-the-keys.html`

Roberto Landrau-Vega was briefly incarcerated. According to the U.S. Justice Department, managers at the federally supported Millstream and Maplewood Apartments changed the locks on his home and tried to evict him.

Landrau-Vega is Black and Hispanic. The federal complaint alleged that a White tenant who was also incarcerated received more favorable treatment.

That comparison is the heart of the case.

The question was not whether incarceration can create legitimate housing problems. A landlord may need to determine whether rent will be paid, whether a unit has been abandoned and who is allowed to enter it.

The question was whether the same basic situation produced a different response depending on the tenant’s race or national origin.

The government also alleged retaliation

Landrau-Vega filed a complaint under the Fair Housing Act. The Justice Department then alleged that the housing providers interfered with the process needed to keep his housing subsidy and tried to evict him again.

A housing subsidy helps make rent affordable. Losing it can mean losing the home.

Retaliation means punishing someone because they reported possible discrimination or exercised a protected right. Laws against retaliation matter because a right to complain is nearly useless if using it puts your housing at greater risk.

The defendants denied the allegations, and the case ended in settlements rather than a trial. One agreement placed $62,500 toward resolving the federal fair-housing claims. Another $22,500 addressed possible claims the housing company might have asserted against Landrau-Vega, including disputed rent. Neither side admitted liability.

The housing provider also agreed to stop using a rule specifically focused on a household member’s incarceration, except where otherwise allowed by law and consistent with the Fair Housing Act.

Discrimination often hides inside discretion

Modern discrimination rarely arrives in writing with the words, “Treat this tenant worse because he is Black and Hispanic.”

It can appear through discretion. One tenant receives a telephone call, extra time and a chance to explain. Another tenant gets a changed lock.

That is why comparisons matter. If two people were in similar situations, received different treatment and race or national origin appears to line up with the difference, the housing provider should be able to show a lawful and documented reason.

The settlement does not prove every allegation. But equal-treatment systems should not wait for a federal lawsuit before checking whether similar tenants receive similar procedures.

Why should an ordinary person care?

Incarceration can already threaten a person’s job, income, childcare and family relationships. When housing disappears too, the punishment spreads to partners and children who were never incarcerated.

A person released without a home is also less able to find work, follow court requirements and rebuild a stable life.

Pull Up a Chair

Housing providers should publish what happens when a tenant is arrested or incarcerated, including notice, deadlines, subsidy procedures and the right to respond. They should also compare outcomes across race to see whether discretion is being applied unevenly.

Tenants should save notices, recertification documents and written communications. If a landlord acts soon after a discrimination complaint, record the timing and contact a local fair-housing organization.

If two tenants did the same thing, show us the same process. If the result was different, show us why.

Who is missing?

Verification Notice

The unequal treatment and retaliation described here were allegations made by the United States. The defendants denied them, and the settlement is not an admission or trial verdict. The agreement allocated $62,500 to resolve the Fair Housing Act claims and $22,500 to resolve possible claims Burlington might assert against Landrau-Vega. It also ended Burlington's incarceration-specific house rule, subject to law and the Fair Housing Act.
