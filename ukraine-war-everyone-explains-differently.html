Ukraine: The War Everyone Explains Differently

Russia calls it a defensive struggle. Ukraine calls it a war for national survival. Western governments call it resistance to aggression. Those narratives are not equally supported—but understanding the conflict requires knowing why each one is told.

Seat Affected: A people’s right to sovereignty, civilian safety and a peace they have a voice in defining
Seat Status: Violated by invasion and endangered by prolonged war
Location: Ukraine, Russia and Europe
Theme: Sovereignty, propaganda, security, negotiation and civilian harm

Ask five people why the Ukraine war happened and you may hear five different wars.

One began with NATO expansion. Another began with Russia’s seizure of Crimea in 2014. Another begins with conflict in the Donbas. Another starts with Vladimir Putin’s denial that Ukraine is fully separate from Russia. The clearest legal account begins on February 24, 2022, when Russia launched its full-scale invasion of a sovereign neighboring state.

Context matters. But chronology can be manipulated by choosing the starting point that makes one’s preferred actor look innocent.

The Missing Seat will not pretend every explanation has equal evidentiary weight. Russia chose to invade. The United Nations General Assembly overwhelmingly demanded that Russia withdraw. No grievance over NATO, Western hypocrisy, Ukrainian politics or the failed Minsk agreements gave Russia a legal right to conquer Ukrainian territory.

That is the foundation. The rest is the harder conversation.

What Russia Says

Russia presents the war as a response to Western encirclement, NATO expansion, threats to Russian speakers, Ukrainian nationalism and Kyiv’s failure to implement the Minsk agreements. Moscow has used “demilitarization” and “denazification” as official justifications.

Some underlying facts are real. NATO expanded eastward after the Cold War. The United States and European powers sought influence in Ukraine. The Donbas conflict killed people on both sides after 2014. Minsk was not fully implemented. Western states have sometimes applied international law inconsistently elsewhere.

But a grievance is not a license.

Ukraine is not legally required to surrender its foreign-policy choices because Russia feels insecure. Claims of protecting Russian speakers do not establish a right to annex their territory. Pointing to Western violations in Iraq or elsewhere exposes hypocrisy; it does not legalize another invasion.

Russia also describes occupied regions as historically Russian or voluntarily reunited. Yet military occupation, coerced political processes and annexation claims made during war do not create freely expressed consent.

What Ukraine Says

Ukraine describes the conflict as a war of national survival that began with Russia’s 2014 seizure of Crimea and support for armed separatism in eastern Ukraine, then escalated into the 2022 invasion.

That account is strongly grounded in the record. Ukraine was internationally recognized within its post-Soviet borders. Russia had previously committed to respect those borders. Russian troops crossed them. Russian forces occupy Ukrainian territory.

Ukraine therefore argues that a ceasefire without credible security guarantees could reward aggression, freeze occupation and give Russia time to rearm.

But defending sovereignty does not remove Ukraine from scrutiny. Ukrainian attacks must comply with international humanitarian law. Mobilization, corruption, military leadership, democratic restrictions under martial law and the treatment of dissent all require examination. Civilian deaths in Russian territory and Russian-occupied parts of Ukraine matter too, even where immediate responsibility is disputed.

Supporting Ukraine’s right to exist does not require pretending every Ukrainian decision is wise or lawful.

What the West Says—and Gains

Western governments describe military support for Ukraine as defense of the UN Charter, European security and the principle that borders cannot be changed by force.

That principle matters. If conquest succeeds because resisting it becomes expensive, smaller countries receive a brutal message about the value of legal guarantees.

Western countries also have interests. Aid weakens a strategic rival without sending NATO armies into direct combat. Weapons producers receive contracts. European governments strengthen alliances and reshape energy policy. Politicians use Ukraine to demonstrate toughness—or use fatigue with the war to campaign against spending.

Interests do not make Ukraine’s defense illegitimate. They do mean generosity and strategy can exist at the same time.

The honest question is not whether the West has motives. Every state does. It is whether the assistance helps Ukrainians defend a lawful right, whether it prolongs fighting without a realistic strategy, and whether Ukrainians—not outside patrons—retain meaningful agency over the terms of peace.

The Human Record

While governments argue about history, the civilian toll is climbing.

The UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission verified at least 1,396 civilians killed and 7,978 injured in Ukraine during the first six months of 2026. June produced the highest monthly civilian casualty total since April 2022: at least 293 killed and 1,990 injured. The UN says the real toll is higher because many cases cannot be verified.

The overwhelming majority of verified casualties have occurred in Ukrainian government-controlled territory. Civilians in occupied Ukraine and inside Russia have also been killed or injured. Claims from areas the UN cannot independently access should be reported as claims, not erased and not treated as verified.

Millions remain displaced. Families live between temporary protection abroad, damaged homes, uncertain return and the fear that a political settlement may decide where they belong without them.

The International Criminal Court has issued arrest warrants alleging war crimes, including the unlawful deportation and transfer of Ukrainian children. A warrant is not a conviction. It is also not propaganda merely because the accused reject the court.

Peace Is Not One Thing

Calls to “end the war” sound simple until the terms are named.

Russia has demanded, among other conditions, that Ukraine abandon NATO ambitions and withdraw from four regions Moscow claims—even territory Russia does not fully control. Ukraine rejects being forced to surrender land and people under occupation. It seeks binding security guarantees because earlier assurances failed to prevent invasion.

A ceasefire could save lives immediately. It could also freeze an unstable front without answering sovereignty, prisoner exchanges, abducted children, sanctions, reconstruction, accountability or protection against renewed attack.

Continuing war also has no morally clean path. More people die while leaders pursue a stronger negotiating position that may never arrive.

The responsible question is not “peace or war?” Everyone claims to want peace. It is:

What terms can stop the killing without teaching powerful states that invasion is an effective route to permanent territorial reward?

No False Equivalence

Both sides use propaganda. Both sides’ military conduct must be scrutinized. Civilians matter regardless of nationality.

Those statements do not make responsibility symmetrical.

Russia launched the full-scale invasion. Ukraine is defending internationally recognized territory. A Ukrainian violation, if proven, remains a violation—but it does not retroactively make the invasion lawful. Western hypocrisy elsewhere does not transfer sovereignty over Ukraine to Moscow.

Nuance means holding multiple facts at once. It does not mean flattening decisive differences until aggressor and invaded become interchangeable.

Who Is Missing?

Ukrainians living under occupation whose preferences cannot be measured freely.

Refugees and displaced families whose futures are discussed as demographic or financial problems.

Russian civilians, conscripts and families who cannot safely challenge the Kremlin’s account.

Children transferred across borders, prisoners of war, people with disabilities, ethnic minorities and those unable to evacuate.

And ordinary citizens in all countries asked to accept slogans where evidence and strategy should be.

One Action

Before sharing a claim, locate its starting date. Does the explanation begin in 1991, 2004, 2014 or 2022—and what disappears because of that choice?

Then ask: Is this context, evidence of legal justification, or an excuse being presented as one? Verify casualty claims through independent monitors where possible. Demand that every peace proposal state its provisions on territory, security guarantees, displaced people, prisoners, children and enforcement.

Return to the Seat

The Missing Seat will return when negotiations produce concrete terms rather than declarations of readiness. We will track whether Ukrainians have a meaningful voice, whether security guarantees are enforceable, whether civilians and transferred children can return safely, and whether any ceasefire reduces harm rather than merely postponing it.

The war can be explained from many angles.

But explanation must never become permission to erase the people whose country became the battlefield.
