The Missing Seat Editorial

You Don’t Look Autistic

“You don’t look autistic” is often meant as reassurance or even a compliment.

But it rests on a false idea: that autism has one recognizable appearance.

It does not.

Autism is a lifelong difference in how a person’s brain processes communication, social interaction, sensory information, routines, movement and attention. It can affect people in very different ways.

Some autistic people speak extensively. Others use few or no spoken words. Some need daily assistance. Others live independently while privately dealing with sensory overload, anxiety, exhaustion or confusing social expectations.

Looking comfortable is not the same as feeling comfortable

An autistic person may make eye contact because they trained themselves to do it. They may rehearse conversations, copy facial expressions or force themselves not to move in ways that help them stay calm.

This is often called masking. Masking means hiding or controlling natural autistic traits to appear more typical and avoid punishment, ridicule or exclusion.

It may help someone get through school, work or a social event. It can also be exhausting.

The person smiling through a noisy meeting may spend the rest of the day recovering. The student who appears quiet may be using all their energy to tolerate bright lights and constant sound.

Behavior is communication

Autistic people are often judged according to how well they meet non-autistic expectations.

Direct communication may be called rude. A need for routine may be called stubborn. Sensory overload may be described as drama or bad behavior. A child who cannot tolerate a crowded room may be punished instead of helped.

Before assuming a person is difficult, ask what their behavior may be communicating. Are they overwhelmed? Do they need clearer instructions, less noise, more preparation or another way to respond?

Support does not mean removing every expectation. It means creating conditions in which the person can understand the expectation and participate without unnecessary harm.

Do not make people prove they are “autistic enough”

Some people are denied understanding because they speak well, have a job, maintain relationships or do not match a stereotype.

Ability in one area does not cancel difficulty in another. A person can be highly skilled and still need support.

Autistic people also deserve a voice in decisions about autism. Parents, teachers and doctors may provide important knowledge, but they should not be the only people heard.

Why should an ordinary person care?

Autistic people are your classmates, coworkers, customers, relatives and neighbors. Clear communication, predictable processes and quieter spaces often help many other people too.

Inclusion is not about asking everyone to behave identically. It is about making participation possible.

Pull Up a Chair

Do not tell someone they do not look autistic. Ask what helps them communicate, work or feel safe.

Give clear instructions. Warn people about sudden changes when possible. Allow reasonable breaks, headphones, written communication or another useful adjustment.

Most importantly, believe people when they explain what costs them energy, even if that effort is invisible to you.

Who is missing?

Ally = Action. 🪑
