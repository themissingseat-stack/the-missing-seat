RACE & EQUAL HUMANITY · MISSING HISTORY

You Don't Want Us Here. You Still Use What We Made.

By Jansen Bowden / The Missing Seat · November 26, 2026

The Short Version

What happened: Racism against Black Americans keeps showing up in public life. Meanwhile, the country runs every day on things Black Americans invented, improved, built or helped create, from a safer traffic signal and an early home security system to large-scale blood banking, the interchangeable game cartridge and music heard around the world.

Who it affects: Black Americans, who are told they don't belong in a country that relies on their work, and everyone who never learned where these things came from.

What you can do: Learn the names below and share them. Teach them to a child. And the next time someone says Black people contributed nothing, show them the receipts.

Website editorial

Some people make it clear they do not want Black Americans here. They don't want to hear about us, live near us or share power with us.

They still use what we made every day.

They stop at the light. They lock the door and check the camera. They give blood and receive it. They play video games, ship cold food across the country, and turn up the radio. Much of that runs through the work of Black Americans. Some built successful careers. Others, as the cases below show, were shut out of credit, pay or even the right to a patent.

Here are the receipts.

On the road and at home

The three-position traffic signal. Garrett Morgan, the son of a formerly enslaved parent, patented an improved traffic signal in 1923 after seeing a crash in Cleveland. Many signals then had only stop and go positions. Morgan's design added an all-stop interval to clear the intersection. [1] He also patented a breathing device, a safety hood that protected rescuers from smoke and gas. [1] He did not invent the first traffic signal. His contribution was a safer one.

The home security system. In 1966, Marie Van Brittan Brown, a nurse in Queens, New York, designed one of the first camera-based home security systems, with a camera, a monitor and a remote door lock, because police took too long to come to her neighborhood. She and her husband, Albert Brown, received the patent in 1969. Later home security systems built on ideas like hers. [2]

Refrigerated trucks. Frederick McKinley Jones received more than 60 patents, including one for the roof-mounted cooling unit that keeps trucks cold on long hauls. [3] Think of that the next time you buy fresh food or a vaccine that traveled across the country.

The light bulb that lasted. Thomas Edison gets the credit for the light bulb, but many inventors worked on it. Lewis Latimer applied in 1881 for a patent on a method of making carbon filaments, granted in 1882. It helped make bulbs longer-lasting and cheaper to produce. [4]

In medicine

Large-scale blood banking. Dr. Charles Drew showed that plasma could be safely separated from whole blood, stored and used for transfusion. [5] In World War II, he led the Blood for Britain program, which collected more than 14,000 donations and shipped about 5,000 liters of plasma overseas. [6] He then became the first director of the American Red Cross Blood Bank. [7] He did not invent every part of blood banking, but his research and leadership shaped how it works on a large scale.

Cataract surgery. Dr. Patricia Bath invented the Laserphaco Probe for treating cataracts and became the first African American woman doctor to receive a medical patent. [3]

The cells that built modern medicine. This one is not an invention. It is something that was taken. In 1951, doctors at Johns Hopkins took cells from Henrietta Lacks, a Black woman being treated for cervical cancer, without her knowledge. Her cells became the first continuously growing human cell line, known as HeLa, and helped develop the polio vaccine, map human genes and develop COVID-19 vaccines. [8] For decades, her family received nothing. In 2023, they reached an undisclosed settlement with Thermo Fisher Scientific, one of the companies that profited. [8] Nobody asked her. They just took.

In technology

The game cartridge. Before 1976, home video games were built into the console. Jerry Lawson led the team at Fairchild that created the first home system with interchangeable cartridges, the Channel F. [9] It helped set the model for buying games one at a time.

The personal computer. At IBM, Mark Dean and his colleague Dennis Moeller developed the ISA bus, the system that let printers, keyboards and other devices connect to the IBM personal computer. Dean holds three of the original nine patents on the IBM PC and is in the National Inventors Hall of Fame. [10]

The Super Soaker. Lonnie Johnson, an engineer who worked on NASA missions to Mars and Saturn, invented the Super Soaker. [11]

In the music you play every day

The blues, jazz, gospel, rhythm and blues and rock and roll grew out of Black American life. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture tells that story from the music enslaved Africans brought with them to today's hip-hop. [12] Hip-hop started at parties in the Bronx in the early 1970s, built by Black American, Afro-Caribbean and Latino young people together. [12] That music now sells and plays around the world.

And the patents they never got

Some of this work was never credited because the law would not allow it. In 1857, a Mississippi enslaved man named Ned invented a new cotton scraper. His enslaver tried to patent it. In 1858, the U.S. Attorney General ruled that neither could hold the patent. Ned was enslaved, so he could not take the inventor's oath, and his enslaver had not invented it. [13] The opinion relied on Dred Scott, the Supreme Court ruling that Black Americans were not citizens. [14] It took the Reconstruction Amendments to end that rule. [15]

We will never know how many inventions were taken that way, with no name attached.

One more correction, because the truth is strong enough without myths: George Washington Carver did not invent peanut butter. He developed hundreds of uses for peanuts and other crops, and held three patents. [16] Every claim in this list is backed by a source below.

Pay us back

So here is the deal. If you don't want us here, if you don't like what we stand for, give it all back. Give back the signal at the corner, the camera on the porch, the blood in the bank, the cold truck, the game cartridge, the plug on the back of your computer and the music on your radio. Then live with only what you have.

Nobody is actually going to do that, and nobody should. It is a moral challenge, not a demand. These things belong to the whole country now, often made by teams of many people, and Black Americans built a share of it.

Black Americans do not have to invent something to deserve equal rights. These contributions expose the dishonesty of treating us as outsiders in a country we helped build.

Pull Up A Chair

  • Learn five names from this list and teach them to a child this week.
  • Ask your school district whether Black inventors and scientists are part of the curriculum, by name.
  • When someone shares a myth, correct it. The real record is stronger.
  • Visit or support the National Museum of African American History and Culture online, and the National Museum of African American Music in Nashville.

Seat Summary

The country uses Black American work every day: on the road, in the hospital, at home, on screens and on the radio. Some of it was uncredited, some was taken without consent, and under the law before the Civil War, an enslaved inventor could not hold a patent. The missing seat is simple: credit, and belonging, for the people who built it.
