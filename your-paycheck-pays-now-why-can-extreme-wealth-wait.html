TAX FAIRNESS • WORK • WEALTH • SMALL BUSINESS • ECONOMIC POWER

Your Paycheck Pays Now. Why Can Extreme Wealth Wait?

Workers pay taxes as wages arrive. Extreme wealth can often grow for years before capital-gains tax is triggered. The question is not whether success should be punished. It is whether work and wealth are being asked to carry a fair share.

Seat Affected: People trying to build security mainly through work
Seat Status: Paying into the system immediately while some forms of accumulated wealth can defer taxation
Location: United States
Theme: Tax Fairness • Work • Wealth • Small Business • Accountability

Regular Joe earns $50,000 working. His wages enter that year's income-tax calculation, subject to deductions and credits, and covered wages are also generally subject to Social Security and Medicare taxes. He does not get to tell the government that his paycheck became more valuable this year but he would prefer to deal with the tax later.

Now imagine Alex already owns $100 million in shares. If those shares rise to $110 million and Alex does not sell them, the $10 million increase generally does not trigger federal capital-gains tax at that moment. If Alex eventually sells, the gain can become taxable. Long-term gains may also receive lower federal income-tax rates than ordinary income. Alex may still pay enormous amounts of tax through other income, property, business activity, dividends or eventual gains. The point is not that wealthy people pay nothing. The point is that the timing and treatment of wealth growth can be fundamentally different from the treatment of wages.

Think of it as a checkout line. Joe's earnings reach the register as they arrive. Alex can often leave investment growth inside the store until a later transaction brings it to the tax checkout.

That difference matters because time is valuable. Money that remains invested can continue growing. A person with substantial assets may also borrow against them for spending money. Genuine loan proceeds generally are not income because they have to be repaid. Debt has interest, risk and collateral requirements, so this is not free money. But it can give a wealthy household access to cash without requiring an immediate sale of appreciated assets.

Then there is inheritance. Under the usual federal basis rule, inherited property generally receives a basis linked to its fair market value at death. That means appreciation that occurred during the previous owner's lifetime may not appear in the heir's later capital-gains calculation. Estate tax is separate, and in 2026 the federal basic exclusion amount is $15 million per person, with additional rules involving prior gifts, spouses and portability.

If you work for your money, this can sound like two different economic languages being spoken inside one tax code.

The Question Is Not "Do Rich People Pay Taxes?"

Of course they do. Some wealthy households pay very large tax bills. A serious tax argument cannot be built on pretending otherwise.

The better question is: what counts as taxable income, when does it become taxable, at what rate, and who has the financial capacity to structure economic life around those rules?

For the person living on wages, most of the economic gain arrives as taxable compensation. For the person whose wealth is largely in businesses, stocks or other appreciating assets, a much larger share of economic growth may occur before there is a taxable sale.

That is why simply comparing one person's tax bill with another person's tax bill can miss the structural issue. The argument is not only about rates. It is about timing, realization, inheritance, deductions, credits, trusts, compensation structures and the ability to wait.

A $1,000 deduction, for example, does not hand somebody $1,000. At a hypothetical 30% marginal income-tax rate, it reduces income tax by roughly $300. The real policy question is which deductions, exclusions, deferrals and preferential rates exist, who can use them, and what public purpose they serve.

Small Business Is Part of This Story Too

Tax fairness should not become another argument in which every business owner is treated as a billionaire.

The U.S. Small Business Administration's Office of Advocacy reports about 36.2 million businesses classified as small in 2026. Those businesses collectively employ 62.3 million people, or 45.9% of private-sector workers, and account for 43.5% of GDP. But even that needs context: the federal research definition of small business is broad, and a large share of those businesses have no employees.

A neighborhood restaurant, independent contractor or family shop does not have the financial structure of a private-equity fund or a multinational corporation. Small operators deal with rent, payroll, supplies, insurance, financing, taxes and compliance while often having little room for error. The same SBA report estimates federal paperwork costs to small businesses at more than $81 billion in 2025, with more than 80% of that burden associated with IRS paperwork.

So a fairer tax system should not simply move pressure from workers onto genuine small businesses. It should distinguish between labor income, operating businesses that need cash to keep employing people, and extreme concentrations of investment wealth that have far more capacity to defer, structure and absorb taxation.

We Already Have Working Pieces

America does not have to invent every possible solution from scratch.

Refundable tax credits already provide a mechanism for getting money to eligible workers and families, including people who owe little federal income tax. The Earned Income Tax Credit and refundable family credits are established systems. Their eligibility rules can be improved, their reach can be debated, and temporary expansions can expire, but the delivery mechanism is not theoretical.

Capital-gains treatment at death can also be changed. Canada, for example, generally treats death as a deemed disposition of capital property at market value, with exceptions including certain transfers to a spouse. That does not mean the United States should copy Canada line for line. It shows that accumulated gains do not have to disappear from the tax base simply because an owner dies.

Other countries have experimented with annual wealth taxes, though the international record is mixed. An OECD review found 12 countries with recurrent individual net-wealth taxes in 1990 and only four in 2017. Countries repealed such taxes for several reasons, including avoidance, narrow tax bases, administrative cost, capital-flight concerns and limited revenue. That history is a warning against simplistic slogans in either direction. Wealth taxes are administratively possible. They are also difficult to design well.

The lesson is not "Europe solved it." The lesson is that tax systems make choices, those choices can be compared, and failed design should be studied rather than ignored.

Congress Already Has Proposals on the Table

As of October 2026, several high-wealth tax proposals exist in Congress or in recent Treasury policy work. They are not current law.

Senator Ron Wyden's Billionaires Income Tax Act, S. 2845, was introduced in September 2025 and referred to the Senate Finance Committee. Its design distinguishes between readily tradable assets and harder-to-value assets rather than pretending every form of wealth can be taxed in exactly the same way every year.

Senator Elizabeth Warren's Ultra-Millionaire Tax Act of 2026, S. 4246, was introduced in March 2026 and referred to the Senate Finance Committee. It proposes an annual tax on very large net fortunes. That raises different questions about valuation, liquidity and constitutional design from an income-tax approach.

Treasury has also previously proposed a 25% minimum tax on broadly measured income, including appreciation, for households with wealth above $100 million. That proposal was not enacted. It remains useful as a concrete design reference because it tries to address the gap between taxable realized income and the economic growth of enormous fortunes.

Congress is also still debating carried interest, the treatment that can allow some investment-fund compensation to receive investment-style tax treatment. Senators Ron Wyden, Sheldon Whitehouse and Angus King introduced another carried-interest reform bill in April 2026. This is not a new argument. Congress has been debating carried-interest treatment for many years.

The fact that these ideas have not become law does not prove that they failed in operation. In many cases, they were never enacted broadly enough to test that claim. There is an important difference between a policy that was implemented and failed and a policy that politicians never passed.

So What Would a Serious Package Look Like?

A serious package should begin with the parts that already have the strongest administrative footing rather than betting everything on one dramatic new tax.

First, strengthen refundable worker and family credits through systems the IRS already knows how to administer. Pay particular attention to workers without children, people with seasonal or self-employed income, benefit interactions and the risk of unexpected repayment when income changes.

Second, tax very high realized investment income more consistently with labor income. Any threshold should be high enough that ordinary retirement saving and normal household investing are not treated as extreme wealth. Lawmakers should model the effect on investment, sales timing, losses and corporate taxation rather than assuming behavior will remain unchanged.

Third, stop very large accumulated gains from simply disappearing from capital-gains taxation at death. That could mean taxing gains at transfer above a substantial exclusion or, as a narrower fallback, using carryover basis so the heir eventually pays tax on the original gain when the asset is sold. Genuine illiquid operating businesses need carefully designed payment arrangements so a tax bill does not force a viable employer to liquidate simply to meet a deadline.

Fourth, close targeted preferences and avoidance routes before pretending a broad new tax solves everything. Carried interest, trust planning and high-income enforcement deserve attention because they operate inside conventional tax structures and can be addressed without relying entirely on a constitutionally uncertain annual tax on unrealized gains or wealth.

Fifth, separately test an extreme-wealth minimum-tax model. Compare a gains-based approach with a wealth-based approach. Publish the assumptions. Show how losses work. Show what happens in low-return years. Show how private businesses are valued. Show how double taxation is prevented. Show how payment works when wealth exists on paper but cash does not.

And most importantly, have the Joint Committee on Taxation, Congressional Budget Office and Treasury independently cost the package before attaching a permanent worker benefit to optimistic revenue estimates.

Do Not Sell Workers Another Promise Without the Receipt

This is where tax politics usually becomes useless.

One side says, "Tax the rich."

The other says, "That will destroy investment."

Neither sentence is enough.

If a proposal raises taxes on high investment income, show what income, above what threshold, and what happens to ordinary retirement accounts. If it changes inheritance rules, show how family businesses are treated. If it creates a worker credit, show who qualifies, what it costs and how it interacts with existing benefits. If lawmakers say a reform will cause capital flight, investment losses or administrative chaos, quantify the concern and show the mechanism.

And if Congress decides to preserve a tax preference, say who benefits from preserving it and why that benefit is worth the revenue the public gives up.

The public should not have to choose between slogans when the tax code is written in numbers.

What Regular Joe Should Ask

Joe does not need to become a tax attorney to ask intelligent questions.

When a politician proposes a tax cut, ask whether it changes taxes on wages, capital gains, corporate income, estates or all of them. Ask whether the benefit is refundable for people who owe little income tax. Ask whether a temporary credit disappears after one or two years while a capital preference remains permanent.

When somebody says "the rich already pay most of the taxes," ask what measure they are using: total dollars, income-tax share, effective tax rate, payroll taxes, consumption taxes, property taxes or some broader measure. Those are different questions and can produce different answers.

When somebody says "unrealized gains are not income," ask the next question instead of ending the conversation: if enormous economic gains can remain untaxed for decades and some appreciation can escape later capital-gains taxation through inheritance rules, what tax base should society use to prevent permanent deferral from becoming permanent exclusion?

When somebody proposes a wealth tax, ask how private companies, art, real estate and other hard-to-value assets will be valued. Ask how losses and debt are handled. Ask whether the government can administer it. Ask what happens if a court strikes one part down.

Those are not anti-rich questions. They are accounting questions.

Who Is Missing?

The missing seat belongs to the person whose economic life is almost entirely visible to the tax system because it arrives as wages.

Joe works. His employer reports the wages. Taxes are withheld. The money enters his bank account and immediately begins paying for life.

Alex may work too. Alex may have built a business from nothing. Alex may deserve every dollar legally earned. But if most of Alex's economic growth now comes from assets that can appreciate without an immediate tax event, the system is no longer treating the two paths to wealth in the same way.

The question is not whether Alex should be punished for succeeding.

The question is whether Joe should spend a lifetime paying as he earns while the largest fortunes can use rules Joe will probably never need because he may never own enough assets to use them.

We already have workable pieces. Refundable credits exist. Capital gains are already taxed when realized. Inheritance rules can be changed. Carried-interest reform has been drafted repeatedly. High-wealth minimum-tax proposals have been written. Other countries provide evidence about what works, what causes administrative problems and what should not be copied blindly.

So the next step is not another vague promise.

Ask Congress to assemble the pieces, independently cost them and vote.

Then ask every lawmaker who says no to identify the exact provision they oppose, the harm they predict, and the alternative they support.

Ask these four questions: Why should wages face taxation as they arrive while enormous asset gains can wait? When does legitimate tax deferral become a system that permanently favors existing wealth? Which reforms can help workers without crushing genuine small businesses? And which elected official is willing to put an independently costed bill behind the answer?

Verification and evidence notice

This editorial distinguishes current law from proposals. S. 2845 and S. 4246 were introduced and referred to the Senate Finance Committee in the official records checked for this publication; neither is described here as enacted law. Treasury's 25% high-wealth minimum-tax design is a prior administration proposal, not current law.

The article does not claim that every wealthy person pays a lower effective tax rate than every worker. Total tax burdens depend on wages, business income, dividends, realized gains, payroll taxes, deductions, credits, state and local taxes, estate rules and individual circumstances.

The international examples demonstrate available mechanisms and design tradeoffs, not proof that importing another country's tax system would produce the same U.S. results. OECD historical data on wealth taxes are presented as historical evidence, not as a 2026 country count.

The reform package is a TMS policy framework for debate. It is not current law, an official revenue estimate, or a claim that any single design is economically or constitutionally settled. Independent costing, legal review and distributional analysis would be required before enactment.
